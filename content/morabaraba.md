## Basic Information

Morabaraba (mlabalaba, mmela, muravava, umlabalaba) is the traditional mill game of Southern Africa — played across South Africa, Botswana, Lesotho, Eswatini, Zimbabwe and Mozambique — on a board of three concentric squares (24 points) with 12 stones per player, called “cows” because the game is popular among cattle herders. It belongs to the worldwide mill-game family (twelve men's morris, shax, achi, tsoro). Mind Sports South Africa (MSSA), recognized by the Department of Sports and Recreation and SASCOC, runs leagues and the Morabaraba World Championships since 1997 with senior, junior and women's divisions; men and women compete on equal terms. Within the mind games on this site, its closest relatives are [Dash Guti](dash-guti.html), [Seega](seega.html), [Latrones](latrones.html), [Oware](oware.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Three phases: (1) placing — players alternately place one cow on an empty point; (2) moving — after all 24 cows are placed, a cow moves to an adjacent empty point along a line; (3) flying — a player reduced to three cows may move one to any empty point. Forming a mill (three own cows in a row on a line) lets the player shoot one opponent cow — a cow in a mill may not be shot unless all opponent cows are in mills; only one cow per move even if two mills are formed. A player wins by reducing the opponent to two cows or by blocking all their moves; repeated-position draws are resolved by “borrowing” rules in tournament play.

## History

The earliest known morabaraba diagram is carved in the temple of Kurna, Egypt, c. 1440 BC, and rock-carved boards in Southern Africa are claimed to be at least 800 years old, refuting the old theory of a European origin; mill games reached the region via Indian Ocean trade (boards also found in Sri Lanka, c. AD 10). The Sotho name moraba-raba means “to mill” or “to go round in a circle.” Among Basotho migrant mineworkers the game appears in the difela song-poems. Mind Sports South Africa formalized the “generally accepted rules” and has organized world championships since 1997; South African federations have also campaigned for the game's inclusion in the Olympic programme.
