## Basic Information

Canicross is cross-country running in which a dog, harnessed in a pulling harness, is attached by an elastic bungee line (1.5–2.5 m) to a waist belt worn by the runner; the dog runs in front and pulls, while the runner steers with voice commands — no collar is ever used, and the waist belt must have a panic snap (International Federation of Sleddog Sports [IFSS], 2026; International Canicross Federation [ICF], 2025). The sport developed from summer training for sled-dog teams in Scandinavia in the 1990s and is governed internationally by the IFSS (dryland disciplines: canicross, bikejoring, scooter) and, for canicross specifically, by the International Canicross Federation (ICF), which runs its own world championship series (IFSS, 2026; ICF, 2025). The Canicross World Championship 2026 was held in Owińska, Poland, from November 5 to 8, under the ICF, following the 2025 edition in Pardubice, Czech Republic (ICF, 2025). Race distances are typically 3–10 km depending on class; classes are split by gender (DCM/DCW), age (junior, veteran) and dog size (open, restricted), with mass starts allowed (IFSS, 2026). Dogs must be at least 12–18 months old depending on class, and welfare rules prohibit competing a dog with reasonable potential for harm (IFSS, 2026). Women are strongly represented at the top of the sport — Belgian Racheal Bailey of Akna K9 Academy is pictured with her two running dogs at a CaniX event in 2008 — and the IFSS/ICF worlds regularly see women's podiums led by European and North American runners (ICF, 2025).

## Rules

Under the IFSS Race Rules 2026–2028 (valid from 15 July 2026), a canicross team is one dog and one runner connected by a bungee line of at most 2 m (IFSS measure) attached to a padded waist belt with a panic clip; the dog wears a proper X-back or H-style pulling harness that leaves the shoulders free, and neck attachment is forbidden. The line must have a shock absorber, and no metal hook, ring or snap is permitted at the runner's end. The striker rules of drafting apply: on narrow trails the overtaking team has right of way, and the overtaken team must move aside when safe. Classes: DCM/DCW (men/women), junior and veteran age groups, open and restricted dog size; kids' classes (DCK1 up to 7, DCK2 8–10, DCK3 11–13) follow the same rules with mass starts allowed. Mandatory equipment checks happen before the start; dogs showing signs of distress are withdrawn, and any assistance from third parties on the course is reported to the race marshal and may bring penalties (IFSS, 2026). The ICF's own 2025 regulations apply at ICF world championships, with the same basic setup and distances of 3–10 km by class (ICF, 2025). Fouls include using a collar, a walking-style harness, a line without shock absorber, or receiving outside help; penalties range from time addition to disqualification (IFSS, 2026).

## History

Canicross emerged in Scandinavia in the 1990s as dryland training for sled-dog (mushing) teams and became a standalone sport with its own race format; the IFSS, founded in 1973 (originally for sled-dog racing), added canicross, bikejoring and scooter to its dryland program, and the International Canicross Federation (ICF) was later created to run dedicated canicross world championships — the 2023 ICF World Championship in Leipa, Germany, drew athletes from more than 25 countries, the 2024 edition was in Bardonecchia, Italy, 2025 in Pardubice, Czech Republic, and 2026 in Owińska, Poland (ICF, 2025; Wikipedia, n.d.). The sport is now practiced across Europe (Belgian Canicross Federation, British CaniX, French FCB), North America and beyond, always with the same dog-first welfare rules. Belgian champion Philippe Wéry was among the early international stars of canicross and bikejoring (Wikipedia, n.d.).
