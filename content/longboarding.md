## Basic Information

Longboarding is skateboarding on a longboard — a longer, wider deck (typically 32-50 inches) with larger, softer wheels and reverse-kingpin trucks that are stable at high speed. Beyond cruising and commuting, longboarding has competitive disciplines: downhill racing, slalom, freeride (slides) and long-distance pushing. Downhill longboarding is sanctioned internationally by the International Downhill Federation (IDF) and the International Gravity Sports Association (IGSA), with riders exceeding 100 km/h on closed public roads.

## Rules

In downhill races, up to four riders start together on a closed road course and race to the finish as fast as possible, standing or in a low luge-style position; riders wear full-face helmets, leather suits and slide gloves, and may draft behind each other. Races are run in heats with timed runs, and the fastest time wins; world record attempts are individually timed with radar or timing traps and verified by an official. Slalom competitions run a course of cones (typically 30-50 cones spaced 5-10 feet apart) where each missed or knocked cone adds a penalty (usually 0.1-0.3 s), and the lowest time wins. Freeride events are judged on the quality, difficulty and execution of slides.

## History

Longboards originated in Hawaii and California in the 1940s-1950s as "sidewalk surfing" when the waves were flat — the first were planks with roller-skate wheels, and manufactured longboards appeared in 1959 from Makaha, Hobie and Jack's. The sport died out by the mid-1960s but returned in 1972 when Frank Nasworthy's Cadillac Wheel Company introduced urethane wheels, which allowed much higher downhill speeds; reverse-kingpin trucks followed in 1979 (Randal and Variflex), making high-speed riding stable and turning downhill longboarding into a legitimate sport under the IGSA. Today the IDF and IDSA run international races and records, and famous descents include events in Europe, South America and North America.
