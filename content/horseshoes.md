## Basic Information

Horseshoes (horseshoe pitching) is a precision throwing game in which players pitch U-shaped iron or steel shoes at a steel stake set in a clay or sand pit, trying to encircle the stake ("ringer") or land closest to it; in North America it is governed by the National Horseshoe Pitchers Association (NHPA) — a federation of 54 state charters with around 15,000 members that sanctions the annual NHPA World Tournament (the 2027 edition is in Kennewick, Washington) — while an estimated 15 million people pitch horseshoes recreationally in the United States and Canada (National Horseshoe Pitchers Association [NHPA], n.d.-a, n.d.-b). The NHPA crowns world champions in men's, women's, boys' and girls' divisions, and play spans ages from 5 to over 85; women pitch from a shorter foul line than men and have their own world championship division (NHPA, n.d.-a) (National Horseshoe Pitchers Association, n.d.-a) (National Horseshoe Pitchers Association, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Under NHPA rules, two stakes are set 40 ft (12.2 m) apart, measured from the front of each stake, in a pit of clay, sand or similar material roughly 31–36 in wide and 43–72 in long; the foul line is 37 ft from the stake for men and 27 ft for women, juniors and elders (20 ft for cadets) (NHPA, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The stake is a 1 in diameter steel pin standing 14–15 in above the pit surface and leaning about 3 in toward the pitcher; a legal shoe weighs at most 2 lb 10 oz, measures no more than 7⅝ in × 7¼ in with an opening of at most 3⅝ in (NHPA, n.d.-a). Each pitcher throws two shoes per inning within a 30-second time limit, alternating with the opponent; scoring is either cancellation or count-all: a ringer — a shoe encircling the stake, verified by the straightedge test — scores 3 points; the shoe closest to the stake scores 1 point if within 6 in, and a leaner (a shoe touching the stake) scores 1 point (NHPA, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Games are played to 40 points, or as shoe-limit games (40 or 50 shoes), with innings alternating; men and women compete in separate divisions with the adjusted foul-line distances described above (NHPA, n.d.-a).

## History

Horseshoe pitching descends from quoits — England set up the first written rules for quoits in 1869 — and from games played with discarded horseshoes by Roman soldiers, by soldiers on the Boston Common in the American Revolutionary War, and with mule shoes in Union Army camps during the Civil War; Dutch and German immigrants brought their kegeln-style pitching games to North America (NHPA, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first tournament open to the world was held in the summer of 1909 in Bronson, Kansas, on dirt courts with stakes only 2 in high and 38.5 ft apart — 34 men entered and W. F. Jackson and Lonnie Wilson of Blue Mound shared the prize (NHPA, n.d.-b). The stake height was raised to 12 in in the late 1930s or early 1940s and fixed at 14–15 in from 1950; two early bodies — including the National League of Horseshoe and Quoit Pitchers, formed in Florida in 1919 — merged, and at the National Convention in Lake Worth, Florida, on 16 February 1925 the organization took the name National Horseshoe Pitchers Association of America (NHPA, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
