## Basic Information

Broom hockey is the dry-land, school and gym-floor version of broomball: two teams of five or six players use brooms to hit, pass and shoot a ball toward the opponent's goal on a hard court or gym floor — without skates, ice or full protective gear. There is no single world federation for broom hockey; the closest international bodies are the International Federation of Broomball Associations (IFBA), which governs broomball, and national bodies such as the United States Broomball Association (USBA), which publish broom hockey rules for school and recreational play (International Federation of Broomball Associations [IFBA], n.d.) (United States Broomball Association [USBA], n.d.). Broom hockey is played in physical education classes and recreational leagues across North America, the United Kingdom and Northern Europe, and it serves as the entry-level version of broomball, whose 2026 world championship was held in Whitehorse, Yukon, Canada, in early November 2026 (IFBA, n.d.).

On the gender side, broom hockey is typically played mixed in schools and clubs; at organized broomball level, the women's division is non-contact (see broomball), and girls' and women's broom hockey teams are common in school leagues (IFBA, n.d.).

## Rules

Under the school and recreational rules published by the USBA, broom hockey is played 5 or 6 players per side, including a goaltender, on a court or gym floor roughly the size of a basketball court, with two goals at either end. Players use a broom (or a hockey-style stick with a small blade) to control the ball; the broom blade must never be raised above waist height, and there is no checking or body contact — deliberately or accidentally hitting an opponent with the broom is a foul (USBA, n.d.). Play starts with a face-off at centre court and after each goal; a goal counts when the ball fully crosses the goal line. Games are usually two halves of 10 to 20 minutes in school play. Fouls — high sticking (blade above the waist), tripping, slashing, pushing and holding — result in a free hit or a penalty shot, and repeated fouls lead to a player sitting out; in many school variants a goalkeeper may use hands within the crease (USBA, n.d.).

Equipment: a soft ball or puck (lighter and softer than an ice hockey puck), brooms or floor-hockey sticks, gym shoes with non-marking soles, and optional light gloves and shin guards; helmets and full pads are not required in the dry-land version, unlike in ice broomball. Men's, women's and mixed teams use identical rules; youth and school divisions shorten games and may use a larger, softer ball (IFBA, n.d.) (USBA, n.d.). Formats range from informal pickup games to school leagues, university intramurals and adult recreational tournaments, often organized alongside broomball clubs.


## History

Broom hockey developed in Canada in the early 20th century as a dry-land offshoot of broomball — the first documented broomball game was played in Perdue, Saskatchewan, on March 5, 1909 — so that the game could be played in gymnasiums and on cleared snow or dirt in schools and community centres without ice skates (IFBA, n.d.). Girls' broom hockey teams were already being photographed in Ontario in the early 1900s, and the game spread through Canadian and American physical education programmes during the 20th century, later reaching British and European schools as "gym broomball" or floor hockey. Today it remains the most accessible entry point into broom sports, with millions of schoolchildren playing it worldwide each year, while organized play continues under broomball bodies such as the IFBA, which staged its 2026 world championship in Whitehorse with 24 or more teams (IFBA, n.d.) (USBA, n.d.).
