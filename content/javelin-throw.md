## Basic Information

The javelin throw is the spear event of athletics: from a 30–36.5 m runway ending in a throwing arc, the athlete sprints and hurls an 800 g spear for men (600 g for women) into a sector of about 29 degrees; the javelin must land tip-first to count. It is governed worldwide by World Athletics and has been on the Olympic programme since 1906 (Intercalated Games) and after the 1906 Intercalated Games, on the Olympic programme since 1908 for men and since 1932 for women (Wikipedia, n.d.). The world records are 98.48 m for men, set by Jan Železný of Czechoslovakia in Jena on 25 May 1996, and 72.28 m for women, set by Barbora Špotáková of the Czech Republic in Stuttgart on 8 September 2008 — both standing in 2026 after the javelin redesigns of 1986 (men) and 1999 (women) reset the records (World Athletics, n.d.-e).

At the end of the 2026 season the women's event was led by Olympic champion Haruka Kitaguchi of Japan (pictured), who won the 2023 world title, the 2024 Olympic gold in Paris (65.80 m) and the inaugural 2026 World Athletics Ultimate Championship in Budapest with a national record 68.92 m, ahead of Yan Ziyi of China, the dominant 2026 Diamond League performer (68.42 m in Brussels), and Flor Ruiz of Colombia; the men's event featured 2025 world champion Keshorn Walcott of Trinidad and Tobago, Olympic champion Arshad Nadeem of Pakistan (Olympic record 92.97 m, Paris 2024; podium pictured), 2023 world champion Neeraj Chopra of India (personal best 90.23 m, 2025) and Rumesh Pathirage of Sri Lanka, who threw 92.62 m in Rome on 4 June 2026 — the second-best mark ever — and won the 2026 Ultimate Championship with 91.09 m (World Athletics, n.d.-d).

## Rules

The javelin is 2.60–2.70 m long and weighs 800 g for men, 2.20–2.30 m and 600 g for women, with the grip at the centre of gravity; it is thrown from a runway at least 30 m and at most 36.5 m long and 4 m wide, behind a 4 m throwing arc, into a sector of about 29 degrees, and must land tip-first (World Athletics, n.d.-a). Throws at major championships begin with a qualification round; in the final each athlete has three attempts, and the best eight receive three further attempts in reverse order of placings, with a one-minute time limit per attempt (World Athletics, n.d.-a). The implement must land completely inside the marked sector, the athlete must not touch the ground or the stop board before the implement lands, and must leave the circle from its rear half after the throw (World Athletics, n.d.-a). The distance is measured to the nearest centimetre from the mark made by the implement to the inside edge of the throwing circle's line (World Athletics, n.d.-a). The run may be abandoned if the athlete crosses the runway's side lines (World Athletics, n.d.-a).

Men and women use different javelin weights; U18/U20 athletes use lighter javelins (700 g / 500 g), and para athletics contests the event in classification classes (World Athletics, n.d.-a). Wind readings do not invalidate javelin records (World Athletics, n.d.-a).

## History

Javelin throwing is one of the oldest athletic contests, part of the ancient Greek pentathlon, and the modern event was codified in Scandinavia in the 19th century; Eric Lemming of Sweden won four Olympic golds between 1908 and 1920 (Wikipedia, n.d.). The men's javelin was redesigned in 1986 to make it land tip-first and shorten throws for safety, resetting the records — Uwe Hohn's 104.80 m of 1984 remains the farthest throw ever with the old model (Wikipedia, n.d.). Jan Železný, pictured, set the world record of 98.48 m in Jena 1996 and won Olympic gold in 1992, 1996 and 2000 plus three world titles (Olympics, n.d.). Barbora Špotáková, pictured, set the women's world record of 72.28 m in Stuttgart 2008 after the 1999 redesign and won three world titles (2007, 2011, 2015); East Germany's Petra Felke holds the old-model record of 80.00 m from 1988 (World Athletics, n.d.-e).
