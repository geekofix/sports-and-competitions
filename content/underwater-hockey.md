## Basic Information

Underwater hockey — nicknamed octopush — is the breath-hold team sport played at the bottom of a swimming pool: two teams of six players (with substitutes) wear mask, snorkel, fins and a colored water-polo cap and push a lead-coated puck along the pool floor with a short curved stick into the opponents' 3 m goal trough (CMAS, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). It was invented in 1954 by Alan Blake, founder of the Southsea Sub-Aqua Club (BSAC No. 9) in Portsmouth, England, together with John Ventham, Jack Willis and Frank Lilleker, and first played in the club's pool at the Guildhall Baths/Eastney Pool; it was announced in the diving magazine *Neptune* in November 1954, and the first competition followed in early 1955 between Southsea, Bournemouth and Brighton (Wikipedia, n.d.). The game spread through British diving clubs, reached South Africa in the mid-1950s (Durban Undersea Club) and Canada in 1962 (Norm Leibeck, Vancouver), and today the world governing body is CMAS, the Confédération Mondiale des Activités Subaquatiques (CMAS, n.d.). The first underwater hockey world championship was held in Canada in 1980 — a 1979 attempt had been abandoned over apartheid politics — and Australia staged its first championships in Margaret River in 1975, adding a women's division in 1981 and a junior division in 1990 (Wikipedia, n.d.). The British Octopush Association was founded on 10 April 1976 (Wikipedia, n.d.). At the inaugural CMAS Intercontinental Championships in 2025, Great Britain's elite women and masters women teams won gold — the leading women first among the champions (CMAS, n.d.).

## Rules

A game is played in a pool 2–4 m deep, in two halves of 10–15 minutes, by two teams of six players with up to four substitutes; players wear mask, snorkel, fins, a water-polo cap, a glove and a mouthguard, and carry a roughly 30 cm one-handed stick (the "pusher") to pass, flick and shoot a lead-coated puck of about 1.3–1.5 kg (CMAS, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Goals are 3 m wide troughs at each end of the pool, and a point is scored when the puck enters the trough; players surface and dive continuously in the sport's characteristic rhythm, and surfacing is never a foul (CMAS, n.d.). Contact rules prohibit body contact and blocking except stick-on-stick and stick-on-puck contact: obstruction, playing the puck with the body or hand, and interfering with an opponent's equipment or mask are fouls punished with free pucks, penalty shots and exclusions (CMAS, n.d.). Men and women play under identical technical rules in separate men's and women's categories, with mixed competition also offered, across junior, senior and masters age groups; CMAS runs the biennial world championships and publishes the official rules (CMAS, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

Underwater hockey was invented in 1954 by Alan Blake of the Southsea Sub-Aqua Club (BSAC No. 9) in Portsmouth, with John Ventham, Jack Willis and Frank Lilleker, to keep divers fit and entertained through the British winter; the first games were played at the Guildhall Baths and Eastney Pool in Portsmouth and the invention was announced in *Neptune* magazine in November 1954 (Wikipedia, n.d.). The first competition was held in early 1955 between Southsea, Bournemouth and Brighton, and the game spread through Britain's diving clubs before reaching South Africa in the mid-1950s via the Durban Undersea Club and Canada in 1962 through Norm Leibeck in Vancouver (Wikipedia, n.d.). The British Octopush Association was founded on 10 April 1976, Australia held its first championships at Margaret River in 1975 with women's and junior divisions added in 1981 and 1990, and CMAS — founded in 1959 with Jacques Cousteau as its inaugural president — took the sport under its wing, staging the first world championship in Canada in 1980 after a 1979 edition was abandoned over apartheid politics (Wikipedia, n.d.; CMAS, n.d.). Today several hundred clubs play worldwide, with heartlands in Britain, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the United States and continental Europe, and the 2025 inaugural Intercontinental Championships crowned Great Britain's elite and masters women among the first champions (CMAS, n.d.).
