## Basic Information

Underwater hockey (octopush) is the breath-hold team sport played at the bottom of a swimming pool: two teams of six (with four substitutes) wear mask, snorkel, fins and water polo caps, diving to push a lead puck along the pool floor with short curved sticks into the opponents' goal trough. Fast, three-dimensional and famously exhausting, it was born in England and is contested at world championships with a strong international federation following.

## Rules

Games are two halves of 10 to 15 minutes in a pool 2 to 4 metres deep: six players per side are in the water at any time, wearing mask, snorkel, fins, a glove and a colored water-polo cap, and play with a roughly 30-centimetre stick held in one hand to push, pass and flick a 1.3-kilogram lead-coated puck along the bottom. Goals are 3-metre troughs at each end, the puck entering the trough scores; players surface and dive continuously (the sport's distinctive cycling rhythm), contact rules prohibit body contact and blocking with anything other than the playing stick, and fouls (obstruction, playing the puck with the body, interfering with an opponent's equipment) bring free pucks, penalties and exclusion. Competitive levels range from club nights to world championships in age and gender categories.

## History

Underwater hockey was invented in 1954 by the British diver Alan Blake of Southsea Sub-Aqua Club, devised to keep divers fit and entertain members through the British winter in the club's pool; the game (first called octopush) spread through Britain's diving clubs and abroad, with the first national championships and world championships staged by the 1980s and the CMAS running the modern world championship every two years. The sport's heartlands include Britain, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the United States and continental Europe, and it has grown into a genuine international competition community with hundreds of clubs worldwide, celebrated for its universality (players compete across age and gender in mixed play) and for the peculiar, chesslike beauty of a game played on a windowpane.
