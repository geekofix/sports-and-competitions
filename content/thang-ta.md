## Basic Information

Thang-ta is the classical Meitei martial art of Manipur in northeastern India — "the art of the sword (thang) and the spear (ta)" — originating in the ancient kingdom of Kangleipak; it is the armed branch of Huiyen Lallong, whose unarmed form is called Sarit Sarak (Wikipedia, n.d.). The art combines the sword, spear and dagger with movement focused on control and breathing, and comprises four parts: Ta-khousarol (the spear dance), Thanghairol (sword fighting), Sarit-Sarak and Thengkourol (touch-and-call) (Wikipedia, n.d.). In India it is governed by the Thang-Ta Federation of India, a government-registered national governing body (Regd. No. S. 38532 of 2001) headquartered in New Delhi and a member of the Asian Thang-Ta Federation and the World Thang-Ta Federation (Thang-Ta Federation of India, n.d.). Men and women both practice and compete in thang-ta, in the same weapon disciplines and categories (Thang-Ta Federation of India, n.d.).

 Within the combat sports and martial arts family on this site, its closest relatives are [Kalaripayattu](kalaripayattu.html), [Silambam](silambam.html), [Karate](karate.html), [Kendo](kendo.html), [Kyudo](kyudo.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Thang-ta competition follows the Thang-Ta Federation of India's rules and covers the four disciplines — Ta-khousarol (spear dance, often performed solo and used in theater), Thanghairol (sword fighting), Sarit-Sarak (unarmed) and Thengkourol (touch-and-call) — using the traditional weapons: the sword (thang), the spear (ta), the dagger and the painted shield, about 90 cm long and 45 cm wide (Thang-Ta Federation of India, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Competitors are grouped into men's and women's divisions across the weapon disciplines, with junior and senior age classes at national championships (Thang-Ta Federation of India, n.d.). The official rule source is the Thang-Ta Federation of India (Thang-Ta Federation of India, n.d.).

## History

Thang-ta originated in ancient Kangleipak (present-day Manipur), where legend says the weapons were fashioned from the body of the creator deity Pakhangba (Tin Sidaba); the art flourished under King Khagemba (r. 1597–1652) (Wikipedia, n.d.). After the Anglo-Manipuri War of 1891 the British banned weapon ownership and with it the martial arts of Manipur, including thang-ta; the art was revived in the 20th century, notably by Maharaja Churachand Singh from 1934, and Gurumayum Gourakishor Sharma, awarded the Padma Shri, carried the revival internationally, while Khilton Nongmaithem was among the first to teach thang-ta outside Manipur (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Thang-Ta Federation of India, registered in 2001 and headquartered in Delhi, now runs national championships and represents the art in the Asian and World Thang-Ta federations (Thang-Ta Federation of India, n.d.).
