## Basic Information

Rowing — called crew in the United States — is the sport of racing boats propelled by oars fixed in rowlocks, on rivers, lakes or the sea; unlike paddling sports, the oars are attached to the boat (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It is governed by World Rowing (FISA), the Fédération Internationale des Sociétés d'Aviron founded in 1892 in Turin — the oldest international sports federation in the Olympic movement — which organized the first European Rowing Championships in 1893 and set the standard 2,000 m race distance at the 1894 Europeans in Mâcon (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Rowing has been on the Olympic programme since 1900 (men; the 1896 race was cancelled for bad weather) and women have competed since Montreal 1976 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The 54th World Rowing Championships were held 24–30 August 2026 on the Bosbaan in Amsterdam, where the host Netherlands topped the medal table with four golds — including the men's eight (Rienks, Florijn, Rustenburg, Salverda, De Graaf, Van Veen, Van Der Bij, Makker, cox De Vries) and the women's quadruple sculls (Keijser, Bruijnincx, Leeuwenburgh, Dullemans) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Among today's leading athletes, the women come first: the Dutch women's quadruple sculls world champions of 2026, Olympic champion Emma Twigg of Great Britain and the Australian single-sculls star Kimberley "Kim" Crow, alongside the men's stars of the Amsterdam eight — while lightweight rowing (crew average 70 kg for men, 57 kg for women) remains a World Championship category but was dropped from the Olympic programme after Paris 2024 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Competitive rowing races are staged over the standard 2,000 m course in straight lanes (typically six), starting from stake boats or starting lights, with umpires and a chief referee; a crew that interferes with another can be warned twice and then disqualified, and false starts are recalled (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Boat classes at the World Championships span 22 events: single sculls (1x), pair (2-), double sculls (2x), coxless four (4-), quadruple sculls (4x), coxed four (4+), eight (8+) and lightweight variants, plus para-rowing classes; Olympic programme events are a subset (World Rowing, n.d.). Each rower uses one oar — sweep rowing (one oar per rower) in pairs, fours and eights, with a coxswain steering in 2+, 4+ and 8+; sculling (two oars per rower) in singles and doubles (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Boats have minimum weights, hulls are checked at boat weigh-ins, and crews must be weighed for lightweight classes (crew average 70 kg men / 57 kg women, no single rower over 72.5 kg / 59 kg) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women and men compete in all boat classes from junior through elite, with equal Olympic programme slots since Tokyo 2020 (World Rowing, n.d.). Head races on rivers — such as the Head of the River Race and Head of the Charles — are time-trial races against the clock over 3–5 miles with staggered starts (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

Organized rowing began in 18th-century England with the Doggett's Coat and Badge race (from 1715), and the first university boat race, Oxford–Cambridge, was held in 1829 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). FISA was founded in 1892 to standardize rules and distances, the European Championships began in 1893, and the World Rowing Championships have been held annually since 1962 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Icons of the sport include the USA's John B. Kelly Sr. (1920 Olympic single-sculls champion and father of Grace Kelly), Britain's Sir Steve Redgrave, the only rower to win five Olympic gold medals (1984–2000), the Czech Ondřej Synek and New Zealand's Māhe Drysdale, and on the women's side Romania's Elisabeta Lipă, who won five Olympic golds between 1984 and 2004, Germany's Kathrin Boron and Australia's Kim Crow (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
