## Basic Information

Kite buggying is the sport of driving a single-seat, three-wheeled buggy — one steerable front wheel and two fixed rear wheels — propelled by a large traction kite on beaches, dry lake beds or grass fields (FISLY, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The idea of kite-drawn carts dates to George Pocock in the United Kingdom in 1827, but modern kite buggying was spawned by the New Zealander Peter Lynn (1946–2026), whose 1990 conversion of a kitesailing craft onto wheels created the sport as it is raced today (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Kite buggies are classified as Class 8 land yachts by FISLY, the International Land and Sandyachting Federation, and reach speeds up to about 110 km/h — the world record stands at about 134 km/h (84.10 mph), set by Brian Holgate with a 2.7 m Peter Lynn Vapor kite (FISLY, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Riders wear helmets and protective clothing and steer by foot pedals while controlling the kite with a control bar; buggies have no brakes — the kite itself is used to slow down and stop (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men and women compete together in the same classes and races (FISLY, n.d.-a).

## Rules

Kite buggying is governed by the FISLY International Rules for Land Yachting, with Class 8 specifications in Appendix 2 (FISLY, n.d.-a). A Class 8 buggy has three wheels — a steerable front wheel controlled by foot pedals and two fixed rear wheels — a rigid chassis with a seat, and is sailed with a traction kite of registered size; wheel types range from thin racing wheels to standard and "bigfoot" sand wheels (FISLY, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Safety rules require a helmet, and riders must be able to release the kite instantly; because buggies have no brakes, speed and direction are controlled entirely through the kite (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Competition follows land-yachting racing guidelines on closed circuits or straight speed courses: races are started by flag, right-of-way rules apply between buggies, and the first across the finish line wins; slalom, long-distance and speed-record events are also held (FISLY, n.d.-a). Classes are defined by kite area, and men's and women's competitors race in the same events under identical rules (FISLY, n.d.-a). Rule source: FISLY, https://www.fisly.org/ (FISLY, n.d.-b).

## History

The principle of steering kites to pull carts was promulgated by George Pocock in the United Kingdom in 1827, and kite-drawn vehicles appeared sporadically in the 20th century (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The modern sport began when Peter Lynn of New Zealand converted a kitesailing catamaran onto wheels in 1990, creating the first true kite buggy; through the 1990s buggying spread across Europe, Australia and the Americas (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). FISLY formalized the buggy as Class 8 land yacht within its international rules, and national associations — such as the Class 8 Kite Buggy Association — organize racing and record attempts (FISLY, n.d.-a). Peter Lynn, who died on 11 April 2026, remained the sport's most influential designer, and the outright speed record of 84.10 mph (about 135 km/h) was set by Brian Holgate on a 2.7 m Peter Lynn Vapor kite (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Icons of the sport are George Pocock, Peter Lynn and Brian Holgate (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
