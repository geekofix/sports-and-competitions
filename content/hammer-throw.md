## Basic Information

The hammer throw is the rotating throw of athletics: from a 2.135 m circle the athlete swings a steel ball on a wire handle — 7.26 kg for men, 4 kg for women, with a maximum overall length of 1.22 m — in three or four turns before releasing it into a 34.92-degree sector protected by a cage. It is governed worldwide by World Athletics and has been on the Olympic programme for men since 1900 and for women since 2000 (Wikipedia, n.d.). The world records are 86.74 m for men, set by Yuriy Sedykh of the Soviet Union at the 1986 European Championships in Stuttgart on 30 August 1986 — unbroken for almost 40 years — and 82.98 m for women, set by Anita Włodarczyk of Poland in Warsaw on 28 August 2016 (World Athletics, n.d.-e).

At the end of the 2026 season the women's event was led by 2025 world champion Camryn Rogers of Canada (pictured), who threw 81.13 m in 2026 — second-farthest ever — with Zhang Jiale of China (79.62 m, 2026) and Anthonett Nabwe of Liberia; the men's event was led by 2024 Olympic and 2025 world champion Ethan Katzberg of Canada (pictured at Paris 2024), who set a championship record 84.70 m in Tokyo 2025 and a Games record 80.97 m at the 2026 Commonwealth Games in Glasgow, with Bence Halász of Hungary (84.25 m in Birmingham, 11 August 2026, second-best ever) and Merlin Hummel of Germany, winner of the 2026 Ultimate Championship (81.46 m) (World Athletics, n.d.-d). Włodarczyk (pictured), three-time Olympic champion (2012, 2016, 2020) and the first woman over 80 m, remains the women's standard (World Athletics, n.d.-e).

## Rules

The hammer consists of a metal ball, a steel wire and a handle, weighing 7.26 kg for men and 4 kg for women with a maximum overall length of 1.195–1.22 m; it is thrown from a 2.135 m circle with a cage into a 34.92-degree sector (World Athletics, n.d.-a). Throws at major championships begin with a qualification round; in the final each athlete has three attempts, and the best eight receive three further attempts in reverse order of placings, with a one-minute time limit per attempt (World Athletics, n.d.-a). The implement must land completely inside the marked sector, the athlete must not touch the ground or the stop board before the implement lands, and must leave the circle from its rear half after the throw (World Athletics, n.d.-a). The distance is measured to the nearest centimetre from the mark made by the implement to the inside edge of the throwing circle's line (World Athletics, n.d.-a). The athlete may wear gloves and must release the hammer from within the circle (World Athletics, n.d.-a).

Men and women use different hammer weights; U18/U20 athletes use lighter hammers (5–6 kg men, 3 kg women), and para athletics contests the event in classification classes (World Athletics, n.d.-a).

## History

The hammer throw descends from the ancient Irish and Scottish hammer games and entered the Olympic programme in 1900, where John Flanagan of the USA won three consecutive titles (1900, 1904, 1908); Karl Hein of Germany set a world record in 1936 that stood for decades (Wikipedia, n.d.). Yuriy Sedykh, pictured, threw 86.74 m at the 1986 European Championships in Stuttgart — still the world record in 2026 — and was married to shot put record holder Natalya Lisovskaya (World Athletics, n.d.-e). Wojciech Nowicki of Poland won the Tokyo 2020 Olympic title. On the women's side, Anita Włodarczyk, pictured, became the first woman over 80 m (81.08 m, 2015), set the world record of 82.98 m in Warsaw 2016 and won Olympic gold in 2012, 2016 and 2020 with four world titles (Olympics, n.d.; World Athletics, n.d.-e).
