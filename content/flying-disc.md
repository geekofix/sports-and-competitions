## Basic Information

Flying disc sports is the family of sports played with a plastic flying disc (Frisbee), including Ultimate, disc golf, freestyle, guts and double disc court. They are governed by the World Flying Disc Federation (WFDF), founded in 1985 and recognized by the IOC, which runs the World Championships and the World Games disc events.

## Rules

Rules are discipline-specific. In Ultimate (the best-known team disc sport, see [Ultimate](ultimate.html)), seven players per side pass the disc to score in the end zone, with no running with the disc and turnovers on incomplete passes; games are to 15 points or timed. In disc golf (see [Disc Golf](disc-golf.html)), players throw from tee to basket target. Freestyle is judged acrobatic routines with the disc (catches, rolls, delays) performed to music, scored on difficulty, execution and artistry. Guts is a fast throwing game where the receiver tries to catch a disc thrown at up to 70+ mph. Double disc court (DDC) uses two courts with two players each, aiming to land the disc in the opponent's court or hit their disc. At WFDF World Championships, each discipline has its own rules of play published by the federation.

## History

The modern flying disc began in 1948 when Walter Frederick Morrison designed the first plastic "Flying Saucer" (later the Wham-O Frisbee, named after the Frisbie Pie Company whose tins Yale students threw). Ultimate was invented in 1968 by Joel Silver and fellow students at Columbia High School in Maplewood, New Jersey, codifying the "Spirit of the Game" self-officiating principle. The WFDF was founded in 1985 and gained IOC recognition in 2013; disc sports were part of the World Games from 2001 (Akita). Disc golf (see separate entry) grew into its own major sport. Freestyle and guts have been played competitively since the 1960s-70s, with world championships run by the WFDF and the Freestyle Players Association.
