## Basic Information

Speed riding combines skiing with a small paraglider wing: the rider is pulled along the snow by the wing and can leave the ground for jumps and fast glides, at speeds up to about 100 km/h downhill (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is an FAI air sport under CIVL, the closest relative of speed flying, and belongs to the winter air-sports family with snowkiting.

![Speed rider with paraglider wing on a snowy slope](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Francois_Bon.jpg?width=640)

*Figure: Tomroo. (2008-01-21). Speed rider with paraglider wing on a snowy slope [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. CC BY-SA 3.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Francois_Bon.jpg*

The wing is a small paraglider (roughly 10–18 m²) attached to the skier via a harness; the rider steers with the skis and the wing risers, using the slope and wind to stay aloft (Wikipedia, n.d.). FAI ratifies speed-riding records and the discipline has world-championship status under CIVL (FAI, n.d.).

## Rules

Rules: participants must hold a ski level and paragliding qualification, wear a helmet, and fly only in designated areas away from ski pistes and lift cables; competitions are judged on timed downhill runs and acro/slalom tasks (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men and women compete together with separate record categories.

## History

Speed riding was invented in the early 2000s in the French Alps — the first dedicated wing for skiers was developed around 2003–2005 at resorts such as Valfréjus and Les Arcs (Wikipedia, n.d.). The sport grew quickly among freeride skiers and paragliders, and the FAI CIVL formalized competition formats in the 2010s (FAI, n.d.). Today speed riding is practiced across the Alps, Scandinavia, North America and New Zealand.
