## Basic Information

Marathon swimming is the Olympic 10 km open water race: swum in a lake, river, bay or the sea on a buoy-marked course with a mass start from a pontoon or beach, it has been on the Olympic programme since Beijing 2008 and is governed worldwide by World Aquatics together with the other open water distances (5 km, 25 km, 3 km knockout sprint and the mixed 4×1.25 km relay) (Olympics, n.d.; World Aquatics, n.d.-c; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The race at the Paris 2024 Olympics was won by Hungary's Kristóf Rasovszky in 1:50:52.8 among the men and by Sharon van Rouwendaal of the Netherlands among the women — her second Olympic 10 km title after Rio 2016 (World Aquatics, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). At the 2025 World Aquatics Championships in Singapore the 10 km titles went to Florian Wellbrock of Germany — his third 10 km world title after 2019 and 2023 — and to Moesha Johnson of Australia, the first Australian woman to win the 10 km world title, who also took the 5 km and was named World Aquatics Open Water Female Athlete of the Year 2025 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; World Aquatics, n.d.-a).

Among the athletes defining the 2026 season, the women lead: Moesha Johnson of Australia (pictured), 2025 world champion over 5 km and 10 km, Olympic silver medallist at Paris 2024 behind van Rouwendaal, and 2026 Pan Pacific 10 km champion, Sharon van Rouwendaal of the Netherlands, double Olympic 10 km champion (Rio 2016, Paris 2024); and Ana Marcela Cunha of Brazil, Olympic champion at Tokyo 2020 (World Aquatics, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). On the men's side Kristóf Rasovszky, the Paris 2024 Olympic champion, and Florian Wellbrock, three-time world champion, head the lists, with Germany's Oliver Klemet among the 2026 challengers (World Aquatics, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

The Olympic marathon swim is 10 km on a course marked by buoys and checked by GPS; all swimmers start together in a mass start and are timed electronically with chips at start and finish (World Aquatics, n.d.-c; Olympics, n.d.). Feeding stations are placed along the course in races of 5 km and longer, where support crews in escort boats hand food and drinks to the swimmers; touching another swimmer outside the course rules, a boat or a buoy, or receiving any assistance, leads to disqualification (World Aquatics, n.d.-c).

The water temperature must be between 16 °C and 31 °C; between 16 °C and 18 °C a full foam wetsuit is compulsory and below 16 °C the race is cancelled for safety; above 31 °C the race is also cancelled (World Aquatics, n.d.-c). Men and women race the same 10 km distance under identical rules, in separate events with separate start orders (Olympics, n.d.). At World Championships the programme additionally includes 5 km, 25 km, a 3 km knockout sprint and a mixed team relay of four swimmers — two men and two women — each covering 1.25 km (World Aquatics, n.d.-c; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## History

Long-distance swimming in open water has been practiced for centuries: the first recorded crossing of the English Channel was made by Captain Matthew Webb in August 1875 in 21 h 45 min, and the Channel remains the classic marathon-swim test, with Britain's Alison Streeter — the “Queen of the Channel” — holding the record for the most crossings (World Aquatics, n.d.-c; Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Marathon swimming became an Olympic discipline at Beijing 2008 with the 10 km race, won by Maarten van der Weijden of the Netherlands — pictured — a former cancer patient whose victory in 1:51:51.6 made him a national hero, and by Britta Steffen of Germany in the women's race (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Since then the Olympic 10 km has been won by Thomas Lurz of Germany (London 2012), Ferry Weertman of the Netherlands (Rio 2016), van der Weijden's successors van Rouwendaal (Rio 2016, Paris 2024) and Rasovszky (Paris 2024) on the women's and men's side respectively (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
