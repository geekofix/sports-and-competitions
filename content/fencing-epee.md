## Basic Information

Épée is the heaviest of the three fencing weapons and the closest to a real duel: the entire body is the valid target, and a touch counts for whoever hits first — there is no right of way, and simultaneous touches (within 1/40 of a second) score for both fencers. See [Fencing](fencing.html) for the shared rules; this entry covers épée-specific rules at the FIE World Championships and the Olympics.

## Rules

In épée, any touch on any part of the opponent's body scores, including the hands, feet and head; double touches (both fencers hit within 40 ms) count for both. There is no right of way, so the fencer who extends the arm and hits first scores. Bouts are to 15 touches in three 3-minute periods, with a one-minute priority period if tied. Because the whole body is valid, épée bouts are slower and more tactical, with frequent attacks on the hand and arm ("arm touches"). Corps-à-corps is penalized only if it prevents a touch; covering a target area is a foul. The épée guard is larger (bell guard) and the blade heavier (max 770 g) and stiffer (V-shaped) than foil or sabre. Team events run relay-style to 45 touches.

## History

The épée developed from the 19th-century dueling sword and was added to the Olympic program for men in 1900 (Paris) and women in 1996 (Atlanta). The FIE rules for épée were codified in the early 20th century; electronic scoring for épée was introduced in the 1930s. Italy and France dominate the men's épée historically (Edoardo Mangiarotti, Lucien Gaudin), while Hungary, China, Italy and France lead the women's event; modern stars include Romain Cannone (Tokyo 2020 men's gold), the Italian Garozzo family and Sun Yiwen (China, women's team gold 2020).
