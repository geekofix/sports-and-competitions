## Basic Information

Tricking (martial arts tricking) is the acrobatic movement sport in which athletes — "trickers" — chain martial arts kicks (from taekwondo, wushu and capoeira) together with gymnastics flips, twists and breakdance moves into flowing combos of kicks, flips and transitions (Wikipedia, n.d.). Signature tricks with their own names include the 540 kick, the corkscrew (cork), the flashkick, the butterfly twist, the double leg and the gainer (Wikipedia, n.d.). Tricking has no single world federation or formal rulebook; it is organized through online communities, teams and crews (such as Loopkicks of San Jose) and through battles and gatherings at events around the world, where women and men compete side by side in the same battles and open jams (Loopkicks, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The world's best trickers are showcased in battle events such as the annual Loopkicks Battle and in video parts, with elite women and men judged on the same criteria (Loopkicks, n.d.).

## Rules

Because tricking has no governing federation, there is no single standardized rulebook; competition conventions have emerged organically (Wikipedia, n.d.). In battle events, two trickers face each other in head-to-head brackets and take turns throwing short combo sequences — typically 30 to 60 seconds — while judges score difficulty, execution (clean rotation, height, controlled landing), variety and style, and the bracket winner advances (Wikipedia, n.d.; Loopkicks, n.d.). In choreographed-run formats, athletes perform a set routine to music judged on the same criteria (Wikipedia, n.d.). A combo is a chain of tricks linked without stopping, and landing each trick cleanly and in control is the mark of a good tricker; falls or uncontrolled landings cost points (Wikipedia, n.d.). Outside competition, gatherings ("sessions" or "jams") run on jam culture, rewarding creativity over medals, and informal trick lists order recognized moves from the easier 540 kick and aerial to the double backflip families (Wikipedia, n.d.). Women and men use identical trick vocabulary, judging criteria and battle formats (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

The taste for flashy, spectacular kicks in martial arts dates to the mid-1960s, when international taekwondo competition encouraged spinning and flying kicks (Wikipedia, n.d.). The direct precursor was Xtreme Martial Arts (XMA), created by the Chinese-American stuntman Michael Chaturantabut in the late 1990s and shown at martial arts tournaments in the 1990s and early 2000s (Wikipedia, n.d.). The sport of tricking itself is an internet phenomenon that emerged in the early 2000s: online hubs such as bilang.com, Trickbusters and Yellwboy.com connected martial artists worldwide — the term "tricking" derives from "martial arts tricks" — and by late 2003 a global online community was well established, with YouTube then driving a massive rise in popularity (Wikipedia, n.d.). Teams and crews formed around the West Coast, with San Jose as the epicenter: Loopkicks was founded there by Chris DeVera in 2003 and Zero Gravity by Tony Chu, alongside crews such as Team FS and the Hurricane Tricksterz (Wikipedia, n.d.). American martial artist and later Quest Crew founding member Steve Terada was among the early figures who helped popularize tricking from the late 1990s onward (Wikipedia, n.d.).
