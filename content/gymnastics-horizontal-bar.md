## Basic Information

The horizontal bar (high bar) is the men's artistic gymnastics apparatus of giant swings: a steel or fibreglass rail 2.40 m long, set about 2.8 m above the floor, on which the gymnast swings in giants, performs release and re-grasp moves (Tkachev, Kovacs, Gienger) and finishes with a dismount (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-c; Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). It is the sixth and final men's apparatus; at Paris 2024 the all-around and high bar titles went to Shinnosuke Oka of Japan (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Olympics, n.d.).

Among the leading high bar workers of the 2026 season: Shane Wiskus of the United States (pictured) competing at the 2026 U.S. Championships, alongside Shinnosuke Oka, Daiki Hashimoto and the Japanese 2026 world champion team. This is a men's apparatus — the women's counterpart is the uneven bars (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-e).

## Rules

The horizontal bar rail is 2.40 m long and set about 2.8 m high; the clear ceiling height above the bar must be at least 8 m, and landing mats with an additional 10 cm soft mat are mandatory (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-c; Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-e). The routine has no time limit and must include giant swings, at least one release move and a dismount; eight elements count toward the D-score with connection bonuses, and the E-panel starts at 10.0 with deductions for amplitude, re-catches and landing (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-a).

A fall costs 0.5 plus deductions and the gymnast may continue within 30 s; the top eight from qualification advance to the final, where the highest D + E wins (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-a; Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-e). Men compete on the horizontal bar; the women's counterpart is the uneven bars; junior categories use a lower bar (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-e).

## History

The horizontal bar descends from the 19th-century *Reck* and has been an Olympic apparatus since Athens 1896, when Germany's Herman Weingaertner, pictured, won the first Olympic title (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Japan's Mitsuo Tsukahara won high bar gold at Munich 1972, West Germany's Eberhard Gienger invented the eponymous release move and won the 1975 world title, and Germany's Fabian Hambüchen took Olympic gold at Rio 2016 with his own signature releases (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
