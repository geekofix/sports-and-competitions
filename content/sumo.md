## Basic Information

Sumo is Japan's national sport: two rikishi (wrestlers) clash in a raised clay ring, the dohyō, trying to force the opponent out of the ring or make any body part other than the soles of his feet touch the ground. Professional sumo is governed by the Japan Sumo Association (JSA), which runs six 15-day grand tournaments (honbasho) a year in January, March, May, July, September and November; the amateur game is governed by the International Sumo Federation (IFS), founded in Tokyo on 10 December 1992, which runs annual Sumo World Championships with weight classes (Japan Sumo Association, n.d.; International Sumo Federation, n.d.). At the top of the professional hierarchy stand two yokozuna — Hōshōryū, the 74th, promoted in January 2025, and Ōnosato, the 75th, promoted in May 2025 after a record 13 tournaments from his debut with six titles (Japan Sumo Association, n.d.). Women's sumo exists at amateur level — the IFS has allowed female competitors since 2001 and women's world titles have been awarded since 2001, and sumo has been on The World Games programme — but women are barred from the professional dohyō by tradition (International Sumo Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

The dohyō is a clay ring 4.55 m in diameter raised about 60 cm, with white shikiri-sen (starting lines) and a roof suspended over it; wrestlers wear the mawashi (belt) and begin each bout from a crouch, slapping hands down in the tachi-ai charge, refereed by a gyōji with two shimpan judges (Japan Sumo Association, n.d.). A bout is won when the opponent is pushed or thrown out of the ring or touches the ground with anything but the soles of his feet; closed-fist punches, hair-pulling, eye attacks and throat grabs are prohibited, and a wrestler whose topknot is untied loses (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Professional wrestlers are ranked in eight divisions from makuuchi down to jonokuchi, each honbasho lasts 15 days with one bout per wrestler per day, and a yokozuna — the highest rank, of which there have been 75 — is never demoted (Japan Sumo Association, n.d.). Amateur sumo under the IFS uses the same ring and rules but sorts athletes into weight classes (open, heavyweight, light-heavyweight, middleweight, lightweight) and, for women, allows a wrestling singlet under the mawashi and caps bouts at three minutes instead of five; the ring itself remains off-limits to women in professional sumo (International Sumo Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## History

Sumo's origins reach back to Shinto rituals of the 8th century, when Emperor Shōmu held court wrestling in 728, and professional sumo crystallized in Edo-period Tokyo in the 17th century; the first wrestlers officially recognized as yokozuna, Tanikaze and Ōnaruto, received licenses in 1789, and the modern Japan Sumo Association was founded in 1925 by merging the Tokyo and Osaka sumo associations (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Legendary icons include Taihō (33 tournament titles in the 1960s), Chiyonofuji (31 titles, 1980s), Akebono — the first foreign-born yokozuna, promoted in 1993 — and Hakuhō, the 69th yokozuna, who retired in 2021 with a record 45 titles (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women's sumo surfaced as spectacle in the 18th century, was banned in 1926, and survived underground until the first national amateur women's championship in 1997 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
