## Basic Information

Hapkido is a Korean martial art characterized by joint locks, throws, kicks, pressure points and weapon techniques, organized around the three principles won (circle), yu (fluidity, like water) and hwa (harmony); it is coordinated internationally by several bodies, notably the World Hapkido Federation (founded 1973 by Myung Kwang-sik) and the World Hapkido Association (WAHA), which held its 2026 World Hapkido Championship at Issyk-Kul, Kyrgyzstan, from 21–25 September 2026 (World Hapkido Federation, n.d.; World Hapkido Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The modern founder is Choi Yong-sool (1904–1986), who was taken to Japan as a child, reportedly trained in Daitō-ryū Aiki-jutsu under Takeda Sōkaku (a claim that remains debated), returned to Korea and began teaching in 1948 at a brewery in Daegu, where his first student was Seo Bok-seob; the name evolved from Yu Sul through Yawara and Yu Kwon Sool to Hap Ki Yu Kwon Sool and finally Hapkido (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Women train in all kwans (schools) and compete in the same categories as men at national and world championships — forms, self-defense, sparring and breaking — with self-defense against grabs and attacks a core part of the women's curriculum as well (World Hapkido Federation, n.d.) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a) (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

 Within the combat sports and martial arts family on this site, its closest relatives are [Judo](judo.html), [Karate](karate.html), [Taekwondo](taekwondo.html), [Boxing](boxing.html), [Bare Knuckle Boxing](bare-knuckle-boxing.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Hapkido has no single official world rulebook — the kwans and international federations publish their own competition regulations, commonly following the World Hapkido Federation and WAHA formats (World Hapkido Federation, n.d.; World Hapkido Association, n.d.). Gradings run from white belt (kyu grades) to black belt (dan grades, 1st–9th dan for grandmasters); competitions comprise forms (poomsae/hyung), one-step and free self-defense (hosinsul), sparring (kyorugi) with protective gear (head guard, chest protector, gloves, shin guards), and breaking (kyokpa) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Sparring is scored for controlled, techniques-based attacks — joint locks applied with control, throws, kicks and strikes to valid targets — with penalties for excessive contact, falling or passivity, and bouts are decided by points or by referee decision; illegal techniques such as strikes to the eyes, throat or groin, and attacks to the back of the head, are forbidden (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The curriculum includes empty-hand techniques plus the short stick (dan bong), cane, rope and nunchaku, systematized especially in Shin Moo Kwan under Kim Moo-hong, whose kicking curriculum was finalized in 1961; Ji Han-jae (1936–2026) further systematized the art and promoted it worldwide (Wikipedia, n.d.-b, n.d.-c). Women and men compete under identical rules in the same categories, with separate women's divisions at some championships; youth classes use adapted contact rules (World Hapkido Federation, n.d.).

## History

Choi Yong-sool (1904–1986) founded the art after returning to Korea, teaching from 1948 at the Seo brewery in Daegu to his first student Seo Bok-seob; the art passed through his early students and the kwans — Bum Moo Kwan (founded by Kim Yun-sik in 1968), Shin Moo Kwan (Kim Moo-hong, 1961) and Sung Moo Kwan (Ji Han-jae) — which shaped today's forms, kicking and self-defense curricula (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Ji Han-jae (1936–2026) was instrumental in systematizing hapkido and spreading it internationally from the 1960s onward, and the World Hapkido Federation was founded in 1973 by Myung Kwang-sik, while the Korea Hapkido Federation and later the WAHA organized world championships; Combat Hapkido, a realistic full-contact offshoot, was founded by John Pellegrini in 1990 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b, n.d.-c; World Hapkido Federation, n.d.).
