## Basic Information

Sport climbing is the competitive form of climbing on artificial walls, covering three disciplines — lead (rope climbing a route within a time limit), boulder (short routes without ropes over mats) and speed (a standardized 15-metre wall raced head-to-head) — plus the combined format used at the Olympics (World Climbing, n.d.). It is governed by World Climbing, the International Federation of Sport Climbing (IFSC) renamed in 2025, which runs the World Cup circuit, world championships and the Olympic qualification pathway; the 19th IFSC World Championships were held in Seoul, 21–28 September 2025 (World Climbing, n.d.). Sport climbing debuted at the Tokyo 2020 Olympics as a combined event and at Paris 2024 as boulder-and-lead combined plus a standalone speed event; from Los Angeles 2028 all three disciplines will be separate medal events (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Among the current leading women, Slovenia's Janja Garnbret is the dominant athlete of the era — Olympic champion in Tokyo 2020 and Paris 2024 combined, nine-time world champion in lead and ten world titles including boulder at the 2025 Seoul worlds, where she scored 99.5 in the lead final — followed by France's Oriane Bertone (silver in boulder at Seoul 2025) and Korea's Seo Chae-hyun; in speed, Poland's Aleksandra Mirosław set the world record of 6.03 and won gold at Seoul 2025, with Japan's Anon Matsufuji among the leading names. Among the men, Japan's Sorato Anraku won boulder gold at the 2025 worlds, a Korean home-nation climber took the lead title in Seoul, and China's Long Jianguo won speed gold (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; World Climbing, n.d.).

## Rules

World Climbing sanctions three disciplines with distinct rulebooks: in lead, athletes climb a route of 15 metres or more within a time limit (typically 4–6 minutes) after a 6-minute route preview, clipping quickdraws in order; scoring counts the highest hold reached, with "plus" for grasping the next hold, and ties are broken by countback to earlier rounds (World Climbing, n.d.). In boulder, athletes attempt up to four problems of about 4 metres height within a 4-minute clock each (5 minutes in finals), scoring tops and zones; a top in fewer attempts and a zone in fewer attempts beat longer solutions, and countback decides ties. In speed, two athletes race the standardized 15-metre IFSC wall head-to-head on a single-elimination bracket, times recorded to thousandths of a second, with fouls such as false starts disqualifying the run (World Climbing, n.d.). Competition formats run qualification, semifinal and final rounds, and the combined Olympic format multiplies the boulder and lead rankings before a speed final. The sport has explicit men's and women's divisions at every level, identical routes and rules for both, and age categories from youth (under 16, 18 and 20) to senior and masters at World Cup level; para climbing adds classification categories for athletes with impairments (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; World Climbing, n.d.).

## History

Competitive climbing began in the 1980s, with the first international competition held in Bardonecchia, Italy, in 1985, and the International Council for Competition Climbing (ICC) was founded in 1989 before becoming the IFSC in 2007 under the International Olympic Committee's recognition; climbing reached the Olympic program at Tokyo 2020 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Icons of the sport now retired from elite competition include France's Catherine Destivelle, the first woman to climb grade 8b and a three-time world champion in the 1990s; America's Lynn Hill, who in 1993 made the first free ascent of The Nose on El Capitan in Yosemite, one of the hardest big-wall climbs in the world; and Spain's Patxi Usobiaga, world champion in lead in the 1990s and 2000s (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
