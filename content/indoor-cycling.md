## Basic Information

Indoor cycling is the umbrella for the two hall disciplines governed by the UCI — artistic cycling (Kunstradfahren) and cycle ball (Radball) — contested at the UCI Indoor Cycling World Championships, most recently with editions such as the 2006 championships in Louny, and confirmed for The World Games 2029 program. Artistic cycling is judged like figure skating: solo and pairs riders perform choreographed routines of tricks, balances and jumps on a 14 × 11 m court to music, scored on difficulty, execution and presentation; cycle ball is a two-a-side football played on bicycles, goals counted in halves (Wikipedia, n.d.; UCI, n.d.). Both disciplines are mixed-gender at junior level and have men's and women's elite events.

## Rules

UCI rules: artistic cycling routines last five minutes (elite) with counting elements from a difficulty catalogue; falls deduct points; judges score difficulty, execution and presentation, and the rainbow jersey goes to the world champion. Cycle ball (Radball): two teams of two players on a 14 × 11 m court with 2 × 2 m goals, two halves of seven minutes each, played with a 500–750 g ball using bike and head only — except the goalkeeper, who may use hands in the goal area; fixed-gear bikes without brakes. Women's cycle-ball world titles have been awarded since 2023 (UCI, n.d.).

## History

Both disciplines trace back to the Swiss-American trick rider Nicholas Edward Kaufmann (born 1861 in Rochester, New York; died 1943 in Berlin): he became 'world champion in professional cycle trick riding' in 1888 and is regarded as the inventor of cycle-ball after a 1893 incident with a dog; Berlin artistic cyclists Paul and Otto Lüders presented cycle-ball to the German public in 1901. The first UCI World Championships were held in Leipzig in 1930 (Germany won the two-man cycle-ball title, France the six-man); artistic-cycling world titles for men date from 1956, for women from 1959, pairs from 1986 and four-rider acts from 2005. Germany is the most successful nation with over 160 world titles, ahead of Switzerland, Czechia and Austria (UCI, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).
