## Basic Information

Cricket fighting is a traditional Chinese pastime in which two male crickets are pitted against each other in a small ring; unlike blood sports such as cockfighting, the fights rarely injure the insects. It dates back more than 1,000 years to the Tang dynasty (618-907) and is still popular in China, where fights are legal but gambling on them is not. The National Cricket Fighting Championships are held annually in Beijing after regional qualifiers.

## Rules

Fighters are matched by weight class, like boxers, and are weighed to the nearest hundredth of a gram before a bout. Both crickets are placed in a small arena with high walls and a divider; handlers stimulate their antennae with a straw or fine brush, and when the divider is lifted the insects grapple with jaws and antennae. The loser is the cricket that first flees, avoids contact, stops chirping or jumps out of the arena. At the national championship each contestant may enter up to 35 insects, each weighed and labeled in advance.

## History

Cricket fighting was nurtured by Tang dynasty emperors; in the 13th century the Southern Song prime minister Jia Sidao wrote a treatise on the sport, and his obsession is said to have contributed to the empire's fall. The Cultural Revolution (1966-1976) banned it as a bourgeois pastime, but it revived afterwards among younger generations. Cricket markets thrive in cities such as Shanghai, and in 2010 more than 400 million yuan was spent on crickets in China. Prized fighters get pedigrees, special diets and even funeral services.
