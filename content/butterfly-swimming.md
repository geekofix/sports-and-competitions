## Basic Information

Butterfly swimming is the most physically demanding of the four strokes: the swimmer stays breast-down while both arms recover simultaneously over the water and the legs drive a dolphin kick; at turns and the finish both hands must touch simultaneously, and after the start and each turn one arm pull plus any number of kicks are allowed underwater up to 15 m; it is raced over 50 m, 100 m and 200 m and governed worldwide by World Aquatics (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; World Aquatics, n.d.-c). The long-course world records at the end of the 2026 season are 49.45 s over 100 m for Caeleb Dressel of the USA (2019) and 54.33 s for Gretchen Walsh of the USA, who on 2 May 2026 beat Sarah Sjöström's 2016 mark of 55.48 by more than a second; 1:50.34 over 200 m for Kristóf Milák of Hungary (2022) and 2:01.65 for Summer McIntosh of Canada, who on 5 July 2026 erased Liu Zige's 2:01.81 of 2009 — the last remaining super-suit-era women's record; and 22.27 s over 50 m for Andriy Govorov of Ukraine (2018) and Sarah Sjöström (women) (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Among the 2026 leaders, the women come first: Gretchen Walsh, the new 100 m world record holder, Summer McIntosh, who also holds the 200 m record and won the 200 m butterfly at the 2025 World Championships in Singapore, Torri Huske of the USA (pictured), the 2024 Olympic 100 m butterfly champion, and Sarah Sjöström of Sweden, the 50 m world record holder and Paris 2024 sprint-double champion in freestyle (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; BBC Sport, 2025, July 27; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). On the men's side Caeleb Dressel's 100 m record of 49.45 s from 2019 stands, and Kristóf Milák of Hungary — 200 m world record holder (1:50.34, 2022) and Olympic champion at Tokyo 2020 and Paris 2024 — heads the long-distance butterfly lists (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Butterfly is swum under the standard pool, lane, block and water-temperature regulations (World Aquatics, n.d.-b; World Aquatics, n.d.-d). The body must remain breast-down from the start, the arms must move forward simultaneously and recover over the water together, and the legs must beat together in the dolphin kick; after the start and after each turn the swimmer may perform one arm pull and any number of dolphin kicks underwater, but must surface before the 15 m mark (World Aquatics, n.d.-d).

At every turn and at the finish the butterfly swimmer must touch with both hands simultaneously; a false start disqualifies under the single-false-start rule (World Aquatics, n.d.-d). Events run as heats, semifinals and finals (50 m, 100 m) or heats and finals (200 m), timed automatically to 1/100 s, with records ratified under Rule SW12 (World Aquatics, n.d.-e). Men and women contest the same distances under identical rules, and para swimming offers butterfly in the S classes (World Aquatics, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

The butterfly stroke developed in the 1930s when swimmers and coaches — notably the USA's David Armbruster and Henry Myers — realized that recovering both arms simultaneously over the water was faster than the underwater breaststroke recovery, and it was recognized as a separate stroke in 1935 and first raced at the Olympics at Melbourne 1956 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first Olympic butterfly champions were the USA's Matt Vogel (100 m and 200 m double at Melbourne 1956) and the women's Shelley Mann (200 m); Mark Spitz added the 100 m butterfly gold at Munich 1972, and Denis Pankratov of the USSR swept the 100 m and 200 m at Seoul 1996 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Michael Phelps of the USA, pictured, became the greatest butterfly swimmer of all time with five Olympic butterfly titles across 2004–2016 and the 100 m world record of 49.45 s set at the Rio 2016 Olympics, a mark that still stands in 2026 (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
