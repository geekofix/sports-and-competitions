## Basic Information

Table tennis (also ping pong) is the racket sport of hitting a lightweight ball across a table divided by a net: two or four players trade lightning-fast strokes on a 2.74-metre table, rallies often faster than the human eye can parse. An Olympic sport since 1988, especially dominant in China, it has the largest playing population of any racket sport in the world.

## Rules

Singles players serve twice in succession from behind the end line, alternating every two points (and the ball must bounce on each side of the net's table halves), and games are scored to 11 points, winning by two, in best-of matches (typically four of seven at the top level). Doubles alternates strokes between partners with a fixed serving rotation. The racket's rubber faces (pimples in or out, with strict color and thickness rules) and the 40-millimetre celluloid or plastic ball define the equipment, while the modern game's strokes "+E+" loops with heavy topspin, drives, chops, pushes and flicks "+E+" plus footwork patterns built around the shakehand or penholder grips make it a sport of spin physics as much as reflexes at the highest level.

## History

The game descends from after-dinner Victorian lawn tennis parlor games of the 1880s (using books as nets and corks for balls) and was commercialized as ping pong and table tennis around the turn of the century; trademarked, branded and then standardized, the sport spread through central Europe and Britain, with national associations forming before the first world championship (London, 1926) crowned the game's first champions. Sponge rubbers from Japan transformed elite technique in the 1950s, and China's arrival made it the sport's superpower from the 1960s onward. Olympic inclusion arrived at Seoul 1988 for singles and doubles, and the sport's heartland (China, Japan and Korea at the top of the table plus strong European programs in Germany and Sweden) produces the fastest racket exchanges in sport and world-class champions.
