## Basic Information

The Kirkwall Ba' Game is a mass street hand-ball game played in Kirkwall, Orkney, Scotland, twice a year on Christmas Day and New Year's Day (moved to the following Monday if either falls on a Sunday) (Visit Orkney, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Two sides — the Uppies ("Up-the-Gates") and the Doonies ("Doon-the-Gates"), names derived from the Old Norse *gata* (road) — contest a cork-filled leather ball, slightly larger than a football, which is thrown up at the Mercat Cross on Broad Street in front of St Magnus Cathedral: the Boys' Ba' at 10:00 and the Men's Ba' at 13:00 (Visit Orkney, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The Uppies score by touching the ball against Mackinson's Corner, the gable end of a house at the junction of Main Street and New Scapa Road opposite the Catholic church, while the Doonies score by immersing the ball in the salt water of Kirkwall Harbour at the Basin (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). There is no referee and no formal rulebook: hundreds of players form a moving scrum that can last for hours as the ball is carried, thrown and pushed through the streets; the winning side acclaims one of its players as the winner, who keeps the ba' itself as a trophy for life (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). At the New Year 2026 games both the Boys' and the Men's Ba' were won by the Uppies, who "hit the wall" at Mackinson's Corner (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Today the game is played by men only, but a Women's Ba' was contested exactly twice, on Christmas Day 1945 and New Year's Day 1946 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

The Kirkwall Ba' Game has no written rules and no referee — it is a form of mob football in which almost anything goes except deliberate injury (Visit Orkney, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Players join the Uppies or the Doonies by birthplace, family allegiance or how they first entered Kirkwall — traditionally, those born south of the cathedral were Uppies and those from the north Doonies (Visit Orkney, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The ba' — a cork-filled, leather-covered ball slightly larger than a football, traditionally brown or black — is thrown up at the Mercat Cross and may be carried, thrown, pushed or kicked through the streets; the play often forms a dense scrum from which the ball suddenly breaks free down a narrow lane (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The game ends when the ba' reaches a goal: the Uppies by touching it against the wall of Mackinson's Corner, the Doonies by immersing it in the salt water of Kirkwall Bay, normally at the Basin in the harbour (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). After the goal, the winning side acclaims a popular individual as winner, who is carried shoulder-high and keeps the ba' for life — winners typically have played for 20 years or more (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The game is played twice a year, on Christmas Day and New Year's Day, each day with a Boys' Ba' (10:00) and a Men's Ba' (13:00), and around 15 similar Ba' games survive across the United Kingdom (Visit Orkney, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Today only men play; a Women's Ba' was attempted after World War II (see History) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## History

The game's origins are lost in the mists of Kirkwall's Norse past; the Uppie/Doonie split is said to derive from the old town gates, and the earliest solid records date to around 1650, with documents from 1684 and a 1797 description calling football "the principal diversion of the common people, which they practise with great dexterity" (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). A 19th-century legend about the merchant Jeremiah Tulloch losing his head at the town's boundary — the supposed origin of the game — has no historical evidence, and the modern customs provably date only from the mid-19th century (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Immediately after World War II a Women's Ba' was established in Kirkwall: it was played exactly twice, on Christmas Day 1945 and New Year's Day 1946, and both games were won by Uppies — Barbara Yule in 1945 and Violet Couper in 1946; Yule's winning ba' is displayed in the Orkney Museum. The women's game was then abolished amid public disapproval of women in such a physical spectacle, and has never been revived (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). In recent decades the games have grown to include players from all over Orkney, with several hundred participants; at New Year 2026 the Uppies won both the Boys' and the Men's Ba' (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
