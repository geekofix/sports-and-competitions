## Basic Information

Pankration (from the Greek pan, "all", and kratos, "power") was the full-contact unarmed combat sport of ancient Greece, combining boxing, wrestling, kicking, joint locks and chokes on the ground, making it the closest ancient equivalent of modern mixed martial arts. It was introduced at the 33rd Olympic Games in 648 BC, where the only prohibited techniques were biting and eye-gouging. Today the name is revived for a modern full-contact combat sport with weight classes and protective equipment, recognized since 2010 by United World Wrestling (UWW), and it is related to [wrestling](wrestling.html), [boxing](boxing.html), [kickboxing](kickboxing.html) and [Brazilian jiu-jitsu](brazilian-jiu-jitsu.html).

## Rules

In the ancient Olympic pankration there were almost no rules: everything was allowed except biting and gouging the eyes, bouts had no time limit and no weight classes, and a fight ended only by submission or knockout, which made it brutal; competitors fought in a dug-out pit (skamma). Modern pankration, as practiced under UWW and at World Pankration Championships, is a regulated full-contact sport with weight classes, rounds with time limits, referees and protective gear; strikes, takedowns, wrestling and submissions are allowed, and the sport is divided into ano pankration, fought standing, and kato pankration, which continues on the ground.

## History

Greek mythology credits the heroes Heracles and Theseus with inventing pankration, and the sport was added to the Olympic program at Olympia in 648 BC, where it remained until the ancient Games were abolished by the Roman emperor Theodosius I in 393 AD. Champions became celebrities and were recruited as elite soldiers; the most famous pankratiast was Arrhichion of Phigalia, who died during his victorious title defense at the 564 BC Olympics and was crowned posthumously, while Sostratus of Sicyon won three successive Olympic titles in 364, 360 and 356 BC, famously bending his opponents' fingers. The sport was excluded when the modern Olympics were revived in 1896 as too violent. Modern revival efforts began with the Greek-American Jim Arvanitis, who coined "Mu Tau" in 1969, and in 2010 pankration was adopted by FILA, now United World Wrestling, giving it an international competitive structure again.
