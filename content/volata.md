## Basic Information

Volata (also called pallavolata) was a team ball game created in Italy in 1926 under the fascist regime, explicitly modeled on the Roman ball game harpastum and promoted as "the most Italian of sports" by the journalist Lando Ferretti; its rulebook was written by Augusto Turati, secretary of the National Fascist Party and from 1928 president of CONI, Italy's Olympic committee (Wikipedia, n.d.). Two teams of eight — one goalkeeper, two defenders, three midfielders and two forwards — played a 400 g leather ball on a 90 × 60 m field toward goals 5 m wide and 2.44 m high set on the end lines (Wikipedia, n.d.). The game was played by men's company teams (dopolavoro) — no women's competition is documented for the 1926–1933 era — and today it survives only in recreational mixed groups and historical demonstrations (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## Rules

The pitch measured 90 × 60 m with goals 5 m wide and 2.44 m high on each end line, and the ball was a leather sphere of about 400 g with a circumference of 68–71 cm (Wikipedia, n.d.). Each side fielded eight players — one goalkeeper, two defenders, three midfielders and two forwards; defenders were not allowed to cross the halfway line, the goalkeeper could hold the ball for at most three seconds, and no player could enter the elliptical area around the goal marked by a line 8 m from it (Wikipedia, n.d.). Tackling was allowed only between waist and shoulders with both arms, the ball carrier had to release the ball after a challenge and could run with it for at most 10 m, and a match lasted 60 minutes in three periods of 20 minutes (Wikipedia, n.d.). More than 100 clubs had taken up the game by 1926, and the only Italian championship, in 1930, was won by the Milan works team Dopolavoro Richard Ginori before the regime abandoned the sport in 1933 in favor of association football (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

Volata was created in 1926 when Augusto Turati — secretary of the PNF and from 1928 president of CONI — commissioned a rulebook for a new "Italian" ball game inspired by the ancient Roman harpastum, and the fascist press hailed it as "the most Italian of sports" (Wikipedia, n.d.). Within months more than 100 clubs across Italy had adopted it, and the regime organized the single national championship of 1930, won by Dopolavoro Richard Ginori of Milan (Wikipedia, n.d.). The experiment was abandoned in 1933, when the regime turned to association football as Italy's mass sport, and volata survived only in pockets of recreational play and historical reenactment (Wikipedia, n.d.).
