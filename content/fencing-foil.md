## Basic Information

Foil is the lightest of the three fencing weapons — a thrusting-only sword of at most 500 g — in which a touch counts only on the opponent's torso, covered by the conductive lamé, and the rules of right of way decide priority when both fencers land a hit in the same action (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men's foil has been contested at every Olympic Games since Athens 1896, and women's foil was the first women's Olympic fencing event, introduced at Paris 1924 (Olympics.com, n.d.). The weapon is governed by the Fédération Internationale d'Escrime (FIE), whose 2026 World Championships in Hong Kong (22–30 July 2026) included men's and women's individual and team foil among the twelve events (FIE, n.d.-a). See [Fencing](fencing.html) on this site for the shared rules of the sport (Fédération Internationale d'Escrime, n.d.-a) (Fédération Internationale d'Escrime, n.d.-b).

In the 2026/27 season the women's foil is led by Italy's Martina Favaretto and Anna Cristino, with the American Olympic champion Lee Kiefer and Lauren Scruggs among the chasing pack; on the men's side the Czech Alexander Choupenitch tops the FIE world rankings in foil (FIE, n.d.-b).

## Rules

The foil blade is at most 90 cm long, the assembled weapon at most 110 cm and 500 g; only the tip scores, and it must be depressed with a pressure of at least 500 g (FIE, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The valid target is the trunk of the body covered by the conductive lamé, including the lower part of the mask bib; touches on the head (except the bib), arms or legs are off-target — they halt the phrase but score nothing (FIE, n.d.-b).

Right of way governs every exchange: the fencer who attacks with the arm extended — by lunge or flèche — has priority, as does the defender who parries the attack and immediately ripostes, the attacker who remises after a parry, and the counter-attacker who takes over an incoming attack; point-in-line also holds priority if the attack is parried (FIE, n.d.-b). When both fencers land a hit within the scoring apparatus window of a few hundred milliseconds, only the fencer with right of way scores; if neither has priority, no touch is awarded (FIE, n.d.-b).

Pool bouts last three minutes and end at 5 touches; direct-elimination bouts are three periods of three minutes with one-minute breaks, first to 15 touches, with a one-minute priority period if tied at 15 (FIE, n.d.-b). Team events are relays of nine bouts counting to a cumulative 45 touches (FIE, n.d.-b). Fouls such as covering the target, turning the back, corps-à-corps and leaving the piste with both feet are penalized with yellow, red or black cards (FIE, n.d.-b).

Men and women fence foil under identical rules, with a compulsory chest protector for women at all weapons; women's foil has been an Olympic event since Paris 1924 (FIE, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Age classifications are cadet (under 17), junior (under 20), senior (20 and over) and veteran (40 and over), each with World Championships and World Cup circuits run by the FIE (FIE, n.d.-a).

## History

The foil derives from the French fleuret, the blunted training weapon of the 18th-century smallsword; because duels were fought to first blood, the vital target — and hence the modern valid target — is the torso (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Foil and sabre were the fencing events of the first modern Olympics in Athens 1896, and electric scoring for foil was introduced at the Melbourne 1956 Olympics (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women's foil debuted at the Paris 1924 Games and was the only women's Olympic fencing event until women's épée was added in 1996 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Among the icons of the weapon are the Italians Nedo Nadi (1894–1940), who won individual and team foil gold at Antwerp 1920, and Valentina Vezzali (b. 1974), who collected nine Olympic medals across five Games and won three consecutive individual Olympic titles in 2004, 2008 and 2012; France's Christian d'Oriola (1928–2007) won four gold medals at London 1948 and Melbourne 1952 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
