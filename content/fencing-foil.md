## Basic Information

Foil is one of the three weapons of modern fencing, in which touches are scored only on the opponent's torso (the metallic lame jacket area), with the rule of "right of way" deciding priority when both fencers hit at the same time. See [Fencing](fencing.html) for the shared rules of the sport; this entry covers the foil-specific rules used at the FIE World Championships and the Olympics.

## Rules

In foil, the valid target is the trunk of the body (covered by the conductive lame), excluding arms, legs, head and hands. A touch counts only for the fencer with right of way: the attacker who starts the attack, or the defender who parries and immediately ripostes; if both hit within the machine's simultaneity window (about 300 ms for foil), only the fencer with right of way scores. A bout is to 15 touches in three 3-minute periods; if scores are tied after time, a one-minute priority period decides. Fencers must wear the lame and mask; the FIE rules penalize corps-à-corps, covering the target area, and turning the back with cards. At team events, bouts run relay-style, with the score counting up to 45 touches.

## History

The foil (fleuret) developed from the light training sword of the 18th century and was the weapon of the first Olympic fencing events in Athens 1896 (professionals' foil was the only fencing event at the first Games). The FIE, founded 1913, standardized foil rules, and the electric scoring machine was introduced in the 1930s. Italy and France have dominated foil history, with Italian masters such as Nedo Nadi (two golds 1920) and modern champions Andrea Cassarà and Daniele Garozzo (Rio 2016 gold). Foil is often considered the most tactical weapon because of the right-of-way rules.
