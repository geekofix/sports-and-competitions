## Basic Information

The longsword is the most practiced weapon of Historical European martial arts (HEMA): the two-handed sword of the German school of fencing (Liechtenauer tradition), trained today with blunted steel simulators (feders) and fought in tournaments at longsword events around the world (Wikipedia, n.d.-c; HEMA Alliance, n.d.). Around 50 German fight books survive, from the Codex Germanicus 3227a (c. 1389) and the rhymed Zettel (c. 1430s) to the glosses of the Five Masters and Joachim Meyer's 1570 treatise, and the longsword is the weapon of the majority of German-school clubs (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Major longsword tournaments include Swordfish in Gothenburg, Longpoint in Baltimore, SoCal Swordfight and the Örebro Open, where fighters compete in pools and direct elimination under published rulesets (HEMA Alliance, n.d.). Competitions are mixed-gender with identical rules for women and men, and women regularly reach the finals of the biggest longsword events (HEMA Alliance, n.d.) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Longsword bouts are fought with steel federschwert simulators of roughly 1.2–1.7 kg with a flexible tip, plus fencing mask, gorget, gloves and joint protection; the target areas are the head and torso foremost, with limbs depending on the ruleset (HEMA Alliance, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-c). Under common tournament rules — for example the HEMAA longsword ruleset — clean cuts and thrusts score points (typically 3–4 for head/torso, fewer for limbs), an afterblow landing within one tempo counts for the defender, doubles (mutual hits) score for neither or both depending on the ruleset, blade grabs are allowed in longsword (unlike rapier), disarms may score bonus points, and a bout ends at a point limit or time cap, run as pools followed by direct with a central referee and judges (HEMA Alliance, n.d.). Techniques come from the sources: the four wards (Vier Huten), the five hews (Fünf Meisterhau), the Vor (before) and Nach (after) principle of initiative, winding (Winden), the Zwerchhau, Schielhau and Krumphau among many named actions (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Women and men compete under identical rules, usually in mixed brackets, with occasional separate women's brackets at large events; junior categories with lighter weapons exist at youth events (HEMA Alliance, n.d.). The style is documented by the world-level bodies of HEMA — the HEMA Alliance and HEMAC — since there is no single world federation for HEMA (HEMA Alliance, n.d.).

## History

The German longsword tradition is first documented in the Codex Germanicus 3227a (c. 1389) and the rhymed Zettel attributed to Johannes Liechtenauer (c. 1430s); the glossators known as the Five Masters — Sigmund Ringeck, Jud Lew, Andres Lignitzer and others — commented on the Zettel in the 15th and 16th centuries, and Joachim Meyer systematized the art in his 1570 Gründtliche Beschreibung der Kunst des Fechtens (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). The longsword was the standard sidearm of the German Landsknechte and burgher militias in the 15th and 16th centuries, fought in judicial duels and on the battlefield, before giving way to the rapier and smallsword in the 17th century (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). The modern HEMA revival of the longsword began in the 1990s with online transcriptions and early clubs, and by the 2010s dedicated longsword tournaments such as Swordfish (Gothenburg, since 2011), Longpoint (Baltimore) and the Örebro Open had established a worldwide competitive circuit (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
