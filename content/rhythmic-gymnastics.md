## Basic Information

Rhythmic gymnastics is a judged gymnastics discipline in which individual gymnasts or groups of five perform choreographed routines with hand apparatus — rope, hoop, ball, clubs and ribbon — combining ballet, dance and acrobatics with complex apparatus handling. It is governed by the International Gymnastics Federation (FIG), has been an Olympic sport for women since the 1984 Los Angeles Games (individual) with group events added at Atlanta 1996, and world championships have been staged since 1963. The eastern European and Asian schools — above all Russia, Bulgaria and, in the group ranks, Italy — have historically collected most of the titles.

## Rules

Under the FIG Code of Points, each routine is scored by adding a Difficulty score (the value of the body difficulties — leaps, pivots, balances and flexibilities — plus the apparatus and dynamic elements with rotation, and in group routines the exchanges between gymnasts) and an Execution score (deductions for technical faults in body and apparatus work, starting from 10). Routines must show continuous linkage between movements and apparatus mastery — throws, catches, rolls and rotations of the apparatus — with music, and the apparatus must never be static; the hoop is 80-90 cm inside, the ball 18-19 cm, the ribbon a 6 m satin strip, the clubs about 40-45 cm and the rope hemp or synthetic. Individual routines last 75-90 seconds and group routines about 2 minutes 15 seconds to 2 minutes 30. World championships are held annually (except Olympic years at some points in the cycle) with individual all-around and apparatus finals plus group all-around and group single-apparatus finals, and Olympic competition comprises an individual all-around and a group all-around only.

## History

Rhythmic gymnastics grew out of the European "rhythmic" and expressive-gymnastics movements of the early 20th century, associated with figures such as Émile Jaques-Dalcroze, Rudolf von Laban and Irène Popard, and crystallized as a competitive sport in the Soviet Union, with the FIG taking over stewardship in 1961 and staging the first world championship at Budapest in 1963 (then called modern gymnastics). Olympic status came at Los Angeles 1984, with the group discipline joining in 1996, and the apparatus program has changed with the times, while successive scoring reforms since the 2000s have pushed the sport toward ever greater acrobatic difficulty and artistry. Champions of the Soviet and Russian school, such as Marina Lobatch, Ekaterina Serebrianskaya and the two-time Olympic champion Evgenia Kanaeva, along with the Bulgarian individual stars and Italy's group dynasty, have defined each generation. The discipline is contested by women at the FIG elite level, though men's rhythmic gymnastics has a dedicated competition tradition in Japan.
