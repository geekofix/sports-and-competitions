## Basic Information

Wheel gymnastics (German wheel, Rhönrad) is a gymnastics discipline performed on a large double-rimmed wheel in which athletes roll, spin and tumble — propelling the wheel in straight lines or circles while executing balances, jumps and turns inside it and on its rims. The wheel is built in sizes up to roughly 245 centimetres in diameter and is adjusted to the athlete's height, and competition is held in three disciplines: straight-line exercises, spiral, and vault.

The sport was developed in Germany, is especially popular in Germany, Switzerland and Japan, and is governed internationally by the International Wheel Gymnastics Federation, which has held world championships since 1995.

## Rules

In the straight-line discipline the wheel rolls back and forth across the floor while the gymnast performs compulsory and free exercises — leaps, drops into the wheel, balances on the straps and turns — scored for difficulty and execution like other gymnastics codes. In the spiral, the wheel itself rolls in large circles on the floor, tilted onto its side, while the athlete rotates with it and changes positions within. In the vault, the gymnast sprints alongside the rolling wheel and hurdles onto it, performing a fast rotating vault over the top with a landing mat beyond the wheel. Judges score difficulty, technique and amplitude, with men's and women's events in each discipline.

## History

The German wheel was invented by Otto Feick in Schönau an der Brend in the early 1920s and was patented in 1925, drawing inspiration from a ship's lantern ring and from a childhood game his grandfather showed him. It was demonstrated at the 1936 Berlin Olympics and spread as a form of artistic gymnastics in German clubs, with gymnastic wheel turning embedded in club culture through the twentieth century. The International Wheel Gymnastics Federation was founded in 1995, the first world championships took place that decade, and the sport has since grown steadily, with world championships held every two years and developing programmes in Asia, Europe and the Americas.
