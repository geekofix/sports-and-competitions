## Basic Information

Skeleton is the head-first sliding sport: athletes race a small sled, lying face down with the chin inches above the ice, down the same refrigerated tracks used by bobsleigh and luge, reaching speeds above 130 km/h (over 80 mph) and up to 5 g of acceleration. Governed today by the International Bobsleigh and Skeleton Federation (IBSF), it is both the oldest of the sliding disciplines \u2014 born on the Cresta Run above St. Moritz \u2014 and a full Olympic sport with men's and women's events since Salt Lake City 2002.

## Rules

A skeleton race begins with a sprint push, the athlete pushing the sled one- or two-handed from the start before diving aboard head-first; from then on the slider steers by shifting weight and applying shoulder, knee and head torque to the sled \u2014 modern sleds of steel runners on a platform chassis respond to tiny shoulder and knee movements, and because the slider's centre of gravity is so low, flips are rarer than in luge. Olympic and World Cup competitions consist of timed runs down tracks of roughly 1,200\u20131,500 metres, with the aggregate of (usually) four runs \u2014 two in World Cup races \u2014 deciding the winner; rankings are decided to hundredths of a second, and the fastest combined time wins. Safety rules mandate helmets with chin guards, spiked shoes for the push and controlled sled dimensions and weights; on the shared tracks the skeleton start order follows seeding, and mixed team events (added from the Milano Cortina 2026 programme) combine results of one man and one woman from each nation.

## History

Skeleton's birthplace is St. Moritz, where British holidaymakers developed tobogganing as a resort sport from the early 1870s and the Kulm Hotel's Outdoor Amusement Committee built the Cresta Run in 1884; the St. Moritz Tobogganing Club (SMTC) followed in 1887, and the same year a rider nicknamed "Mr. Cornish" rode the Grand National head-first on a new American flexible-runner sled, a style that by 1890 all competitors had adopted \u2014 the head-first "Cresta" style became known as skeleton (possibly a garbled anglicisation of the Norwegian "kjelke", sled), with the metal skeleton sled standardised by L. P. Child's 1892 design. The sport appeared at the Winter Olympics at St. Moritz in 1928 and 1948 \u2014 both times on the Cresta Run, where Nino Bibbia of the host town won the 1948 gold \u2014 but then vanished from the Games for 54 years because competition was possible only on the natural-ice St. Moritz track; the International Bobsleigh and Tobogganing Federation (FIBT, founded 1923) rejuvenated the discipline on artificial ice tracks, with the first refrigerated track at K\u00f6nigssee in 1969, a new sled in 1970, world championships from 1982 and a World Cup from 1986. In October 1999 the IOC readmitted skeleton to the Games, and Salt Lake City 2002 staged men's and women's events \u2014 the women's race won by Britain's Alex Coomber, icons of the modern era include Britain's Amy Williams and Lizzy Yarnold, both Olympic champions, and their nation's honour roll is outsized for a country with no ice track of its own;
