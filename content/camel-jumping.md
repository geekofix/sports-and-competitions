## Basic Information

Camel jumping is a traditional sport of the Zaraniq (al-Zaraneeq) tribe on the Tihama plain along the Red Sea coast of western Yemen, around Al Hudaydah and the villages southeast of Hodeidah such as Bajil and Zabid. Barefoot men sprint down a runway and vault in a single bound over a row of standing dromedary camels lined up side by side; the competitor who clears the most camels wins. Events are held at weddings and festivals such as Mawlid and the al-Khamis festival at the end of the palm season, and camel jumping was also shown at the Sanaa Summer Festival. There is no formal governing body; the custom is maintained by the tribe itself, and as of 2026 only informal contests take place amid the ongoing conflict in Yemen (Smithsonian Magazine, n.d.) (Topend Sports, n.d.). The unofficial record of six camels is held by Zaid Omer of Yemen, and Bhayder Mohammed Yusef Qubaisi is regarded as the champion jumper of his generation (Topend Sports, n.d.).

On the gender side, women have traditionally been excluded from the competitions, which are part of male tribal gatherings; women take part in the accompanying singing, dancing and festival culture but not in the jumping itself (Smithsonian Magazine, n.d.). Note for readers: the sport uses live camels as obstacles, so it raises animal-welfare questions that are discussed in the context of Yemeni festival culture (The World, 2016).

## Rules

For a contest, camels of similar height are lined up closely in a single row and held by their reins and tails; the jumper takes a running start on flat ground, uses a small mound of packed mud and dried weeds as a takeoff ramp, and attempts to clear the whole row in one leap, landing on the far side on his feet. There are no points for style — only the number of camels cleared counts — and each competitor normally gets one try, with the row reset between attempts (Topend Sports, n.d.). Ordinary competitors clear four to five camels; professionals have been known to clear six or seven, and the unofficial record stands at six camels (Zaid Omer). Fouls: touching a camel with any part of the body, stumbling on landing or leaving the runway results in a failed attempt; there is no time limit. Equipment: none beyond the camels themselves and the takeoff mound — jumpers are barefoot. Men's and women's rules would be identical, but women do not traditionally compete; children sometimes attempt shorter rows at festivals (Smithsonian Magazine, n.d.). Formats: informal one-against-one contests and group events at weddings and festivals; no league or championship structure exists (Topend Sports, n.d.).


## History

The sport is said to have begun generations ago as a dare between two Zaraniq tribesmen haggling over the price of a camel. It remained a tribal tradition in the Tihama desert for centuries, declined during much of the 20th century, and has been revived among younger tribe members in recent decades (Smithsonian Magazine, n.d.). Photojournalists such as Adam Reynolds (Smithsonian) and Ed Ou documented the jumpers in the 2000s and 2010s, bringing international attention — Reuters photographs showed camel jumping at the Sanaa Summer Festival in 2013, and in 2016 The World (PRX) reported on camel festivals combining wrestling, racing, jumping and beauty contests (The World, 2016). The jumpers are famous throughout Yemen for their speed, strength and courage, and the sport is accompanied by traditional singing and dancing; as of 2026, amid the war in Yemen, only informal contests in tribal areas continue (Topend Sports, n.d.).
