## Basic Information

Kitesurfing (kiteboarding) is a water sport in which the rider stands on a board — a twin-tip, a directional surfboard or a hydrofoil — and is pulled across the water by a large controllable kite flown on 20-27 m lines, steered with a control bar while wearing a harness. It combines aspects of wakeboarding, windsurfing, surfing and paragliding, and is practiced for racing, freestyle, wave riding and big-air jumps. The sport is governed by World Sailing for Olympic and international racing, with the International Kiteboarding Association (IKA) running freestyle and big-air competition.

## Rules

In racing (course racing), fleets of up to 20 riders compete on upwind-downwind courses around buoys, with heats and finals; the first across the finish line wins, and Formula Kite (hydrofoil) is the Olympic class. In freestyle, riders perform tricks — unhooked jumps, rotations and board-offs — judged on difficulty, execution and style. In wave riding, riders surf waves while powered by the kite. Safety rules require a quick-release system on the harness, a helmet and impact vest in competition, a life jacket in many venues, and right-of-way rules on the water (the rider with the right of way has right foot forward). Riders must stay clear of swimmers and boats.

## History

Kitesurfing was developed in the late 1990s by combining traction kites (from kite buggying and power kiting) with water boards; the Legaignoux brothers of France and Cory Roeseler of the US are among the pioneers, and the first commercial kites and boards appeared around 1997-1998. The sport exploded in popularity in the 2000s, with professional tours, world championships and kitesurfing schools worldwide; it was shortlisted for the Olympics and made its debut as Formula Kite at Paris 2024. Famous riders include Robby Naish (windsurfing and kitesurfing pioneer), Youri Zoon (freestyle) and Olympic champions of Paris 2024.
