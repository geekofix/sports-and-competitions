## Basic Information

Silambam is a weapon-based Indian martial art originating from Tamil Nadu in South India, whose core weapon is a staff — traditionally bamboo, about 1.68 m (five and a half feet) long, its length matched to the practitioner's height. Mentioned in the Tamil Sangam literature, silambam embraces a full range of traditional weapons alongside the staff and, in competition, is practised both as pre-arranged forms and as sparring with light weapons under sporting rules. Its international governing body is the World Silambam Association (WSA), founded in 1999.

## Rules

Training begins with footwork and the high-speed rotation of the staff — the art's signature skill — before progressing to pairs work, sparring and the wider armoury, which includes the sedikuchi (short stick, often wielded in pairs), the curved sword vaal, the flexible sword urumi, the machete aruval, the whip savuku, the deer-horn thrusting weapon maru, the knife katti and the push-dagger kattari, while the unarmed component is the separate art of kuttu varisai. The staff itself is preferably bamboo or teak, strengthened by immersion in water and beating on water surfaces, and often tipped with metal rings; its length should reach about three fingers above the practitioner's forehead. Modern silambam competition under World Silambam Association sporting rules includes individual and pair forms judged on technique, plus semi-contact sparring events with protective equipment, scored for touches with the staff on permitted target zones.

## History

References in the Silappadikaram and other Sangam-era literature show that staff fighting has been practised in the Tamil country since at least the fourth century BC; the art is linked in tradition to the Kurinji hills, whose silambam bamboo gave the art its name, and the ancient city of Madurai became its spreading-point, with the staff traded as far afield as Egypt, Greece and Rome and carried through Southeast Asia by Tamil seafarers. Tamil kings maintained "Thadi Pattalam" — staff-armies — of silambam soldiers, and leaders such as Puli Thevar, Veerapandiya Kattabomman and the Maruthu brothers relied on silambam-trained fighters against the British East India Company in the eighteenth century. Under colonial rule the art was suppressed and declined sharply, but after Indian independence it revived strongly in Tamil Nadu and the diaspora: the art gained a national federation presence in the 2000s, today is taught in school and university physical-education programmes and has been spread worldwide by Tamil communities, with international federations running world championships.
