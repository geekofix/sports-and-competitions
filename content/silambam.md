## Basic Information

Silambam is the ancient Tamil martial art of the long bamboo staff, practised in Tamil Nadu and among the Tamil diaspora; the name derives from silam ("hill") and bambu (the bamboo of Kerala's Kurinji hills), and the art is documented in Sangam literature from at least the 4th century BC, including the Silappadikaram (Wikipedia, n.d.). The weapon is a staff of about 1.68 m — cut to the wielder's forehead height — of bamboo or teak with metal-ringed ends, and the system also includes the curved machete (aruval), knife (katti), flexible sword (urumi), whip (savuku) and a three-foot stick (sedikuchi), plus unarmed animal-form sequences (kuttu varisai) (Wikipedia, n.d.; WSA, n.d.). Today silambam is practised and competed worldwide under the World Silambam Association (WSA) and the International Silambam Federation, with competition rules (5th edition, 22 July 2021) used in 22 countries (International Silambam Federation, n.d.; WSA, n.d.).

Women train and compete in silambam alongside men — the WSA competition rules include sub-junior and junior categories for both male and female athletes, and female exponents such as the practitioner shown above demonstrate the art's locking techniques internationally; masters are traditionally called asaan, periyasaan or iyan, and Guruji Murugan Chillayah, who documented the name "Silambam" on 22 November 1999, teaches women students among his international disciples (WSA, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).

 Within the combat sports and martial arts family on this site, its closest relatives are [Kalaripayattu](kalaripayattu.html), [Boxing](boxing.html), [Bare Knuckle Boxing](bare-knuckle-boxing.html), [Kickboxing](kickboxing.html), [Muay Thai](muay-thai.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Under the WSA competition rules (5th edition, 22 July 2021), events include nedunkambu veechu (single-stick sparring), nadukambu suttru (single middle-stick circling) and the stick point game, contested in age categories that include sub-junior and junior divisions for both male and female competitors (WSA, n.d.; International Silambam Federation, n.d.). Competitors wear the traditional waistband (kattam) with its twelve coloured portions, and the long staff — about 1.68 m, of bamboo or teak with metal-ringed ends — is used with controlled strikes to designated target areas while asaan/periyasaan masters referee the bouts (Wikipedia, n.d.; WSA, n.d.). Men's and women's events use the same rules, with separate categories by age and gender (WSA, n.d.).

## History

Silambam is attested in Sangam-era Tamil literature from at least the 4th century BC — the Silappadikaram describes stick fighting — and the bamboo staff came from the Kurinji hills of Kerala; legendary warriors such as Veerapandiya Kattabomman and the Maruthu brothers (active 1760–1799) trained in the art against the British (Wikipedia, n.d.). After the second Palaiyakkarar war the British banned the martial art in 1804, and it survived taught in disguise as the dance-like Silambattam; it was revived in the 20th century and organised internationally: the name Silambam was documented by Guruji Murugan Chillayah on 22 November 1999 and registered with the Malaysian Ministry of Home Affairs, the World Silambam Association gained UN ECOSOC consultative status (recommended 21–30 January 2019, New York) and joined GAWSF in 2019, the All India Silambam Federation was registered in Tamil Nadu in 2004, the International Silambam Federation was formed on 17 August 2010 in Nagercoil, and Silambam Asia (SILA) covers 12 Asian countries (WSA, n.d.; International Silambam Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).
