## Basic Information

Ssireum is Korea's traditional folk wrestling: two athletes grapple in a circular sand ring about 7 metres across, each gripping a *satba* belt looped around the opponent's waist and right thigh, starting from a kneeling position, and the first to force any part of the opponent's body at knee level or higher to the ground — or, in some formats, out of the ring — loses (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). It has been inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity since 2018, and professional ssireum in Korea is organized by the Korea Ssireum Association with four weight classes named after mountains — Taebaek (80 kg), Geumgang (90 kg), Halla (105 kg) and Baekdu (up to 160 kg) — where the season champion is named *cheonhajangsa* and traditionally receives an ox (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The World Ssireum Federation organizes international championships, the first held in Šiauliai, Lithuania, with 120 wrestlers from 40 countries. Women's ssireum exists under the same rules — women wear tops over the belt and compete among themselves — so the sport is open to both genders in separate divisions (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Among today's leading Korean professionals, the *Baekdujangsa* (heavyweight) title holders of recent seasons include Lee Man-gi in the lower classes and the veteran Kang Ho-dong, now retired, who won the *cheonhajangsa* five times and the *Baekdujangsa* seven times; women's professional ssireum has its own national championship in Korea with the same belt-and-sand rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## Rules

A ssireum match takes place in a *syrym jang*, a sand circle about 7 m in diameter and 10–20 cm deep; the two wrestlers, wearing the *dongbal* shorts and the *satba* belt around the waist and right thigh, start kneeling facing each other and grip each other's belt at the *baro japki* signal (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). A wrestler wins when any part of the opponent's body at or above the knee touches the sand; being pushed out of the ring restarts the bout rather than ending it (unlike sumo), and striking, tripping with the feet outside the grip or twisting fingers is a foul. Professional bouts are best-of-three in early rounds and best-of-five in the final, judged by three side referees plus a chief referee who can order a restart. The four professional weight classes are Taebaek (up to 80 kg), Geumgang (90 kg), Halla (105 kg) and Baekdu (up to 160 kg), each awarding a season title and the *cheonhajangsa* overall title with the prize ox (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women's ssireum follows the same rules with women competing among themselves, and amateur and international events run open and women's divisions (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## History

Ssireum's origins reach back to the 4th century, depicted in the Goguryeo tomb murals at Gakjeochong, and it flourished as a folk contest during the Joseon dynasty, when it was a highlight of the Dano festival; it was inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2018 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Professional ssireum organized under the Korea Ssireum Association peaked in the 1980s and 1990s; icons now retired include Lee Man-gi, one of the most celebrated *Baekdujangsa* of the modern era, Kang Ho-dong — seven-time *Baekdujangsa*, five-time *cheonhajangsa* and the youngest man ever to win the overall title — and Choi Hong-man, who later became a K-1 kickboxer and MMA fighter (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
