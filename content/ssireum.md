## Basic Information

Ssireum is the traditional folk wrestling style and a historical national sport of Korea: two contestants, each wearing a satba (a belt wrapped around the waist and thigh), grip each other by the belt and use leverage, trips and throws to bring any part of the opponent's body above the knee to the ground. Contested in a sand pit on festal days since antiquity, it is inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage lists following an unprecedented joint application by North and South Korea.

## Rules

A match begins with the two wrestlers facing each other; in the modern left-sided style, each grips the opponent's satba with both hands, one hand at the waist band and one at the thigh. From that locked position, wrestlers deploy a repertoire of techniques "+E+" inner and outer leg trips, twists, lifts and throws that turn the opponent's torso and shift their weight onto a collapsing leg "+E+" to topple them so that any part of their body above the knee touches the sand: the first such fall loses the bout. Techniques are expressly intended to inflict little harm, and weight classes (introduced in 1956) plus multi-bout formats keep elite tournaments fair and fast; in keeping with centuries of tradition, the winner of major tournaments still receives an ox, a prize symbolizing the champion's strength.

## History

The earliest evidence of ssireum dates to the Goguryeo period (37 BC to 668 AD), when a tomb mural of the era shows two figures locked in the wrestler's hold; the game then grew into a popular pastime of royal militaries and common people through the Goryeo and Joseon dynasties, with tournaments held on feast days such as Dano, the fifth day of the fifth lunar month. The first modern competition was staged in Seoul in 1912, and the first nationwide championship followed in the 1930s as the sport organized national championships; after the division of the peninsula, ssireum became a nationally televised professional sport in South Korea, with the Korea Ssireum Association running the National Championship, the President's Cup since 1964, and professional leagues that made stars of heavyweight champions. In 2018, following an unprecedented joint application by both Korean states, ssireum was inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage lists, and the sport remains a living symbol of Korean culture at folk festivals and professional arenas alike.
