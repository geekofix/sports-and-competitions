## Basic Information

Popinjay (Dutch: *papegaai schieten*, "shooting the parrot") is a traditional archery game of Belgium and the Netherlands in which members of a schutterij (archery guild) shoot arrows at a wooden bird — the papegaai — mounted atop a tall pole, trying to knock it down; the first to do so becomes the "King" (or "Emperor") of the guild for the year (Wikipedia, n.d.). It belongs to the archery family and is closely related to target archery and the other traditional guild shoots of Flanders and the Netherlands.

The bird is a carved wooden figure, historically about the size of a parrot, fixed on a pole that can be more than 25–30 m high; guilds shoot from a marked line at the base, one arrow at a time in turns, and the shoot continues until the bird falls (Wikipedia, n.d.). In modern competitions targets of standardized size are used and scoring may be by rounds rather than the pure knock-down format (Wikipedia, n.d.). The photo above shows the guild tradition: archers of a Belgian schutterij on the meadow where popinjay is shot at village festivals.

## Rules

Rules are set by the local guild federations: shooting distance and target height are fixed before the shoot, each member shoots in a drawn order, and misses are simply the next archer’s turn (Wikipedia, n.d.). Safety rules require the shooting line and fall zone to be cordoned off and only the archer on the line when shooting (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men and women compete together in most guild shoots, with separate ladies’ kings in some guilds; youth categories exist in larger federations (Wikipedia, n.d.). There is no single world federation — the sport is governed nationally, e.g. by the Belgian archery federations, while World Archery covers archery generally.

## History

Popinjay shooting dates back to the medieval schutterijen of the Low Countries — guilds that combined military archery practice with festivals — and is documented from the 15th century; the famous c. 1430 image of Jacoba van Beieren receiving prizes after a popinjay shoot at Goes illustrates how old the tradition is (Rijksmuseum, n.d.). The guilds were once powerful civic institutions, and the "king’s shoot" was the high point of the town year (Wikipedia, n.d.). Today popinjay survives mainly in Belgium (Flanders), the Netherlands and among Dutch-descended communities overseas, contested at village fêtes and in organized leagues.
