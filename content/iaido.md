## Basic Information

Iaido is the Japanese martial art of the sword in which the practitioner draws the katana and cuts down an opponent in a single, controlled motion — the art of *saya no uchi* ("victory from within the scabbard") — and is practiced today chiefly through prescribed forms (*kata*) (All Japan Kendo Federation [AJKF], n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In Japan iaido is governed by the All Japan Kendo Federation (ZNKR, founded 1952), which organizes the All Japan Iaido Championship (held since 1966); internationally the International Kendo Federation (FIK, more than 60 member nations) and the European Kendo Federation govern it, and iaido is contested at the triennial World Kendo Championships (first held in 1970) (International Kendo Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The standardized competition forms are the 12 *Seitei iai* kata (created in 1969 with 7 kata, extended to 10 in 1981 and to 12 in 2000); in competition two athletes perform kata simultaneously on parallel lanes while three judges award the win by red/white flags (AJKF, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Iaido is open to women and men of all ages on equal terms — the women's side of the art has grown strongly, with dedicated women's sections at the All Japan championships — as the photograph of a female practitioner shows (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## Rules

Each of the 12 Seitei kata — Mae, Ushiro, Ukenagashi, Tsuka-ate, Kesagiri, Morote-tsuki, Sanpōgiri, Ganmen-ate, Soete-tsuki, Shihōgiri, Sōgiri and Nukiuchi — consists of four phases: *nukitsuke* (drawing and the initial cut), *kiritsuke* (the finishing cut), *chiburi* (removing the blood from the blade with a shaking motion) and *noto* (resheathing) (AJKF, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Practitioners train with an unsharpened practice sword (*iaitō*) or, at advanced levels, a real blade (*shinken*), wearing a *keikogi* and *hakama*; alongside the Seitei forms, the classical schools (*koryū*) such as Musō Jikiden Eishin-ryū, Musō Shinden-ryū and Hoki-ryū are still practiced (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Gradings run from *kyū* to *dan*; the All Japan Iaido Championship has been held since 1966 with dan-grade brackets. In shiai (competition), two competitors perform the same or different kata at the same time on two parallel embusen (performance lines), and three or five judges decide the winner by majority flag (red/white), with penalties for stepping off the line or major technical faults (AJKF, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Men and women compete in the same events under identical rules, and age categories (juniors, seniors, veterans) exist at national championships (AJKF, n.d.-a).

## History

The art of drawing and cutting in one motion developed among the samurai of Japan's Edo period; its central principle *saya no uchi de katsu* is attributed to the swordsman Yagyū Munenori (1656–1702) in his treatise *Heihō Kadenshō* (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Modern organization began with the Dai Nippon Butoku Kai's iaido section (ZNIR, 1948) and the founding of the All Japan Kendo Federation in 1952, which created the standardized *Seitei iai* in 1969 (7 kata), expanded it to 10 in 1981 and to the current 12 kata in 2000, and has run the All Japan Iaido Championship since 1966 (AJKF, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Internationally, iaido spread with kendo through the International Kendo Federation (founded 1970), and it has been part of the World Kendo Championships every three years since 1970 (International Kendo Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
