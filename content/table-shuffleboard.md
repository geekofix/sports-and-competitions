## Basic Information

Table shuffleboard (also known as shuffleboard or bocce-style table play) is the tabletop sport of sliding weighted pucks down a long, smooth wooden board into scoring zones, knocking opponents' weights off in the process. Played on cruise ships, in bars and purpose-built clubs, it is the indoor cousin of deck and floor shuffleboard with its own championships.

## Rules

Players slide weighted metal-and-plastic pucks (called weights or biscuits) down a narrow playing field of maple or polymer, aiming to stop within the numbered scoring zones "+E+" 1, 2 and 3 points "+E+" marked along the board (typically six to seven metres long), while legal shots may also knock opponents' weights out of scoring position or into the alley. Games are played one-on-one or in doubles, with players sliding four weights per round from alternating ends; a frame ends when all weights are shot and the closer side scores the total value of its weights lying farther down the board than any opponent weight, the standard game running to a set total (commonly 15 or 21 points). Wax or shuffleboard powder is applied to the board to control speed, and technique blends touch (lagging for the far 3-zone) with tactics (blocking, knock-outs and clearing the board).

## History

Table shuffleboard descends from the old European game of shove ha'penny and the deck shuffleboard of ocean liners and New York social clubs of the nineteenth century; the table form boomed in American bars and clubs across the early twentieth century, where heated boards, coin operation and table leagues built a factory-town pastime. The incorporated club circuit staged national championships from the mid-twentieth century in the United States, and the sport found strongholds in Florida, Canada and on cruise ships worldwide. Modern table shuffleboard is organized through national associations and local leagues with regular tournaments (the largest US gathering each year at the shuffleboard club mecca of St. Petersburg, Florida), and outdoor and indoor variants together give the discipline one of the largest senior-sport followings in North America.
