## Basic Information

Pelota purépecha, called Uárukua Ch'anakua ("a game with sticks") in the Purépecha language, is an indigenous Mexican stick-and-ball sport from the state of Michoacán, similar to the hockey family. In the variant called pasárutakua the ball is set on fire and played at night. The sport is one of the oldest living Mexican games, with roots going back some 3,500 years, and it has a league, several practicing communities and about 800 players across Mexico.

## Rules

Two teams of players contest a rectangular court, traditionally about 8 meters wide and 120 to 200 meters long. Each player carries a wooden stick shaped like an "L". The objective is to drive the ball over the opposing team's goal line to score a point, called a jatsíraku. To start the match, one player from each team meets at the center of the court and the two strike their sticks together three times before the ball is launched. Fouls include striking an opponent, stopping or covering the ball deliberately with the body, and touching the ball with hands or feet instead of the stick. Players may not raise the stick above the waist when striking or after the stroke, unless the ball is in the air. Match duration is set by the team captains before the start, either by a number of goals or a time limit.

## History

The game is rooted in the Purépecha tradition, which holds that it commemorates the birth of the sun and the balance of the universe—the flaming ball symbolizes the sun's journey across the sky. Figurines from the pre-Hispanic site of El Opeño suggest the game's origins over three millennia ago, and similar stick-and-ball games appear on the murals of the Palacio de Tepantitla at Teotihuacan. Under colonial-era communities the game survived, and the earliest descriptions tell of matches played between entire villages with no fixed limit on player numbers. Today the sport is practiced in communities across the four Purépecha regions of Michoacán, including villages around Lake Pátzcuaro such as Pátzcuaro, Uruapan, Paracho, Angahuan and Zacán, and modern leagues stage day and night matches, including games with the flaming ball dedicated to the god Kuricaveri at the Purépecha New Year on 1 February.
