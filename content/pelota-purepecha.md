## Basic Information

Pelota purépecha, called Úrukua Ch'anakua ("a game with sticks") in the Purépecha language, is an indigenous Mexican stick-and-ball sport from the state of Michoacán, similar to the hockey family and played with a heavy wooden ball and L-shaped wooden sticks (Secretaría de Cultura, 2022). In the variant called pasárukua the ball is set on fire and played at night. The sport is one of the oldest living Mexican games, with roots going back some 3,500 years, and it has a league, several practicing communities and about 800 players across Mexico (Pueblos Originarios, n.d.). Traditionally a men's game, pelota purépecha today also has women's teams in several communities of the Purépecha region, and mixed day-and-night matches are staged in the modern leagues (Comisión Nacional de Deportes, n.d.). The traditional playing field, the cancha, is pictured below (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Pelota purépecha is the indigenous stick-and-ball cousin of the [Basque Pelota](basque-pelota.html) family — [Valencian Pilota](valencian-pilota.html), [Jai Alai](jai-alai.html), [Mesoamerican Ballgame](mesoamerican-ballgame.html), [Ulama](ulama.html) — and the other wall games listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Two teams contest a rectangular earthen court, the cancha, traditionally about 8 meters wide and 120 to 200 meters long (Secretaría de Cultura, 2022). Each player carries a wooden stick shaped like an "L". The objective is to drive the heavy wooden ball over the opposing team's goal line to score a point, called a jatsírakua. To start the match, one player from each team meets at the center of the court and the two strike their sticks together three times before the ball is launched. Fouls include striking an opponent, stopping or covering the ball deliberately with the body, and touching the ball with hands or feet instead of the stick. Players may not raise the stick above the waist when striking or after the stroke, unless the ball is in the air. Match duration is set by the team captains before the start, either by a number of goals or a time limit (Pueblos Originarios, n.d.). Men's and women's matches follow the same rules; the night variant pasárukua, played with a flaming ball, adds the additional rule that the fire must be kept burning throughout the match (Comisión Nacional de Deportes, n.d.).

## History

The game is rooted in the Purépecha tradition, which holds that it commemorates the birth of the sun and the balance of the universe — the flaming ball symbolizes the sun's journey across the sky (Secretaría de Cultura, 2022). Figurines from the pre-Hispanic site of El Opeño suggest the game's origins over three millennia ago, and similar stick-and-ball games appear on the murals of the Palacio de Tepantitla at Teotihuacan; the game is thus a living relative of the great Mesoamerican ballgame, covered on this site as [Mesoamerican ballgame](mesoamerican-ballgame.html) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Under colonial-era communities the game survived, and the earliest descriptions tell of matches played between entire villages with no fixed limit on player numbers. Today the sport is practiced in communities across the four Purépecha regions of Michoacán, including villages around Lake Pátzcuaro such as Pátzcuaro, Uruapan, Paracho, Angahuan and Zacán, and modern leagues stage day and night matches, including games with the flaming ball dedicated to the god Kuricaveri at the Purépecha New Year on 1 February (Pueblos Originarios, n.d.). The Mexican government's CONADE documents the sport as part of the country's indigenous sporting heritage (Comisión Nacional de Deportes, n.d.).
