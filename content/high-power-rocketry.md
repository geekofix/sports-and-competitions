## Basic Information

High-power rocketry (HPR) is the branch of model rocketry using motors from H impulse (160 N·s) up to O (large research motors), in rockets that can weigh tens of kilograms and reach several kilometres of altitude (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is sanctioned by the Tripoli Rocketry Association and the NAR in the US, with Level 1–3 pilot certifications, and belongs to the rocketry family.

![Level 3 high-power rocket on the launch pad](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Level_3_high_power_rocket_at_launch_pad.jpg?width=420)

*Figure: National Association of Rocketry. (2014). Level 3 high-power rocket on the launch pad [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. CC BY-SA 3.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Level_3_high_power_rocket_at_launch_pad.jpg*

By the NFPA definition, a high-power rocket has a motor with more than 125 g of propellant, total impulse above 160.1 N·s, or average thrust above 80 N; such flights follow NFPA 1127 and need FAA waivers above certain altitudes and motor sizes (Wikipedia, n.d.). Certification: Level 1 (H–I), Level 2 (J–L), Level 3 (M–O) with witnessed flights and documentation (Tripoli, n.d.).

## Rules

Competition altitude records are ratified by Tripoli and NAR per motor class; at BALLS 33 in the Black Rock Desert on 21 September 2025, a two-stage amateur rocket reached 53,289 ft (16.2 km), breaking the previous Tripoli record of just under 45,000 ft (Rocketry Forum, 2025). The FAI ratifies spacemodelling altitude records (S1) for competition-spec models (FAI, n.d.). Launches take place at dedicated desert sites such as Black Rock (Nevada), Lucerne Dry Lake (California) and BALLS, with range safety officers and launch control (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men and women both fly and hold records.

## History

High-power rocketry grew out of the 1960s–70s amateur rocketry scene — the Reaction Research Society (founded 1943) and later Tripoli (founded 1985) gave amateurs a safe framework beyond NAR’s certified-motor-only rule (Wikipedia, n.d.). The big desert launches — ROCstock, LDRS, BALLS — became the heart of the hobby (Wikipedia, n.d.). Student and amateur teams have pushed the boundary: on 20 October 2024 the USC Rocket Propulsion Laboratory’s Aftershock II reached 143 km, exceeding the Kármán line and setting the amateur altitude record (Wikipedia, n.d.).
