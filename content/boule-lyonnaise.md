## Basic Information

Boule lyonnaise — also called sport-boules, "la lyonnaise" or "la longue" — is a French boules sport played on a long rectangular pitch called the cadre, where teams of one to four players take turns rolling heavy metal boules toward a small wooden target ball, the but. The team that places more boules closer to the but than the opponent's best boule scores one point per better-placed boule, and matches are played to 13 points. Unlike pétanque, where boules are thrown from a standing circle, the boule lyonnaise is rolled over distances of up to 27.5 m, and the game combines two techniques: pointing (pointer) to get closer to the but and shooting (tirer) to knock an opponent's boule away. The sport is governed internationally by the Fédération Internationale de Boules (FIB), which organizes world and continental championships in senior, under-23 and under-18 categories, and since 2021 it sits under the IOC-recognized umbrella body World Pétanque and Bowls Federation (World Pétanque and Bowls Federation [WPBF], n.d.); boule lyonnaise has been on the World Games programme (Fédération Internationale de Boules [FIB], n.d.-a). Internationally the game is dominated by France and Italy, and the 2026 FIB calendar included the European women's championship at Vigone, Italy (FIB, n.d.-b) (Fédération Internationale de Boules, n.d.-a) (Fédération Internationale de Boules, n.d.-b). Within the wider boules-and-bowls family on this site it sits next to [Pétanque](petanque.html), [Bocce](bocce.html), [Bocce Punto](bocce-punto.html), [Bocce Raffa](bocce-raffa.html), [Bocce Volo](bocce-volo.html), [Lawn Bowls](lawn-bowls.html), [Indoor Bowls](indoor-bowls.html), [Crown Green Bowls](crown-green-bowls.html), [Irish Road Bowling](irish-road-bowling.html), [Varpa](varpa.html), [Ice Stock Sport](ice-stock-sport.html), [Shuffleboard](shuffleboard.html), [Table Shuffleboard](table-shuffleboard.html) and Paralympic [Boccia](boccia.html).

On the women's side, women compete in all formats and championships with slightly lighter boules (minimum 800 g, diameter from 88 mm upwards); the 2026 European women's championship in Vigone, Italy, underlined the growing international women's programme, and women's and mixed events are standard at club level in France and Switzerland (FIB, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Under the FIB Règlement Technique International, the cadre is a rectangle 27.50 m long (tolerance 5 cm) and 2.5 to 4 m wide, divided into seven zones; the but is thrown from the end zone and must come to rest between 12.5 and 17.5 m from the throwing line, at least 50 cm from the side and back lines (FIB, n.d.-b). Boules are hollow metal spheres of 90 to 110 mm diameter and 900 to 1,200 g; women and players aged 14 and under may use lighter boules of at least 800 g and 88 mm diameter. The but is a plain wooden ball, usually boxwood, 35 to 37 mm in diameter (Ministère de la Culture, n.d.).

Each frame (mène), one team throws the but, then the players alternate rolling ("pointing") their boules from the end zone, always with a foot behind the throwing line; a team keeps playing while the opponent holds the advantage — that is, while its own closest boule is farther from the but than the opponent's closest boule. When a player chooses to shoot instead, they must announce the target (a boule or the but) before the run-up; the thrown boule must land within 50 cm of the announced target, otherwise the shot and all its effects are annulled and displaced objects are restored to their marked places. At the end of the frame, the team holding the advantage scores one point for every boule closer to the but than the opponent's closest boule; a match is played to 13 points (FIB, n.d.-b).

Formats and classifications: simple (one against one), doublette (two against two), triplette (three against three) and quadrette (four against four), with the roles of pointeur (pointer), milieu and tireur (shooter) and a set number of boules per player depending on the format. Beside the traditional game, "boule sportive" includes precision events — tir de précision and point sur cible at fixed distances such as 8 m and 12.50 m with ten boules per player — as well as combined events, with separate men's and women's and youth (under-14, under-18, under-23) categories at world and European championships (FIB, n.d.-a). Fouls include stepping over the throwing line, boules or the but leaving the cadre (lost objects), irregular shot lines and failure to announce the target; each team marks its boules with two strokes at a 90° angle.

## History

The game of boules took root in the Lyon region in the 18th century, where it was known as "jeu de grosses boules" or "la longue"; it was elevated to a sport in 1850 with the founding of the first official society, the Clos Jouve in Lyon, and the first properly regulated concours was held in Lyon on June 3–5, 1894, on 63 terrains with 1,200 players (Ministère de la Culture, n.d.). In the 20th century the French federation grew rapidly, and in 1981 the game was officially renamed "sport-boules"; since 2012 the practice is listed in France's inventory of intangible cultural heritage (Ministère de la Culture, n.d.). Early boules were made of stone, then of wood, horn or boxwood, later iron-studded, and from 1923 hollow bronze-aluminium; the famous Intégrale factory in Lyon produced competition boules.

Internationally, the Fédération Internationale de Boules was founded in 1946 and has organized world championships since the mid-20th century; the older Confédération Mondiale des Sports de Boules (CMSB, founded in Monaco in December 1985) was dissolved in 2020 and restructured in 2021 into the World Pétanque and Bowls Federation, which is recognized by the International Olympic Committee and represents boule lyonnaise at the World Games (WPBF, n.d.).
