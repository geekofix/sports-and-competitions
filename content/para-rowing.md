## Basic Information

Para rowing (adaptive rowing) is rowing for athletes with physical, visual or intellectual impairments, governed by World Rowing, which drew up the first guidelines in 2001; the sport raced at the 2002 World Rowing Championships in Seville and made its Paralympic debut at Beijing 2008 with 90 athletes in four events (World Rowing, n.d.-c; World Rowing, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Classes are PR1 (arms and shoulders only; fixed seat, chest strap, stabilising pontoons on the single scull), PR2 (trunk and arms; fixed seat) and PR3 (legs, trunk and arms; sliding seat, also open to visual and intellectual impairments), with nine boat events at the World Championships (World Rowing, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Races are 2,000 m (the distance was 1,000 m until 2016) and Paris 2024 added the PR3 mixed double sculls as a fifth event, making LA28 the first Games with five gender-balanced para rowing events (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Olympics, n.d.). At the 2026 World Rowing Championships on the Bosbaan in Amsterdam (24–30 August 2026) the women's PR1 single sculls went to Anna Sheremet of Ukraine in 9:48.91 ahead of Laurence Vandevyver of Belgium and Claire Ghiringhelli of Switzerland, and Benjamin Pritchard of Great Britain defended the men's PR1 title (2026, September 1; World Rowing, n.d.-a).

Leading women today include Moran Samuel of Israel, the Paris 2024 PR1 W1x gold medallist ahead of Birgit Skarstein of Norway and Nathalie Benoit of France, and Lauren Rowles of Great Britain, the only rower with three Paralympic golds, all in the mixed double sculls; the picture shows the women's single sculls medal ceremony at London 2012 (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). Among the men, Roman Polianskyi of Ukraine is the most successful male rower (gold 2016 and 2021, silver 2024) and Erik Horrie of Australia the most decorated (four medals across four Games) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

World Rowing's Racing Rules apply: races over 2,000 m in marked lanes with aligned starts and video finish; in mixed boats at least half the crew must be of each gender, and in the PR3 mixed coxed four the coxswain may be of either gender and able-bodied (World Rowing, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Classification places athletes in PR1, PR2 or PR3; PR1 single sculls must carry stabilising pontoons, PR1/PR2 row from fixed seats with the PR1 rower strapped at chest level, and PR3 uses standard sliding seats; men and women race separately in the single sculls and together in the mixed double sculls and coxed four (World Rowing, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## History

Rowing for people with disabilities began in 1913 when headmaster George Clifford Brown founded a club at Worcester College for the Blind in England; the first competitive races followed in 1914, and in October 1945 blinded US servicemen raced at the Navy Day Regatta in Philadelphia, sparking international interest (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Adaptive events joined the World Rowing Championships in 2002 (classes LTA, TA and AS, renamed PR3, PR2, PR1 in 2017) and the Paralympics in 2008; the pictured LTA mixed coxed four final is from the 2010 World Rowing Championships (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
