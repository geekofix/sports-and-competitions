## Basic Information

Duck herding is a dog sport in which a trained herding dog moves a small group of ducks (usually three to five) through a fenced arena course with gates, pens and obstacles, directed by whistle and voice commands from the handler. It is run as part of herding trials by kennel clubs — notably the American Kennel Club (AKC), which explicitly allows ducks as livestock in its Herding Test and Trial program — and by dedicated duck-herding clubs that hold standalone competitions.

## Rules

In AKC herding trials with ducks, the dog must gather the ducks at the handler's direction and drive them through a course (Course A, B or D) within a time limit (typically 10 minutes for ducks), passing through gates, into pens and around obstacles, while the judge scores control, pace and the dog's ability to keep the group together. Ducks work well in groups of five and regroup easily if split. In standalone duck-herding competitions (common in the UK and US at country shows), the format is similar: a small flock of ducks is released in a fenced field, and the handler directs the dog to move them through a marked course or into a pen, judged on time and points deducted for lost control or missed obstacles. Points are lost if ducks escape the arena or scatter beyond recovery within the time limit.

## History

Herding ducks with dogs goes back centuries on farms, where ducks were driven to and from water and pens. Competitive duck herding developed alongside sheepdog trials in the 20th century, and ducks became a popular alternative to sheep because they are cheaper, easier to transport and slow the action down, making them ideal for training young dogs. The AKC formally recognized ducks as trial livestock, and organizations such as the American Herding Breed Association and UK clubs run duck-herding classes. Standalone duck-herding contests are a staple of agricultural shows and dog festivals, with world-record-style challenges televised occasionally.
