## Basic Information

Headis (also called Kopfballtischtennis, "heading table tennis") is a fun sport invented in 2006 by the sport student René Wegner in Kaiserslautern, Germany — the football pitch was occupied, so play moved to a free table-tennis table and the ball was played with the head only; the name combines "head" and "tennis" (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It is played on a regular table-tennis table with a metal net using a special oversized ball of roughly 100 g and 18 cm diameter (circumference about 50 cm); only the head may touch the ball, while the table may be touched with any body part (HEADIS, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Since 2006 an annual World Championship has been held, since 2008 ten to twelve World Cup tournaments per year feed the world ranking (the last 15 tournaments count), the European Championship has been held since 2015 in mixed-gender competition, and the Headis Masters since 2011 invites the 18 best men and 8 best women plus wildcards; by 2016 around 100,000 people played headis and 18 German universities offered it (HEADIS, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). As of the latest championships, the leading women include the multiple women's world champion Margarita Marmol Fernandez, known by her player name "Klausi", while the most successful men's player is Cornelius Döll ("Headsinfarkt"), world champion in 2013, 2015, 2016 and 2018, with Sebastian Headdel winning in 2017 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

The official rules are published by HEADIS: play takes place on a standard table-tennis table with the net replaced by a metal net, and only the head may play the ball — contact with the ball by any other body part loses the point — while the table itself may be touched with any body part (HEADIS, n.d.-a). The special headis ball weighs around 100 g and measures about 18 cm in diameter; volleys are allowed, and after each ball the player must touch the floor with any body part before heading the ball again (HEADIS, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The service must bounce once on the server's own side and then on the opponent's side; if the served ball touches the net or the table edge it is a let and is replayed (HEADIS, n.d.-a). Matches are played as best of three sets to 11 points, and a set must be won by two points (HEADIS, n.d.-b). Tournaments are scored for the world ranking — the last 15 tournaments count — and the World Championship is the only five-star event, run over two days including qualification, with the leading ranked players qualifying; the European Championship (since 2015) and the Headis Masters (since 2011) are additional international events (HEADIS, n.d.-b). Women and men compete together in mixed tournaments under identical rules, with separate women's and men's titles at the World Championship and the Masters (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

René Wegner invented headis in 2006 in the washroom area of a Freibad (outdoor pool) in Kaiserslautern, where the football pitch was occupied but the table-tennis table was free, and play with the head over the net began; the first institution to offer headis courses was the University of the Saarland in 2008 within its university sports programme, and the sport spread through the German student scene — the "adh-Trophy Headis" university championship was held in Kaiserslautern in 2010 and 2011 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first World Cup tournaments were held in 2008, the first World Championship followed in 2006-era Kaiserslautern scene and has run annually since; by 2016 eleven World Cup events with more than 1,000 participants were held, around 100,000 people played headis, and 18 German universities offered it, with clubs such as 1. FC Kaiserslautern and SV Darmstadt 98 also offering training; the sport gained media attention through TV appearances such as TV total and viral internet videos (HEADIS, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). International partners exist in Switzerland, Belgium, the Netherlands, Czechia, the Dominican Republic, China, Japan and Australia (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
