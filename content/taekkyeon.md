## Basic Information

Taekkyeon (also spelled taekkyon) is Korea's traditional martial art and combat sport, characterized by fluid, dance-like movement: the signature pumbalbgi ("stepping on triangles") footwork, sweeping kicks and leg trips that unbalance or throw the opponent (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It is distinct from taekwondo — a frequent misconception — and was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity on 28 November 2011, the first martial art ever to receive that honor, after South Korea designated it Important Intangible Cultural Property No. 76 on 1 June 1983 (UNESCO, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Domestically the art is organized by the Korea Taekkyeon Association (Korea Taekkyon Federation, founded 1991), the only taekkyeon body recognized by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism and a member of the Korean Sport & Olympic Committee since 2007; internationally the World Taekkyeon Federation (founded by Ko Yong-woo, incorporated 2019) spreads the art, and taekkyeon has been an official event at Korea's National Sports Festival since 2022 and featured at the World Martial Arts Masterships in 2016 and 2019 (Korea Taekkyeon Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Women compete in the same official tournaments — national championships and festival events are held in weight classes for both men's and women's divisions (Korea Taekkyeon Association, n.d.).

 Within the combat sports and martial arts family on this site, its closest relatives are [Taekwondo](taekwondo.html), [Ssireum](ssireum.html), [Tang Soo Do](tang-soo-do.html), [Boxing](boxing.html), [Bare Knuckle Boxing](bare-knuckle-boxing.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Competitive taekkyeon (kyonggi) is fought on a marked court under standardized rules issued by the Korea Taekkyeon Association: matches are contested in weight classes with separate men's and women's divisions, and points are scored for legal kicks to the opponent's body or head and for throws, sweeps and inner and outer leg trips that knock the opponent down or out of the ring (Korea Taekkyeon Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In keeping with the art's principle of dominance without unnecessary damage, grabbing, joint attacks and deliberately brutal techniques are prohibited and penalized, while the fluid pumbalbgi stepping remains the technical hallmark of legal movement (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Competition formats range from festival duels to single-elimination brackets at the annual National Taekkyon Championship, with age and weight classifications and referee guidelines standardized in multilingual form by the KTF (Korea Taekkyeon Association, n.d.).

## History

Taekkyeon's roots reach back more than 2,000 years — a Goguryeo tomb mural at Samsil depicts a taekkyeon-like fight, and the first written mention of "tak'kyon" appears in Yi Song-ji's Chaemulbo around 1790; during the Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) the art was practiced across all social classes and reportedly by kings, one of whom banned public matches over the gambling that surrounded them (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The Japanese colonial authorities banned taekkyeon in the early 1930s under threat of the death penalty, and by the 1950s only one master, Song Deok-gi (19 January 1893 – 23 July 1987), was left to transmit the art; he trained under Im Ho from age 12, preserved taekkyeon through the occupation and the Korean War, and gave the first public demonstration in decades for President Syngman Rhee in 1958 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Together with Shin Han-seung (1928–1987), who systematized a modern curriculum, Song Deok-gi secured the 1983 cultural-property designation; the Korean Traditional Taekkyeon Preservation Society was founded in 1973, the Korean Traditional Taekgyeon Association in Chungju in 1977, the Korea Taekkyeon Association in 1991, and after Song Deok-gi's death his senior student Ko Yong-woo founded the World Taekkyeon Federation in 2019 — the road that led to the UNESCO inscription in 2011 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; UNESCO, n.d.).
