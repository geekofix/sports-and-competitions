## Basic Information

Freestyle swimming is the fastest and most contested stroke of competitive pool swimming: over distances from 50 m to 1500 m the swimmer may use any style — in practice the front crawl — which is why the freestyle events are the blue-riband races of the Olympic Games and the World Aquatics Championships (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; World Aquatics, n.d.-c). The discipline is governed worldwide by World Aquatics, which ratifies the world records under Swimming Rule SW12 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; World Aquatics, n.d.-e). At the end of the 2026 season the long-course world records stand at 20.88 s over 50 m for Cameron McEvoy of Australia (set at the 2026 China Open) and 23.19 s for Kate Douglass of the USA; 46.40 s over 100 m for Pan Zhanle of China (Paris 2024) and 51.68 s for Marrit Steenbergen of the Netherlands (2026); 1:42.00 over 200 m for Paul Biedermann of Germany (2009) and 1:52.85 for Mollie O'Callaghan of Australia (2023); and 3:40.07 over 400 m for Biedermann (2009), while the 800 m and 1500 m records belong to Zhang Lin of China (7:32.12, 2009) and Sun Yang (14:31.02, 2012) on the men's side and to Katie Ledecky of the USA (8:04.79, 2016 and 15:20.48, 2016) on the women's side (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Seventeen global swimming standards were set in 2026 alone, thirteen of them in long course (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Among the athletes defining the 2026 season, the women lead: Kate Douglass of the USA holds eleven world records including the 50 m freestyle mark of 23.19 s set in 2026, Marrit Steenbergen of the Netherlands (pictured) set the 100 m freestyle world record of 51.68 s in 2026, Summer McIntosh of Canada holds the 400 m freestyle record (3:54.18, reclaimed at the 2025 World Championships in Singapore), Lani Pallister of Australia headed the 2026 800 m freestyle lists as the first swimmer other than Katie Ledecky to top them since 2011, and Ledecky herself still holds the 800 m and 1500 m world records (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). On the men's side, Pan Zhanle's 46.40 s over 100 m from Paris 2024 remains the world record, with David Popovici of Romania (46.56) leading the 2026 100 m lists, Cameron McEvoy holding the 50 m record, and the 2026 200 m and 400 m leaders Zhang Zhanshuo of China (1:43.49) and Sam Short of Australia (3:40.52) chasing Biedermann's 2009 marks (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Freestyle races are held in certified 50 m long-course (LCM) pools at the Olympic Games and World Championships or 25 m short-course (SCM) pools at the World Swimming Championships (25 m); championship pools have eight lanes (ten at the Olympics and World Championships) each 2.5 m wide, with starting blocks and electronic touchpads, and the water temperature must be between 25 and 28 °C (World Aquatics, n.d.-b; World Aquatics, n.d.-d). In a freestyle event the swimmer may use any stroke — but in the individual medley and the medley relay the freestyle quarter or leg must be swum in a style different from backstroke, breaststroke and butterfly (World Aquatics, n.d.-d).

All freestyle starts are crouch starts from the blocks; a false start disqualifies the athlete under the single-false-start rule in force since 2010. At each turn and at the finish the swimmer must touch the wall with any part of the body; after the start and after each turn the swimmer may remain underwater for at most 15 m, and pulling on the lane rope is prohibited (World Aquatics, n.d.-d). Races are swum in heats, semifinals and finals — with heats and finals only over 800 m and longer and in the relays — and times are recorded to 1/100 s by automatic timing equipment with a backup to 1/1000 s; a world record additionally requires ratification paperwork under Rule SW12 (World Aquatics, n.d.-d; World Aquatics, n.d.-e). Men and women contest identical events and distances, the mixed 4×100 m freestyle relay consists of two men and two women in any order, junior world championships run separate age groups, and para swimming offers the same freestyle events in the S-class classification system (World Aquatics, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

Competitive swimming emerged in 19th-century Britain, where the National Swimming Society organized races from the 1830s, and swimming has been on the Olympic programme since Athens 1896 for men and Stockholm 1912 for women; the freestyle events, swum with the front crawl that the Australian Richmond brothers and later Duke Kahanamoku popularized from Hawaii, have produced the sport's biggest icons (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Mark Spitz of the USA, pictured, won seven gold medals at Munich 1972, including the 100 m freestyle in a world record; his compatriot Johnny Weissmuller had won the 100 m freestyle at Paris 1924 and Amsterdam 1928 as the first man under one minute (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). On the women's side Dawn Fraser of Australia, pictured, won the 100 m freestyle at three consecutive Olympics (Melbourne 1956, Rome 1960, Tokyo 1964) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Other freestyle legends include Alexander Popov of Russia (50 m and 100 m gold at Barcelona 1992 and Atlanta 1996), Matt Biondi of the USA (five golds at Seoul 1988), Ian Thorpe of Australia, who set the 400 m freestyle world record of 3:40.08 at Sydney 2000, and Michael Phelps, whose 200 m freestyle relay exploits made him the most decorated Olympian of all time (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
