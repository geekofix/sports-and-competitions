## Basic Information

Luge is a winter sliding sport in which one or two athletes ride a small sled supine (feet first) down an ice track, steering with subtle shifts of the shoulders and calves at speeds above 140 km/h (International Luge Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It has been an Olympic sport since the first Winter Games at Innsbruck 1964 and is governed by the International Luge Federation (FIL), founded in 1957 (International Luge Federation, n.d.). At the Milano Cortina 2026 Winter Olympics, held at the Cortina Sliding Centre from 7 to 12 February 2026, five medal events were contested — including the historic Olympic debut of women's doubles, 62 years after the men's doubles was added (International Luge Federation, n.d.-a). Germany's Julia Taubitz won the women's singles gold in 3:30.625 including a track record, ahead of Elīna Ieva Bota of Latvia (silver) and Ashley Farquharson of the United States (bronze, the first US women's luge medal since 2002); Germany's Max Langenhan won the men's singles, Italy took both doubles titles, and Germany won the team relay in a track record 3:41.627, with the doubles pair Tobias Wendl and Tobias Arlt becoming the most decorated Olympic lugers ever with seven gold and one bronze medal (International Luge Federation, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Singles races are run over two heats (four at the Olympics) with the combined time deciding the medals; doubles race a single run; the team relay combines one women's singles, one men's singles and one doubles run per nation (International Luge Federation, n.d.-a). Athletes start seated, rock on two handles to build momentum, then propel with spiked gloves on the ice and steer by flexing the sled's runners with their calves and shifting shoulder pressure (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Timing is electronic from the release of the start handles to the finish light, recorded to the thousandth of a second (International Luge Federation, n.d.-a). Tracks are 1,000–1,400 m of artificially refrigerated ice with up to 20 banked curves and must be FIL-homologated; false starts, exceeding weight limits for sled plus athlete, and equipment violations bring penalties or disqualification (International Luge Federation, n.d.-a). Athletes wear aerodynamic one-piece suits, full-face helmets and special start shoes (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men's and women's singles use identical tracks, sleds and timing; doubles (now also a women's event since 2026) ride a longer, heavier sled with the front athlete steering with the feet and the rear with the shoulders (International Luge Federation, n.d.-a).

## History

Competitive luge originated in the Swiss health resorts of Davos and St. Moritz in the mid-to-late 19th century, promoted by hotelier Caspar Badrutt, who organized the first sled races on the Cresta Run in St. Moritz from 1884 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Luge was governed from 1935 within the International Bobsleigh and Tobogganing Federation (FIBT) until the FIL was founded as its own federation in 1957; the first world championships were held in 1955 in Oslo, and luge debuted at the 1964 Innsbruck Olympics (International Luge Federation, n.d.-b). The most decorated singles luger is Germany's Georg Hackl (born 9 September 1966 in Berchtesgaden), who won Olympic gold in 1992, 1994 and 1998 — the only man to win three consecutive Olympic singles golds — plus silvers in 1988 and 2002, making him the first Winter Olympian to win medals in the same individual event at five consecutive Games (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). Italy's Armin Zöggeler matched the longevity with six consecutive Olympic medals from 1994 to 2014, while Germany's Natalie Geisenberger (Olympic champion 2010 and 2014) and Felix Loch (2010, 2014) defined the women's and men's eras before the 2020s, and the Canadian pair Caitlin Nash and Natalie Corless became the first all-female team in a senior World Cup doubles race in 2019 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
