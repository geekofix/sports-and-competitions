## Basic Information

Pole bending is a timed rodeo and gymkhana event in which a horse and rider weave at speed through a straight line of six poles, running a serpentine pattern against the clock (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is one of the two most common pattern races at youth rodeos, gymkhanas and breed shows, alongside [barrel racing](barrel-racing.html), and is a standard event of the National High School Rodeo Association and of American Quarter Horse Association competition. It is not a Professional Rodeo Cowboys Association event, so its biggest stages are youth, college and breed championships (Equine Info Exchange, n.d.). The event is open to girls and boys alike — every youth rodeo runs separate girls' and boys' divisions with identical patterns and penalties — and women compete at the highest college and breed levels (Nebraska 4-H, n.d.). ## Rules

The standard pattern uses six poles set in a straight line 21 feet apart, with the first pole 21 feet from the start-and-finish line; the poles are about six feet high, with a base no more than 14 inches in diameter so that they fall when struck (Nebraska 4-H, n.d.). The rider crosses the starting line, rides to the far end of the line, turns around the sixth pole, weaves back through the poles, turns around the first pole, weaves again to the far end, turns around the sixth pole once more and races back across the finish line. Knocking over a pole carries a five-second penalty, and breaking the pattern results in disqualification or a no-time. Electronic timers record the run to the hundredth of a second; an off-the-line start is a false start. Competitions typically allow a running start (Iowa State University Extension, n.d.). Girls' and boys' divisions, as well as women's and men's college divisions, use the identical pattern, penalties and timing (Nebraska 4-H, n.d.).

## History

Pole bending grew out of the timed "games" or playday events of American riding clubs and 4-H programs, where it and barrel racing served as youth events that rewarded quick, agile horses rather than brute speed (Wikipedia, n.d.). It became institutionalized through the National High School Rodeo Association, whose rulebook fixes the 21-foot pattern used across the sport, and through breed associations: American Quarter Horses dominate the event nationally, with American Paint Horses and Appaloosas also strong, and the AQHA stages pole bending at its world-level youth shows (Equine Info Exchange, n.d.). Successful pole horses combine a short, quick stride with frequent lead changes every 21 feet; many trainers, following the practice of AQHA professionals, school young speed-event horses on poles before barrels because the weaving teaches rate and control. The event remains a fixture of high-school and college rodeo, 4-H shows and gymkhanas across North America, with girls' divisions as prominent as boys' (Equine Info Exchange, n.d.).
