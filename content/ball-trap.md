## Basic Information

Ball trap — also known as automatic ball trap (ABT) — is a clay discipline in which a single trap launches a target that can move like a driven bird in any direction, and the shooter, positioned behind the trap line, takes 25 targets per round in groups of five (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is part of the clay-target family alongside trap, skeet, five-stand and compak.

![Ball-trap laser training device at a clay range](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Ball-trap_laser_bl.jpg?width=480)

*Figure: F.Lamiot. (2007-01-07 00:00:19). Ball-trap laser training device at a clay range [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. CC BY-SA 3.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Ball-trap_laser_bl.jpg*

ABT uses the Laporte-style trap mechanism: the target is thrown on the shooter’s call with random angles, so unlike Olympic trap (where five traps fire known angles) every target is a surprise (Wikipedia, n.d.). Internationally, ABT is contested under ISSF/FITASC rules at world-level events, and the ISSF includes it in its rulebook as "automatic ball trap" (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## Rules

A round is 25 targets (five squads of five); scoring is 1 point per broken target (Wikipedia, n.d.). Gun rules as in other clay disciplines: 12 gauge, shot limits, two shells loaded, mounted before the call (FITASC, n.d.). Men and women compete together with Lady categories at championships.

## History

Ball trap developed from the single-trap "ball trap" machines invented by the Laporte company in the early 20th century — the classic wooden trap houses visible in the Plaue photo are the descendants (Wikipedia, n.d.). The discipline was standardized by the ISSF in the mid-20th century and remains popular in Europe, especially Germany and the Benelux countries, where historic Wurftaubenschießstände (clay pigeon ranges) still operate (Wikipedia, n.d.).
