## Basic Information

Russian pyramid, also known as Russian billiards or simply pyramid, is a form of pocket billiards played on a large table with unusually tight pockets and larger, heavier balls than pool or snooker — widely considered the most difficult cue sport to pocket balls. It is the dominant cue sport in Russia and across much of the former Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, played both casually and at world-championship level, and is a direct descendant of the 19th-century European game of pyramid pool.

## Rules

The game is played on a 12-by-6-foot table with sixteen balls: fifteen numbered white object balls racked in a pyramid and a coloured cue ball (usually red or yellow). Official tournament balls are 67 mm and about 255 g — much larger and heavier than pool balls — and the pockets are only 3 mm wider than the ball at the corners (12–13 mm at the sides), demanding near-perfect pocketing accuracy. Cues are thicker and heavier than pool cues, with tips up to 15 mm, and jump and massé shots are prohibited as fouls. The main rule variants are Free pyramid (any ball may serve as the cue ball, with the struck ball caromed into a pocket), Dynamic (Siberian/Nevsky) pyramid, where the cue ball itself may be potted via a carom, Combined (Moscow) pyramid with restrictions after a potted cue ball, and Classical pyramid, a fifteen-ball points game to 71 of 130 possible points. In most variants the first player to pocket eight object balls wins the frame, shots need not be called, and fouls see balls re-spotted behind the baulk line.

## History

Pyramid games trace back to the Continental European pyramid pool recorded in Vienna in 1795; the modern Russian variant evolved in the Russian Empire in the 19th century, taking on its distinctive large balls and tight pockets in the billiard rooms of St Petersburg and Moscow, and cementing its place in Russian culture — where a competitive local scene thrived through the Soviet era as the standard "Russian billiards" game of the saloon and the club. The game professionalised in the post-Soviet decades: World Pyramid Championships recognised by the World Pool-Billiard Association have been held since 2000 — the 1999 and 2000 world titles were won by players such as Evgeny Stalev, who later also won the 2003 Asian championship — while international tours, Euro-Asia pyramid circuits and Russian national championships continue to this day, and pyramid tables are now a fixture of Russian diaspora billiard halls worldwide. The game retains its reputation as the sternest test of cue accuracy: even top pool and snooker professionals struggle with its enormous balls and razor-thin pockets.
