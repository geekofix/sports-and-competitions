## Basic Information

Wine slinging is a comic harvest-festival contest in which participants project wine as far as possible — most famously by spitting a mouthful from behind a marked line, or by slinging a measured quantity from a cup or container at a target — in the manner of the better-known cherry-pit and watermelon-seed spitting contests (Wikipedia, n.d.). It belongs to the novelty games of European wine festivals, alongside grape stomping and barrel rolling, and is played by men and women alike in the same heats at village fêtes (German Wine Institute, n.d.).

There is no international federation and no unified rulebook: every festival committee sets its own rules, distances, attempt counts and prize lists, and results are a matter of local pride rather than a governed international sport (Wikipedia, n.d.). The contest appears at harvest celebrations across the wine regions of Germany, Italy, France and Spain — the German Palatinate's great wine festivals, such as the Dürkheimer Wurstmarkt in Bad Dürkheim, the world's largest wine festival, being classic venues (German Wine Institute, n.d.).

## Rules

Contests take place on a marked lane: participants take the wine provided by the organisers, step behind the spit-line, and spit or spray it as far as possible along the measured lane, with the distance from the line to where the wine lands deciding the winner over a fixed number of attempts (Wikipedia, n.d.). Alternative formats replace spitting with slinging a measured quantity of wine from a container in an underarm throw at a target — longest or closest wins — with penalties for stepping over the line or fouling the lane (Wikipedia, n.d.). Local rules fix the number of tries, wine allowances, age limits and prize lists; there are no standardised classes, equipment or protective gear beyond what each festival mandates, and no world ranking exists (German Wine Institute, n.d.).

## History

Wine slinging is one of numerous harvest-time games that European wine villages have celebrated at rural festivals for centuries, tied to the vineyard cycle and the September–October vintage (Wikipedia, n.d.). Modern wine festivals in Germany, Italy, France and Spain carry comic events between tastings — grape stomping, wine slinging, barrel rolling — with each village keeping its own records and champions (German Wine Institute, n.d.). Memorable contests of this kind have been run from the great Palatinate festivals in Germany to local fairs from Austria to Australia, kept alive entirely by festival committees (Wikipedia, n.d.).
