## Basic Information

Hang gliding is the sport of flying foot-launched, unpowered flexible or rigid wings — FAI Class O aircraft — governed internationally by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI, founded 1905) and its hang gliding and paragliding commission CIVL (Commission Internationale de Vol Libre) (Fédération Aéronautique Internationale [FAI], n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Competitions are flown as cross-country tasks over set turn points, scored under the GAP system (distance, leading and arrival points — with no arrival points in Class 2 in the 2026 specification), in three classes: Class 1 (flexible wings), Class 2 and Class 5 (rigid wings) (FAI, n.d.-a). The 22nd FAI European Hang Gliding Class 1 Championships and the 11th FAI World Hang Gliding Class 5 Championships were held together at Gemona del Friuli, Italy, from 31 May to 13 June 2026, with up to 125 pilots launching from Cuarnan and Valinis/Meduno; Alex Ploner of Italy won the European title for the fourth time (2012, 2018, 2022, 2026), with Christian Ciech second, while in the Class 5 worlds Markus Baisch of Germany won gold ahead of Andreas Sand and his father Karl Baisch (CIVL, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The 24th FAI Class 1 World Championships were held in Ager, Spain, in July 2025 (FAI, n.d.-b). Championships are open to all genders with combined rankings — there is no separate women's class at the World Championships, and women compete directly against men in the same classes (FAI, n.d.-a) (Fédération Aéronautique Internationale, n.d.-a) (Fédération Aéronautique Internationale, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Hang gliding competitions are regulated by the FAI Sporting Code, Section 7, and the CIVL competition rules; pilots launch on foot from a slope, fly a task of several turn points around a goal, and are scored by the GAP formula — points for distance flown, for leading the task and for arrival speed, with the 2026 specification awarding no arrival points in Class 2 (FAI, n.d.-a). Each championship day can set tasks, with a maximum number of tasks and a final goal time; pilots must reach the goal to score arrival points, and the overall ranking decides the champion (FAI, n.d.-a; CIVL, n.d.). Safety rules require a certified wing, reserve parachute, helmet and harness checks, and a mandatory safety briefing before the first task (FAI, n.d.-b). Classes: Class 1 flexible hang gliders (topless or kingposted), Class 2 rigid wings with control by weight shift, and Class 5 rigid competition wings; paragliding is a separate FAI discipline (see [Paragliding](paragliding.html) on this site) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Gender and age differences: the FAI championships are open — women and men fly in the same classes and rankings; junior categories (under 25) exist at some events (FAI, n.d.-a).

## History

The pioneer of controlled hang gliding was Otto Lilienthal (1848–1896), who from 1891 made more than 2,000 flights in several glider designs from an artificial hill near Berlin and died in a crash on 9 August 1896 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The modern sport began with the Rogallo flexible wing (invented by Francis Rogallo and Gertrude Rogallo in the 1940s–50s): Australian John Dickenson built and flew the first modern hang glider in 1963 and received the FAI Gold Medal in 2012, and commercial production started in 1969 when Bill Bennett flew a Rogallo wing across the US and Bill Moyes sold the first commercial gliders (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The FAI was founded in 1905, the CIVL organizes the World and European Championships, and the first FAI Class 1 World Championship was held in Kössen, Austria, in 1973 (FAI, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
