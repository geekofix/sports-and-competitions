## Basic Information

Lethwei is the traditional bare-knuckle boxing of Myanmar (Burma), often called "the art of nine limbs" because, like Muay Thai, it uses fists, elbows, knees and feet — but historically also the head, and without gloves, only hand wraps. Bouts are fought over five three-minute rounds in a ring, and the sport has been practiced for centuries in Myanmar, where it was part of royal court entertainment and military training. Today it is broadcast globally through promotions such as ONE Championship and the World Lethwei Championship (WLC).

## Rules

A professional lethwei bout consists of five rounds of three minutes with one-minute rests. Fighters wear shorts and hand wraps but no gloves, and strikes with fists, elbows, knees, shins and feet are allowed; in traditional rules the headbutt was also permitted. A fighter wins by knockout or technical knockout, or by judges' decision at the end of the fifth round, which is scored as a whole rather than round by round. A knockdown does not automatically end the bout in the traditional ruleset — the referee may allow the fight to continue — and a fighter who cannot continue is stopped as TKO. Weight classes and medical checks apply at professional events.

## History

Lethwei has been practiced in Myanmar since at least the Konbaung dynasty (18th-19th centuries), where it was fought at pagoda festivals and for the entertainment of kings; it was suppressed during British colonial rule but revived in the 20th century. The World Lethwei Championship (WLC) was founded in 2001 and staged regular events in Myanmar, and since 2016 ONE Championship has brought lethwei to a global audience with televised events. Canadian fighter Dave Leduc became the most famous international lethwei practitioner, winning multiple world titles. Lethwei remains deeply tied to Burmese culture, with pre-fight rituals such as the wai khru-style dance and music played on traditional instruments.
