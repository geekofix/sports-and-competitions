## Basic Information

Kenjutsu is the umbrella term for the pre-modern Japanese sword schools (*koryū*) that flourished before the Meiji government's Haitōrei edict of 1876 banned the carrying of swords; today the surviving schools are preserved and practiced in Japan and worldwide, usually with wooden swords (*bokutō*) in paired kata (Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The oldest preserved school is Tenshin Shōden Katori Shintō-ryū, founded in 1447 (traditionally dated to about 1480) by Iizasa Chōisai Ienao (about 1387–1488) at Katori Shrine in Sawara, Chiba Prefecture; it was designated an Intangible Cultural Asset of Japan on 6 March 1960, the first martial art ever so recognized (Katori Jingu, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Kenjutsu is the common ancestor of the modern budō arts kendo, iaidō and jōdō, and is coordinated internationally by the Nihon Kobudō Kyōkai (Japan Budo Federation) and the Kokusai Bujin Renmei (International Martial Arts Federation) (Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, n.d.-a; Kokusai Bujin Renmei, n.d.). Men and women both train in the surviving koryū — women have practiced kenjutsu in several schools for generations and participate fully in demonstrations and gradings today (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## Rules

Kenjutsu has no competitive point system: training consists of set kata — paired forms handed down in the school's scrolls (*densho*) — practiced with bokutō and, at advanced levels, with steel swords under strict safety rules, plus free-style practice (*randori*) in some schools (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, n.d.-a). Progression is marked by the traditional licence ranks culminating in *menkyo kaiden* (full license), awarded by the headmaster (*sōke* or *shihan*) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Each koryū has its own curriculum, weapons — long sword (*katana*), short sword, spear, naginata and others — and etiquette, codified in its densho; the Nihon Kobudō Kyōkai and the Kokusai Bujin Renmei organize demonstrations, seminars and the preservation of the schools rather than tournaments (Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, n.d.-a; Kokusai Bujin Renmei, n.d.). Safety rules in modern practice require control of the bokutō, correct distance (*maai*) and immediate stopping on the instructor's call; striking without control is forbidden (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men and women follow the same curriculum and grading in the schools that admit both (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Rule source: the individual koryū densho, coordinated by the Nihon Kobudō Kyōkai, https://www.kobudo.jp/ (Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, n.d.-a).

## History

The koryū sword schools emerged in the Muromachi period (1336–1573) and flourished in the Edo period (1603–1868) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Iizasa Chōisai Ienao founded Tenshin Shōden Katori Shintō-ryū at Katori Shrine in 1447 — the oldest martial art still practiced in its original form, designated an Intangible Cultural Asset on 6 March 1960 (Katori Jingu, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Tsukahara Bokuden (1489–1571) founded the Kashima Shin-ryū and developed the concept of *mutekatsu* — victory without a pre-emptive strike (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Miyamoto Musashi (about 1584–1645), Japan's most famous swordsman, founded the Hyōhō Niten Ichi-ryū (Two Heavens as One) school and wrote the *Book of Five Rings* (*Gorin no Sho*) in 1645 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). After the Haitōrei edict of 1876 and the collapse of the samurai class, the koryū nearly died out but were preserved by dedicated masters; the Nihon Kobudō Kyōkai was established to safeguard them, and the Kokusai Bujin Renmei now links koryū practitioners internationally (Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, n.d.-a; Kokusai Bujin Renmei, n.d.). Icons of kenjutsu are Iizasa Chōisai Ienao, Tsukahara Bokuden and Miyamoto Musashi (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
