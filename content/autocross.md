## Basic Information

Autocross — called Solo in the United States — is a timed motorsport in which one car at a time drives through a temporary course marked out with traffic cones on a large paved area such as an airfield or parking lot. The driver with the fastest clean run wins; each cone knocked over adds a two-second penalty. In the United States the discipline is governed by the Sports Car Club of America (SCCA), whose Tire Rack SCCA Solo National Championships are the pinnacle of the sport — the 53rd edition was held at Lincoln Airpark in Nebraska from September 7–11, 2026, with more than 1,200 drivers, roughly 8,400 timed runs and about 80 car classes, including dedicated Ladies classes (Sports Car Club of America [SCCA], n.d.-a). In Europe, autocross is also run as FIA-sanctioned racing on unpaved surfaces, with the FIA European Autocross Championship as its top series (FIA, n.d.) (Sports Car Club of America, n.d.-a) (Sports Car Club of America, n.d.-b).

The sport is one of the most accessible forms of four-wheel motorsport: competitors drive their own road cars, classified from near-stock "Stock" classes through "Street" and "Street Touring" up to heavily modified "Modified" classes, and a ProSolo variant adds a drag-race-style start with starting lights. Because the courses are short — typically 40–70 seconds — autocross is often called grassroots motorsport in its purest form (SCCA, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Under the SCCA Solo Rules, each competitor gets a set number of runs (typically three to six at nationals), and the best single run counts; each cone displaced adds a 2-second penalty to that run, and going off course or missing a gate voids the run. Cars are grouped into classes by level of preparation: Stock (unmodified production cars), Street (lightly modified), Street Touring, Street Prepared, Prepared, and Modified, with separate Ladies classes mirroring several of these; every car must meet safety rules such as a secure battery, functioning brakes and no loose items (SCCA, n.d.-b).

The course is designed so that speeds stay low — typically averaging 50–70 km/h with brief peaks around 80 km/h — and must include tight turns and a slalom section. Drivers walk the course before competing. In ProSolo, two identical mirrored courses are run side by side with a drag-tree start; in Solo II, two drivers share one car. The FIA European Autocross Championship, by contrast, races up to six cars simultaneously on closed dirt or gravel circuits against the clock, with qualifying heats, semifinals and a final (FIA, n.d.).

There are no separate women's rule sets — Ladies classes exist only to create a dedicated competitive field within the same technical rules. Age classes include a Junior division, and helmets meeting Snell or FIA standards are mandatory in most classes; open-wheel cars additionally require arm restraints.

## History

Autocross originated in the United States in the late 1940s, when sports car enthusiasts began timing runs through cone courses at airfields; the first SCCA-sanctioned Solo event is generally dated to 1948. The sport grew rapidly after World War II with the return of servicemen and the popularity of European sports cars. The SCCA Solo National Championships began in 1973 and moved to Lincoln Airpark, Nebraska, in 2010, where they have been held ever since (SCCA, n.d.-a).

In Europe, autocross developed in parallel from the 1950s, particularly in Germany, Czechoslovakia, Austria and the Netherlands, where dirt and grass courses became popular; the FIA created the European Championship for Autocross Drivers in 2004, run on closed unpaved circuits with multiple cars per heat. Notable champions include multi-time SCCA national champions such as Shelly Monfort, a 15-time Ladies class champion by 2026, and top Modified-class drivers such as Andy Mohr and Mark Thornton (SCCA, n.d.-b; FIA, n.d.). The women's side has grown steadily, with dedicated Ladies classes at every national event since the 1980s.
