## Basic Information

Backstroke swimming is the only stroke swum on the back: the swimmer starts in the water holding the grips, feet below the surface, and finishes on the back after touching the wall in the back position at every turn; it is raced over 50 m, 100 m and 200 m at the Olympic Games and World Aquatics Championships and is governed worldwide by World Aquatics (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; World Aquatics, n.d.-c). The long-course world records at the end of the 2026 season stand at 23.55 s over 50 m for Kliment Kolesnikov of Russia (Kazan 2023) and 26.56 s for Sara Curtis of Italy, who set the mark at the 2026 European Aquatics Championships in August 2026, breaking Kaylee McKeown's 26.86 of 2023; over 100 m for Thomas Ceccon of Italy (men) and Regan Smith of the USA (women); and over 200 m for Aaron Peirsol of the USA (1:51.92, 2009) and Kaylee McKeown of Australia (2:03.14, 2023) (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Among the leading backstrokers of the 2026 season, the women come first: Regan Smith of the USA (pictured), the long-course 100 m backstroke world record holder, Kaylee McKeown of Australia — double Olympic champion over 100 m and 200 m backstroke at Tokyo 2020 and Paris 2024, ten-time individual world backstroke champion and holder of the 200 m world record — and Sara Curtis of Italy, the new 50 m world record holder (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). On the men's side Kliment Kolesnikov holds the 50 m record, Thomas Ceccon the 100 m record, and China's Xu Jiayu became the first Asian man under 24 seconds over 50 m with 23.92 at the 2026 Chinese national championships, while South Africa's Pieter Coetze set a Commonwealth record of 51.98 over 100 m at the 2026 Commonwealth Games (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Backstroke races are swum in the same certified LCM or SCM pools, lanes and water conditions as all pool events (World Aquatics, n.d.-b; World Aquatics, n.d.-d). At the start the swimmer is in the water, facing the starting wall, holding the grips with both hands and with feet below the water surface; the body must be on the back from the start until the finish, except that at each turn the swimmer may turn onto the breast and must touch the wall in the back position, with a somersault turn permitted (World Aquatics, n.d.-d).

At each turn and at the finish the backstroke swimmer must touch the wall while on the back; after the start and each turn no more than 15 m underwater is allowed, and a false start disqualifies under the single-false-start rule (World Aquatics, n.d.-d). Races run as heats, semifinals and finals (50 m and 100 m) or heats and finals (200 m), timed to 1/100 s; world records are ratified under Rule SW12 (World Aquatics, n.d.-d; World Aquatics, n.d.-e). Men and women contest the same distances under identical rules, junior championships use the same events in age groups, and para swimming offers backstroke in the SB classes (World Aquatics, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

Backstroke was swum at the second modern Olympics in Paris 1900 — won by Germany's Ernst Hoppenberg — and has been on every Olympic programme since, with the women's event added at Stockholm 1912 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Early icons include the USA's Harry Hebner, the 100 m backstroke champion of 1912, and Germany's Warren Kealoha, the 1920 Antwerp champion (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Pieter van den Hoogenband of the Netherlands, pictured, won Olympic gold over 100 m and 200 m backstroke at Sydney 2000 and again over 200 m at Athens 2004, and Aaron Peirsol of the USA, pictured, won the 100 m and 200 m backstroke at Athens 2004 and Atlanta 2008 and set the 200 m world record of 1:51.92 in Rome 2009 — a mark that still stands in 2026 (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
