## Basic Information

Coasteering is an outdoor adventure sport in which participants travel along a stretch of coastline on foot and in the water — scrambling over rocks, climbing gullies, jumping into rock pools, swimming through channels and scrambling back out — following a route that combines cliff scrambling, scrambling, swimming and rock jumping (National Coasteering Charter, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The term was coined in 1973 by the British climber Edward C. Pyatt in his book *Sea Cliff Climbing*, and the sport was established as guided activity in 1986 in Pembrokeshire, Wales, by Andy Middleton at Twr-y-Felin (Wikipedia, n.d.). Today coasteering is offered on the coasts of the United Kingdom — Cornwall, Dorset (Dancing Ledge and Durdle Door), Pembrokeshire in Wales, the Isle of Anglesey, Northumberland, Scotland and Northern Ireland — as well as in New Zealand, Madeira, California and Croatia, and timed coasteering races are held on some stretches of coast (National Coasteering Charter, n.d.). In the UK the activity is coordinated by the National Coasteering Charter, formed in 2011 with the RNLI and RoSPA to set safety standards for providers (National Coasteering Charter, n.d.). A coasteerer scrambling at Dancing Ledge, Dorset, in June 2026 is pictured below. There is no world federation; the National Coasteering Charter is the UK rule/safety body, and participants are mixed-gender groups with a minimum age of about 10 and a 25 m swim requirement (National Coasteering Charter, n.d.).

## Rules

Coasteering has no single official rulebook because there is no world federation; in the UK the de facto standard is the National Coasteering Charter's safety guidance, developed with the RNLI and RoSPA, which governs guided groups (National Coasteering Charter, n.d.). Participants wear a 4–5 mm wetsuit, a helmet certified to BS EN 1385 (water sports helmet), a buoyancy aid / personal flotation device and closed-toe footwear that is secured so it cannot be lost in the surf; the group stays together under a qualified guide (National Coasteering Charter, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Entry requirements for guided sessions are typically a minimum age of about 10 years and the ability to swim 25 m confidently in open water; younger children join family "mini" routes (National Coasteering Charter, n.d.). Routes are graded for difficulty, and timed races exist on some coasts, but the standard activity is untimed and non-competitive — the "rules" are really safety rules: stay within the group, jump only where the guide has checked depth, and never enter the water alone (National Coasteering Charter, n.d.). There are no gender or age categories in competition because coasteering is predominantly a guided leisure activity open to mixed groups; organized races, where they exist, are usually mixed or split simply into adult and junior classes (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

The sport grew out of British sea-cliff climbing: the term "coasteering" was coined in 1973 by Edward C. Pyatt in his book *Sea Cliff Climbing*, and in 1986 Andy Middleton began running guided coasteering trips from Twr-y-Felin in Pembrokeshire, Wales, combining cliff scrambling with swimming and rock jumping (Wikipedia, n.d.). The activity spread along the Welsh, Cornish, Devon and Scottish coasts in the 1990s, and providers formed the National Coasteering Charter in 2011 with the RNLI and RoSPA to standardize safety after a series of incidents (National Coasteering Charter, n.d.). Iconic venues include Dancing Ledge and Durdle Door in Dorset, the coast of Anglesey and the caves of Northumberland; the sport also took root in New Zealand, where the long coastline suits it, and in Madeira and parts of the US West Coast (Wikipedia, n.d.). Women have been part of coasteering since its beginnings — many of the early guides and record holders of timed routes were women, and today guided groups and races are fully mixed (Wikipedia, n.d.). In 2026 coasteering at Dancing Ledge, Dorset, remains one of the most popular guided outdoor activities in England (Wikipedia, n.d.).
