## Basic Information

Bökh (Mongolian wrestling) is the traditional folk wrestling style of the Mongols and one of the "three games of men" at the Naadam festival, alongside horse racing and archery. Wrestlers wear the open-chested zodog jacket and shuudag shorts; the bout is lost the moment any part of the body other than the hands or feet touches the ground. Naadam was inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2010, and bökh is also contested at the World Nomad Games.

## Rules

A bökh bout has no time limit and no weight classes: two wrestlers grapple standing, using grips on the jacket and belt, trips, lifts, hooks and throws to make the opponent touch the ground with anything other than hands or feet. Before the bout, wrestlers perform the eagle dance (devekh) and slap their thighs; after a win, the victor performs a victory dance and the loser's zasuul (coach) sings praise. At the National Naadam in Ulaanbaatar, 512 or 1024 wrestlers compete in a single-elimination tournament of nine or ten rounds; the highest-ranked wrestler may choose his opponent. Lifetime titles are awarded by rounds won: nachin (falcon, 5 rounds), khartsaga (hawk, 6), zaan (elephant, 7), garuda (8), arslan (lion, 9) and avarga (titan, champion).

## History

Wrestling has been part of Mongol military training and festivals since at least the 13th century — the Secret History of the Mongols (1240) records a match between Buri the Wrestler and Belgutei in 1200. Genghis Khan promoted wrestling to select warriors and rank candidates. The structured Naadam festival dates from 1639 and was made an annual state festival in 1912; the record 6,002-wrestler tournament in 2011 earned a Guinness World Record. Bökh is also a World Nomad Games sport (as "Mongol Bokh"), and Mongolian wrestlers such as Hakuho (later yokozuna in sumo) began in bökh.
