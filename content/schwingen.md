## Basic Information

Schwingen — also called Swiss wrestling and known natively as Hosenlupf ("breeches-lifting") — is a style of folk wrestling native to the pre-alpine, German-speaking parts of Switzerland. Two wrestlers (Schwinger) grip each other's jute breeches and attempt to throw each other onto the back in a sawdust ring; the winner of the sport's biggest festival is crowned Schwingerkönig, the "wrestling king", and receives a bull as prize. Schwingen is considered a national sport of Switzerland, alongside Hornussen and Steinstossen, and is governed by the Eidgenössischer Schwingerverband (ESV), founded in 1895.

## Rules

Bouts (Gänge) take place in a circular sawdust ring twelve metres in diameter. Each Schwinger wears a Schwinghose — shorts of jute over his clothes — which the opponents grip at the back belt area; the winner is the one who throws his opponent so that both shoulder blades touch the ground, without releasing the grip on the trousers. Throws bear names such as kurz, übersprung, hüter, wyberhaagge and brienzer — several closely resembling judo techniques — and bouts are judged by three referees who award points for winning throws (a maximum of ten) and activity-in-points in undecided matches; by tradition, the winner brushes the sawdust from the loser's back afterwards. At festivals each Schwinger wrestles six opponents (eight at the Eidgenössische), with the two leading point scorers meeting in the Schlussgang; pairing is made by a "fight court" and there are no weight classes, though champions are typically powerful men well over 100 kg. The supreme event is the Eidgenössisches Schwing- und Älplerfest (ESAF), staged every three years, whose winner takes the title of Schwingerkönig and the traditional prize of a live bull (a Muneli); the Unspunnenfest, held near Interlaken roughly every six years, carries comparable prestige and 2025-era festivals draw over 300,000 spectators.

## History

Wrestling images with the characteristic trouser grip appear as early as a 13th-century Lausanne cathedral illustration, but Schwingen as Alpine shepherd culture took shape in the 17th and 18th centuries in the Emmental, Haslital and Entlebuch — villages meeting on Alpine pastures for mountain wrestling festivals (Bergschwinget). The Entlebuch pastor Franz Josef Stalder recorded a first rule set in his 1797 Fragmente über Entlebuch, and the modern sport was institutionalised after the patriotic Unspunnenfest of 1805 near Interlaken, staged to revive Swiss tradition under French occupation and repeated in 1808; the Bernese physician Rudolf Schärer's 1864 wrestling manual spread Schwingen via the Turnen movement's national festivals at which it was classified as Nationalturnen alongside stone throwing. The national federation (Eidgenössischer Schwingerverband) followed in 1895 — prompted by the success of the first Eidgenössisches Schwinger- und Älplerfest, held in 1889 in industrial-era Zurich — and the ESAF has since grown into the sport's flagship, staged at three-year intervals. The elite wrestlers of each era are known as the "Böse" (the "wicked"), and the sport today thrives on a national circuit of roughly 120 festivals a year, regionally anchored by the Brünig, Stoos and Rigi events, with an active women's Schwingen circuit run since 1980 by the Frauen-Schwingverband.
