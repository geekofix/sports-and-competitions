## Basic Information

Formula One (F1) is the highest class of international single-seater auto racing, governed by the FIA under its Technical and Sporting Regulations; the 2026 season is the 77th FIA Formula One World Championship, running from March 8 to December 6, 2026 (FIA, n.d.-a; Formula 1, n.d.-a). The 2026 drivers' world champion is Andrea Kimi Antonelli of Mercedes, born August 25, 2006, in Bologna, who clinched the title at the Mexico City Grand Prix on November 1, 2026 by finishing third (he needed only ninth): at 20 years and 68 days he is the youngest world champion in history, beating Sebastian Vettel's mark of 23 years and 134 days, and the first Italian champion since Alberto Ascari in 1953 — the 36th different world champion (FIA, n.d.-a; Formula 1, n.d.-b). Antonelli won nine races in 2026, including his maiden victory in China and a home win at Monza from 19th on the grid, and kept his racing number 12; Mercedes sealed its ninth Constructors' Championship at the São Paulo Grand Prix on November 8, and after Las Vegas the standings read Antonelli ahead of Max Verstappen (279 points) and George Russell (262) (Formula 1, n.d.-a). The 2026 regulations introduced a roughly 50/50 split between the combustion engine and the electric hybrid system, fully sustainable fuel, active aerodynamics (the low-drag "X-mode" on straights) and narrower, lighter cars, with the 11th team, Cadillac (Sergio Pérez and Valtteri Bottas), joining the grid and the new Madring circuit in Madrid hosting its first Grand Prix; the Bahrain and Saudi Arabian rounds were postponed because of the regional conflict (FIA, n.d.-c). The 2025 champion was Lando Norris of McLaren (FIA, n.d.-a).

On the women's side, Formula 1 today fields its future female stars through the F1 Academy support series, while historically five women — headed by Maria Teresa de Filippis, the first to start a Grand Prix in 1958 — have started World Championship races (F1 Academy, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## Rules

The FIA Formula One Sporting and Technical Regulations 2026 are the rulebook (FIA, n.d.-b; FIA, n.d.-c). Each of the 10 teams fields two identical-spec cars; the 2026 power unit combines a 1.6-litre turbo V6 with a much more powerful electric system (roughly 350 kW combustion plus 350 kW electric), running on fully sustainable fuel, with active front and rear wings for the low-drag X-mode and a minimum car weight set by the Technical Regulations (FIA, n.d.-c). Tyres are supplied by Pirelli (three dry compounds per weekend plus intermediates and wets). A Grand Prix weekend runs free practice, qualifying in a three-part knockout (Q1 18 cars, Q2 15, Q3 10) and the race of 305 km (260 km at Monaco) limited to two hours of racing and three hours total; eight sprint weekends add a short Saturday race with points 8-7-6-5-4-3-2-1 (FIA, n.d.-b). Race points are 25-18-15-12-10-8-6-5-4-3-2-1 plus 1 for the fastest lap if the finisher is in the top 10 (FIA, n.d.-b). DRS may be used in the designated zones when within one second of the car ahead; parc fermé rules freeze car setups after qualifying; fouls — blocking, causing a collision, ignoring flags, unsafe rejoins — are punished with 5- or 10-second penalties, drive-throughs, stop-go penalties, grid drops or reprimands by the stewards, with a safety car or virtual safety car neutralizing the race when needed (FIA, n.d.-b). Drivers need a FIA Super Licence (minimum age 18, 40 points from feeder series; since 2015 only 17-year-old Max Verstappen held a dispensation) — women compete on equal terms, with the F1 Academy as the dedicated feeder (FIA, n.d.-b; F1 Academy, n.d.).

## History

Grand Prix motor racing began with the 1906 French Grand Prix at Le Mans, organized by the Automobile Club de France, and the FIA — founded in 1904 as the AIACR — created the World Championship for Drivers in 1950; its first champion was Giuseppe "Nino" Farina of Alfa Romeo (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The sport's icons include Juan Manuel Fangio (five titles, 1951 and 1954–1957), Alberto Ascari (1952–53), Stirling Moss, Jim Clark (1963, 1965), Jackie Stewart (three titles, 1969, 1971, 1973), Niki Lauda (1975, 1977, 1984), Ayrton Senna of Brazil (1988, 1990, 1991), Alain Prost (four titles, 1985, 1986, 1989, 1993), Michael Schumacher (a record seven titles, 1994–95 and 2000–04), Lewis Hamilton (record seven titles, 2008 and 2014–2017, 2018–2020) and Max Verstappen (four consecutive titles, 2021–2024) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Senna, who died in 1994 after the San Marino Grand Prix, remains one of the most beloved figures in the sport's history (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
