## Basic Information

The keirin is a motor-paced sprint race in track cycling: six to nine riders start from blocks and settle in behind a pacer motorcycle (a derny), which gradually accelerates and then leaves the track, leaving a flat-out sprint to the finish at speeds above 70 km/h (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.). It was developed in Japan around 1948 as a legalized betting sport to fund post-war reconstruction, and it became an Olympic event for men at Sydney 2000 and for women at London 2012; it has been a UCI World Championship event for men since 1980 and for women since 2002 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Among the current leading riders — women first — are Ellesse Andrews of New Zealand, the Paris 2024 Olympic keirin champion, and the Netherlands' Harrie Lavreysen, the 2024 Olympic champion and 2025 world champion with his fourth keirin rainbow jersey (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).

## Rules

Under the UCI Cycling Regulations, riders use brakeless fixed-gear bikes and draw lots for their starting positions behind the pacer (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.). On a 250 m track the riders must remain behind the derny for the first laps — the pacer starts at about 30 km/h and builds to 50 km/h — and leaves the track 750 m before the finish, i.e. with three laps to go; the first rider across the line wins, with finishing speeds over 70 km/h (Wikipedia, n.d.). Races are about 1.5–2 km in total (eight laps on a 250 m track, six on 333 m, four to five on 400 m), and competitions run in rounds: first-round heats, repechages for the eliminated riders, semifinals and a final for places 1–6 (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.). Dangerous riding — pushing, blocking or crossing the inner line — can cost relegation or disqualification (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men and women race the same format in separate events; the women's world championship has been held since 2002 and the Olympic women's keirin since London 2012 (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.).

## History

The first keirin race was held on 20 November 1948 at the Kokura Velodrome in Kitakyushu, Japan, under the post-war Bicycle Racing Law, drawing over 55,000 spectators and ¥19.73 million in bets on day one as cities used the sport to rebuild after World War II (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Nihon Jitensha Shinkōkai (NJS) was founded in 1957 to standardize the sport, today regulated by the JKA Foundation; aspiring professionals train 15 hours a day at the Japan Keirin School, and by 2011 annual bets exceeded ¥600 billion (Wikipedia, n.d.). Japanese women raced from 1949 to 1964 and were reintroduced in July 2012 as "Girls Keirin" (Wikipedia, n.d.). Internationally, the IOC admitted the event in December 1996 and it debuted at Sydney 2000 — an admission later clouded by a BBC investigation into payments from the Japan Keirin Association to the UCI (Wikipedia, n.d.). Olympic men's champions ran from Florian Rousseau (2000) via Chris Hoy (2008, 2012) and Jason Kenny (2016, 2020) to Harrie Lavreysen (2024); the women's champions are Victoria Pendleton (2012), Elis Ligtlee (2016), Shanne Braspennincx (2020) and Ellesse Andrews (2024) (Wikipedia, n.d.). Japanese icons include Koichi Nakano, winner of ten consecutive sprint world titles from 1977 to 1986, and Katsuaki Matsumoto (1928–2021), the record holder with 1,341 career wins who retired in 1981 at age 53 (Wikipedia, n.d.).
