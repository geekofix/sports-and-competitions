## Basic Information

Skijoring is a winter racing sport in which a skier is pulled across snow by a horse, a dog team or a motor vehicle while holding a tow rope (ISDRA, n.d.). Competitive skijoring is organised internationally in dog classes — one-dog and two-dog — for men and women at world championships, while horse skijoring is raced at historic events such as the White Turf in St. Moritz, Switzerland, held since 1907 on the frozen lake, and in North America, where the Leadville Skijor in Colorado, first held in the late 1940s and staged for the 75th time in March 2023, is the oldest continuously held race of its kind (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). SkijorUSA, the United States national body, slated 31 races for 2026 (SkijorUSA, n.d.). Among the current leading women, the men's and women's classes at the world championships and the White Turf are contested on equal terms, with women winning overall titles in both horse and dog skijoring. The longest traditional races are the 440 km Kalevala race in Finland and the 120-mile River Runner in Whitehorse, Canada (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## Rules

In dog skijoring, governed internationally by the International Sled Dog Racing Association (ISDRA) and in Europe by the European Skijoring and Dog Racing Association (ESDRA), the skier is attached by a tow line to a harness on one or two dogs; races run over roughly 5–20 km of groomed track, the dog(s) provide all propulsion, and the skier may use poles but may not be pulled by anything else (ISDRA, n.d.). Men and women race in separate one-dog and two-dog classes at the world championships, and age-group classes exist at major events (ISDRA, n.d.). In horse skijoring, a horse and rider tow the skier at speeds up to 60 km/h; the skier holds a tow rope attached to the saddle, must not let go over jumps (dropping the rope or a fall brings disqualification), and races are started at intervals with staggered horse starts so the skier is not run over (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Courses are point-to-point over snow-covered roads or frozen lakes, with mandatory helmets and, in dog skijoring, mandatory harness and line equipment; officials check tow lines at the start (ISDRA, n.d.). Because the horse or dog is a trained animal working in harness, the sport is a regulated trained-animal discipline rather than an animal-harm sport.

## History

Skijoring takes its name from the Norwegian skikjøring, "ski driving", practised in the 19th century, and it appeared at the Nordic Games of 1901, 1905 and 1909 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). It was a demonstration sport at the first Winter Olympics in Chamonix 1924 and again at St. Moritz 1928, where on 12 February 1928 eight skiers were towed behind horses on the frozen lake and the podium was swept by the hosts — Rudolf Wettstein, Bibi Torriani and Henryk Mückenbrunn (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Torriani, also an Olympic bobsledder, became one of the best-known exponents of the sport. The White Turf in St. Moritz, first held in 1907, remains the most famous horse-skijoring event, and in North America skijoring spread with European settlers, becoming a staple of Rocky Mountain winter carnivals such as Leadville (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Dog skijoring developed alongside sled-dog racing in the 20th century and now has its own international championship structure under ISDRA and ESDRA (ISDRA, n.d.).
