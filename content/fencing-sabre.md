## Basic Information

Sabre is the cutting-and-thrusting weapon of fencing — derived from the 19th-century cavalry saber — in which the valid target is everything above the waist, including head and arms, the rules of right of way decide priority, and the bouts are the fastest and most explosive of the three weapons (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men's sabre has been contested at every Olympic Games since Athens 1896, and women's sabre joined the Olympic program at Athens 2004 (Olympics.com, n.d.). The weapon is governed by the Fédération Internationale d'Escrime (FIE), whose 2026 World Championships in Hong Kong (22–30 July 2026) included men's and women's individual and team sabre among the twelve events (FIE, n.d.-a). See [Fencing](fencing.html) on this site for the shared rules of the sport.

In the 2026/27 season the women's sabre is led by France's Toscane Tori and Bulgaria's Yoana Ilieva, while the men's sabre rankings are headed by France's Sébastien Patrice; Hungary's Áron Szilágyi, three-time individual Olympic champion from 2012 to 2020, remains the reference of the weapon (FIE, n.d.-b).

## Rules

The sabre blade is at most 88 cm long, the assembled weapon at most 105 cm and 500 g; unlike foil and épée, a touch can be scored with the point or with the cutting edge of the blade (FIE, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The valid target is the whole body above the waist — head, arms and torso; at sabre the conductive lamé covers this area and the mask bib and the glove cuff are also part of the target (FIE, n.d.-b). Off-target hits do not score.

Right of way works as in foil: the attacker — with a lunge, a flèche or a cut to the head or arm — holds priority, as does the defender who parries and ripostes or counter-attacks (FIE, n.d.-b). When both fencers hit within the apparatus window, only the fencer with right of way scores (FIE, n.d.-b). Crossing the feet is illegal in sabre, which distinguishes it from foil and épée, where the flèche — a running attack with crossed feet — is allowed (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Pool bouts last three minutes and end at 5 touches; direct-elimination bouts are three periods of three minutes with one-minute breaks, first to 15 touches, with a one-minute priority period if tied; team events are relays of nine bouts counting to 45 (FIE, n.d.-b). Fouls such as corps-à-corps, covering the target and turning the back are penalized with yellow, red or black cards (FIE, n.d.-b). Sabre was the last weapon to receive electric scoring, in 1988 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Men and women fence sabre under identical rules, with a compulsory chest protector for women; women's sabre has been an Olympic event since Athens 2004 (FIE, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Age classifications are cadet (under 17), junior (under 20), senior (20 and over) and veteran (40 and over), each with FIE World Championships and World Cup circuits (FIE, n.d.-a).

## History

The sabre evolved from the cavalry saber of the 19th century and was one of the two weapons of the first modern Olympics in Athens 1896; when the FIE was founded in Paris in 1913, Hungarian rules were adopted for the sabre (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Electric scoring reached the sabre in 1988, completing the electrification of all three weapons (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women's sabre entered the Olympic program at Athens 2004, with the American Mariel Zagunis as the first Olympic champion in the event (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Hungary has historically dominated the weapon: Aladár Gerevich (1910–1991) won seven Olympic golds, Pál Kovács (1912–1995) six team golds, and Rudolf Kárpáti (1920–1944) six gold medals between 1936 and 1960 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
