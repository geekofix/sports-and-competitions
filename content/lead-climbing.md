## Basic Information

Lead climbing is the rope-and-quickdraw discipline of competition climbing: the climber ties into a rope at the base of an artificial wall, clips the rope into fixed quickdraws as they ascend a route of at least 15 m, and scores for the highest hold reached (World Climbing, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). A fall does not end the attempt — the climber may re-enter the rope and continue, but the score stops at the highest point reached (World Climbing, n.d.-a). Lead is one of the three Olympic disciplines alongside bouldering and speed, and the international federation — long known as the International Federation of Sport Climbing (IFSC), founded in 2007 in Frankfurt — rebranded as World Climbing on 10 December 2025, with the IFSC World Cup becoming the World Climbing Series from the 2026 season (World Climbing, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Men and women compete under identical rules in separate categories, and the women's field is led by Slovenia's Janja Garnbret, who had collected 34 lead World Climbing Series victories by November 2026 — the most of any lead climber — while Japan's Neo Suzuki won the men's 2026 lead series (Wikipedia, n.d.-c) (World Climbing, n.d.-b).

## Rules

In senior competition, qualification is climbed on two routes with a 6-minute time limit each, followed by a semi-final and a final on single routes; finalists study the final route from the ground during a fixed sighting period before climbing on sight, while earlier rounds are climbed from isolation without route preview (World Climbing, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The score is the hold number reached; a climber who grasps the next hold scores "plus"; ties are broken by countback — comparing earlier attempts — and ultimately by climbing time, with the faster climber ranked higher (World Climbing, n.d.-a). Exceeding the time limit stops the attempt at the next hold; clipping the rope out of sequence, falling with the rope unclipped behind the last quickdraw, leaving the marked route or using footholds on the wall's side panels results in disqualification (World Climbing, n.d.-a). Equipment rules cover rope diameter, harnesses, helmets in youth categories and chalk; routes are set by certified route setters and homologated for grade reporting (World Climbing, n.d.-a). Competition climbing also includes bouldering (short unroped problems, zone/top bonuses) and speed (a standardized 15 m wall against the clock), with a combined boulder-and-lead format used at the Olympics since Paris 2024 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Men's and women's lead use the same routes, time limits and scoring, differing only in the athlete category; youth categories U16, U18 and U20 follow the same framework (World Climbing, n.d.-a).

## History

Competition climbing grew out of rock climbing and alpine culture: the International Council for Competition Climbing was created inside the UIAA in 1997, and the IFSC became its independent successor in 2007, gaining full IOC recognition in 2010 (World Climbing, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The first world championships were held under UIAA auspices in 1991, and climbing debuted at the Olympic Games at Tokyo 2020 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Among the shaping icons are Lynn Hill of the United States, who in 1993 became the first person to free climb the Nose of El Capitan; Austria's Angela Eiter, four-time world champion who in 2017 became the first woman to climb grade 9b; Czech Adam Ondra, who in September 2017 made the first ascent of Silence, graded 9c — the first of that grade; and Chris Sharma of the United States, who in 2013 climbed La Dura Dura, then the hardest sport route in the world (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). On the women's side, Janja Garnbret has been the dominant figure of the 2020s, with multiple world titles and Olympic gold at Paris 2024 (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).
