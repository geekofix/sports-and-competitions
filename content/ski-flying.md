## Basic Information

Ski flying is the extreme version of ski jumping, in which athletes launch from specially enormous hills and soar more than 200 metres through the air, with ratified world-record jumps beyond 250 metres (FIS, n.d.-a). It is governed by the International Ski Federation (FIS) and contested on only a handful of certified ski flying hills — Vikersund in Norway, Planica in Slovenia, Oberstdorf in Germany, Kulm in Bad Mitterndorf, Austria, and Harrachov in the Czech Republic (FIS, n.d.-b). The discipline has its own World Cup crystal globe and its own biennial FIS Ski Flying World Championships; the 2026 championships, the 29th edition, were held 22–25 January 2026 in Oberstdorf, and the 2025–26 FIS Ski Flying World Cup concluded on 29 March 2026 in Planica (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Among the current leading women is Nika Prevc of Slovenia, who won the inaugural official women's ski flying World Cup in 2025–26 with a record 2,676 points — the first women's ski flying globe in history — and holds the women's world record of 242.5 m, set in Planica in 2026; among the men, her brother Domen Prevc won the 2025–26 overall and ski flying titles and holds the men's world record of 254.5 m, set in Planica in 2025 (FIS, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Ski flying is scored exactly like ski jumping under the FIS International Competition Rules: measured distance points plus style points from five judges, with wind and gate compensation equalising changing conditions — but on hills whose construction point (K-point) is 185 metres or more, so flight phases exceed four seconds and in-runs are reached at around 100 km/h (FIS, n.d.-b). The full flight demands an extremely stable V-style aerodynamic position over the mammoth snow slope and a clean telemark landing to avoid falls at speeds above 100 km/h. Individual competitions normally consist of two jumping rounds — the second run in reversed order of the standings — and the FIS Ski Flying World Championships additionally feature a team competition on each flying hill used (FIS, n.d.-a). Women and men compete in separate classifications: since the 2025–26 season the women's ski flying World Cup awards its own small crystal globe, the first official women's season in the discipline, while the women's world record (242.5 m, Nika Prevc, 2026) and the men's world record (254.5 m, Domen Prevc, 2025) are ratified separately by the FIS (FIS, n.d.-a). Ski flying has never been an Olympic event; Olympic ski jumping is contested on normal and large hills instead. Only five hills worldwide currently hold FIS ski flying certification, and after a 2011 rule change the FIS permitted construction up to HS 225–235 while pausing further expansion (FIS, n.d.-b).

## History

Ski flying began in 1934–1935 in Planica, then in Yugoslavia, where the giant Bloudkova velikanka hill was built to push jumps beyond 100 metres; the Austrian Josef Bradl achieved the first jump over 100 m there in 1936, and the hill's designer, the Slovene engineer Stanko Bloudek, made Planica the cradle of long-distance jumping (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Birger Ruud and Sigmund Ruud of Norway pioneered the aerodynamic style that made such distances possible in the 1930s. The FIS long viewed the discipline with suspicion, but the KOP working group (Kulm, Oberstdorf, Planica), formed in 1962, liaised with the FIS on hill standards, and the first FIS Ski Flying World Championships were held in Planica in 1972; a dedicated World Cup classification has been awarded since 1991 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The distance record climbed steadily on the globe's few flying hills: Bjørn Einar Romøren's 239.5 m at Planica in 2005, Johan Remen Evensen's 246.5 m at Vikersund in 2011, Peter Prevc's historic first ratified 250 m at Vikersund in 2015 (he retired in 2025), and Anders Fannemel's 251.5 m in 2015. Ryōyū Kobayashi's 291 m landing in a private 2024 project in Iceland stands outside FIS ratification. The showpiece finales in Planica and Vikersund, the Planica 7 festival in particular, draw crowds of tens of thousands (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
