## Basic Information

Snowboarding is the winter sport of riding a snowboard — a single wide board strapped to both feet — down snow-covered slopes, whether carving pistes, racing gates, boosting out of halfpipes or floating through deep powder. Born in American counterculture of the 1960s\u201380s, it became an Olympic sport at Nagano 1998 and is governed today by the International Ski and Snowboard Federation (FIS), which runs World Cup circuits, world championships and the Olympic snowboard programme across its freestyle (halfpipe, slopestyle, big air), racing (snowboard cross, parallel giant slalom) and para snowboard events.

## Rules

Competitive snowboarding divides into judged freestyle and timed racing branches. Freestyle disciplines — halfpipe, slopestyle and big air — score runs 0\u2013100 on difficulty, execution, amplitude and overall impression, in pipes of 22-foot walls, park courses of rails and jumps, and giant single kickers. Racing disciplines put the clock first: parallel giant slalom pits two riders head-to-head on mirrored courses, while snowboard cross races four to six riders down a jump-and-berm course with the first across the line advancing; para snowboard's banked slalom and cross formats mirror the Olympic events. Equipment splits accordingly — twin-tip freestyle boards with soft boots and bindings versus directional alpine boards with hard boots and plate bindings — helmets are mandatory, and FIS competition rules (the ICR Book VI) govern everything from course homologation to licensing.

## History

The modern snowboard descends from the 1960s "Snurfer" of Michigan's Sherman Poppen — a toy sold in the millions that Jake Burton Carpenter and Tom Sims developed into real boards with bindings in the late 1970s, founding Burton Snowboards and Sims Snowboards. The sport fought for resort access through the 1980s (many US resorts banned snowboarding outright) while building its own culture of halfpipes, contests and magazines; the dramatic growth of the era led to a governance split between the rider-owned International Snowboarding Federation (ISF, founded 1990s pro tours) and the FIS, which took the sport into the 1998 Nagano Olympics — on condition that FIS, not the ISF, sanction it, a decision that caused the initial rider boycotts and defined the sport's commercial era. Olympic halfpipe and giant slalom debuted at Nagano 1998  and the programme grew to the modern set — parallel giant slalom from 2002, snowboard cross from 2006, slopestyle from 2014, big air from 2018 and mixed team snowboard cross from 2022 — while Shaun White's halfpipe legacy, Chloe Kim's, Ayumu Hirano's and the slopestyle stars of the X Games era made the sport a global youth culture as well as an Olympic staple. Today snowboarding's professional calendar spans the FIS World Cup, X Games, Dew Tour and resort film culture, with the sport's freestyle branch in particular fuelling massive participation growth worldwide.
