## Basic Information

Palín is the traditional stick-and-ball game of the Mapuche people of Chile — a cousin of field hockey and hurling — in which two teams on a long, narrow field (the paliwe) strike a small leather or wooden ball with curved wooden sticks (wiño), aiming to drive it over the opponent's goal line (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is one of the oldest documented team games of the Americas, described by Spanish chroniclers already in the 16th century, and is recognized in Chile as living traditional sport heritage (Wikipedia, n.d.). The game is played by community clubs across south-central Chile, with regional championships and both men's and women's teams keeping the tradition alive as living Mapuche culture (Wikipedia, n.d.).

 Within the combat sports and martial arts family on this site, its closest relatives are [Boxing](boxing.html), [Bare Knuckle Boxing](bare-knuckle-boxing.html), [Kickboxing](kickboxing.html), [Muay Thai](muay-thai.html), [Muay Boran](muay-boran.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Matches are played on a long, narrow, rectangular field — historically about 200 m long and 12 m wide, shortened for modern organized play — between two teams of equal size, commonly six a side in today's club form (Wikipedia, n.d.). Players strike the small ball (pali) with curved wooden sticks and advance it toward the opposite goal line; each goal (tripal) is worth one point, and when the trailing team scores, a point is deducted from the leading team's score — a team must score four consecutive unanswered points to win (Wikipedia, n.d.). Strong physical contact is part of the traditional game, so injuries are common, and modern community leagues regulate tackling and enforce referee control in a spirit of sportsmanship that the Mapuche tradition treats as part of the game itself (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men's and women's teams play under the same community rules, with mixed junior teams common in school play (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

Palín was played by the Mapuche long before the colonial era — the first Spanish expedition in 1550 already reported the Mapuche as "very great players of chueca," as the conquistadors called the game — and 17th-century chroniclers such as Alonso de Ovalle (1646) and later Claudio Gay (1854) drew and described matches (Wikipedia, n.d.). The game served ritual, social and war-training purposes, and the Spanish colonial government repeatedly banned it during the 17th and 18th centuries because of these associations; it was revived after Chilean independence in the 19th century (Wikipedia, n.d.). In the 20th century Leotardo Matus wrote the first written regulations in 1920 for school use, and today palín survives as the only pre-Columbian Mapuche game still played, with community clubs in the Araucanía and Biobío regions (Wikipedia, n.d.).
