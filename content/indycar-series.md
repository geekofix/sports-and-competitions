## Basic Information

The IndyCar Series is the premier championship of American open-wheel racing, sanctioned by INDYCAR and contested on a mix of oval speedways, permanent road courses and temporary street circuits across the United States. Cars are Dallara chassis powered by turbocharged V6 engines from Honda or Chevrolet, reaching speeds well above 370 km/h on ovals. The centerpiece of the season is the Indianapolis 500 at the Indianapolis Motor Speedway, first held in 1911 and billed as "The Greatest Spectacle in Racing".

## Rules

A season consists of roughly 17 races of 200 to 500 miles; the championship is decided by points awarded for finishing position, with bonus points for pole position and leading laps. Races begin with a rolling or standing start, and full-course caution periods bunch the field behind the pace car. Cars make pit stops for tires and adjustments, and on road and street courses drivers may use the push-to-pass system, a limited amount of extra turbo boost per race. At the Indianapolis 500, qualifying decides a field of 33 cars by four-lap average speed, with the fastest earning pole; the race itself is 200 laps around the 2.5-mile oval. Penalties apply for avoidable contact, blocking and pit lane infringements.

## History

The Indianapolis 500 was first run on May 30, 1911 and won by Ray Harroun in the Marmon Wasp; American championship car racing was long sanctioned by the AAA and later USAC, and CART was founded in 1979. After a split in 1996, Tony George created the Indy Racing League, which reunified with CART as the IndyCar Series in 2008. Legendary drivers include A.J. Foyt, the first four-time winner of the Indianapolis 500, Al Unser and Rick Mears with four wins each, Mario Andretti, who also became Formula 1 World Champion, and Emerson Fittipaldi. Modern stars include Scott Dixon, a multiple series champion, Will Power, Josef Newgarden and Álex Palou, racing for powerhouse teams such as Team Penske and Chip Ganassi Racing.
