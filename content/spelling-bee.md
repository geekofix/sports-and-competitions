## Basic Information

A spelling bee is a competition in which contestants are asked to spell a broad selection of words, with each speller eliminated upon a single misspell; the last speller standing wins. Contested from classrooms up to the famous Scripps National Spelling Bee in the United States, it is one of the world's oldest and largest academic competitions, and the Scripps final stands among the most televised youth events in American culture.

## Rules

In a standard bee, spellers take the stage in numbered order; the pronouncer reads a word (often with its definition, language of origin and use in a sentence on request) and the speller must say the word, spell it and repeat it correctly and entirely from memory. A single error eliminates the speller for that round provided it is officially ruled a misspelling; when only one speller remains and that speller is correct in a one-person, one-word round, a champion is declared, or the field can face a lightning-round spell-off to break ties. The national competition is staged in four segments (preliminaries, quarterfinals, semifinals and finals) with time controls, vocabulary questions and permitted study materials listed in official rules; spellers qualify by winning sponsored regional bees, must attend an enrolled school and meet age and grade limits (no speller may be past eighth grade or older than fifteen).

## History

Organized competitive spelling in the United States dates to 1908, when the National Education Association ran a nationwide spelling contest, and the series that became the Scripps National Spelling Bee began in 1925, when nine newspapers sent champion spellers to Washington, D.C. The E.W. Scripps Company took over sponsorship in the 1940s (with a wartime pause between 1943 and 1945) and expanded the event to more than two hundred spellers from across the United States, its territories and other countries; the first non-American winner, Jody-Anne Maxwell of Jamaica, was crowned in 1998. Over time the event formalized its word lists (from Words of the Champions through Paideia to today's 4,000-word study booklet), moved onto national television and made champions famous alongside sizable cash awards. The bee's spellers have also become cultural figures through documentary film (Spellbound, 2002), fiction (Akeelah and the Bee, 2006) and a long record of dramatic final words.
