## Basic Information

Octopush, better known as [underwater hockey](underwater-hockey.html), is a team sport played at the bottom of a swimming pool in which two teams push a weighted puck along the pool floor toward the opponents' goal, a three-meter-wide cage lying on the pool bottom. Players wear a mask, snorkel and fins and hold a short stick, and because they must hold their breath, play is fast, continuous and fully three-dimensional; the name octopush comes from the original eight players per side and the "pusher" stick. The sport is governed worldwide by CMAS (the World Underwater Federation) and is related to [diving](diving.html) and [field hockey](field-hockey.html) in its equipment and tactics.

## Rules

Two teams of six players in the water (ten per squad with rolling substitutions) compete in a pool, usually 25 m long, over two halves of 15 minutes. Each team defends a goal 3 m wide set on the bottom of the pool at each end, and scores by pushing the puck, a weighted plastic-coated lead disc of about 1.5 kg, into the opponents' goal. Players may use only their stick to move the puck, may not touch it with hands, feet or body, and may not interfere with opponents who do not have the puck; holding one's breath is part of the game, as scuba equipment is forbidden, so substitutions are made continuously on the fly. The team with the most goals after two halves wins, with a penalty shot awarded for serious fouls. The official rules are those of CMAS, which sanctions the World Championships, first held in 1980 in Canada.

## History

Underwater hockey was invented in 1954 by Alan Blake of the Southsea Sub-Aqua Club at the Guildhall Baths in Portsmouth, England, originally with eight players per side and a lead puck nicknamed the squid; the name octopush was coined from the eight "pushers" and their sticks. The game spread through British sub-aqua clubs in the 1950s and 1960s, reached CMAS in the 1960s, and the first World Championship was held in Canada in 1980, with championships held every two to three years since. The sport is played in more than 40 countries, is especially popular in Europe, and is a demonstration and growing competitive discipline at the CMAS World Games; it is sometimes confused with underwater rugby, but the two are distinct sports.
