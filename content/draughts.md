## Basic Information

Draughts (international checkers) is a two-player abstract strategy board game played on the 50 dark squares of a 10 × 10 board with 20 pieces per side; pieces move diagonally, capture by jumping, kings "fly" along the diagonals, and a player must capture the maximum number of pieces when capture options exist (FMJD, n.d.-a). It is governed worldwide by the World Draughts Federation (FMJD, Fédération Mondiale du Jeu de Dames), which organizes the World Championship, the Women's World Championship, continental championships and the FMJD rating list (FMJD, n.d.-a). The women's world title match of 2026 was played 8–17 November 2026 at the Hotel Palace in Tallinn, Estonia, where reigning champion Viktoriya Motrichko of Ukraine defended her title against Darya Tkachenko of the Netherlands, decided in a super-blitz tiebreak after drawn classical games — Motrichko's third world title in the classical format after 2023 and 2025 (ERR, 2026; FMJD, n.d.-b). At the end of 2026, the leading current players are — in the women's game — world champion Viktoriya Motrichko of Ukraine (b. 1989, Odesa), also 2026 world blitz champion, and five-time champion Darya Tkachenko, and — in the men's game — the reigning world champion listed on the FMJD champions page (FMJD, n.d.-a; ERR, 2026) — the sections below cover rules, history and more.

## Rules

International draughts is played under the FMJD Laws of the Game (FMJD, n.d.-a). The board is 10 × 10 with only the dark squares used, and each player starts with 20 men on the four rows nearest them (FMJD, n.d.-a). Men move one square diagonally forward and capture forward or backward by jumping over an opponent piece onto an empty square; when several captures are available, the player must choose the sequence that captures the maximum number of pieces (FMJD, n.d.-a). A man reaching the far rank is crowned king and may move and capture any number of squares along a diagonal ("flying" kings) (FMJD, n.d.-a). A player who cannot move loses; draws are agreed or declared under the rules (FMJD, n.d.-a). At world title matches, the classical time control is 90 minutes for the first 45 moves plus 30 minutes for the rest of the game with a 30-second increment per move, followed by rapid (20 min + 10 s), blitz (10 min + 5 s) and super-blitz tiebreaks if the match is tied (FMJD, n.d.-b; ERR, 2026). The FMJD also sanctions rapid and blitz world championships and variants such as Russian and Brazilian draughts-64, Frisian and Turkish draughts (FMJD, n.d.-a). Men's and women's championships are held separately at world and European level, with identical rules (FMJD, n.d.-b).

## History

Draughts is one of the oldest board games, with roots in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, and the 10 × 10 form of international draughts took shape in the Netherlands between 1550 and 1700 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first world championship was played in France in 1885, and since 1948 the FMJD — founded in 1947 — has organized the men's World Championship, with the women's World Championship added in 1973 (FMJD, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Icons of the women's game include Elena Mikhailovskaya, who won the first three women's world titles from 1973 to 1977, Latvia's Zoja Golubeva, record holder with the most world titles, and Russia's Darya Tkachenko, five-time world champion (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). On the men's side, the Dutch champions of the mid-twentieth century and later Soviet and Russian grandmasters such as Alexei Chizhov, a ten-time world champion, and Alexander Schwarzman shaped the modern game (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Computer draughts advanced rapidly from the 1990s, and in 2012 Alexander Schwarzman beat the program Maximus in a celebrated match (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
