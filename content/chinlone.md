## Basic Information

Chinlone (Burmese: *kanaung bein*) is the national sport of Myanmar — a non-competitive team game in which a circle of players keeps a woven rattan ball airborne using only feet, knees, chest, shoulders and head, never the hands or arms (Wikipedia, n.d.). A team of six players stands in a circle about 6.7 m (22 ft) in diameter, and the ball — roughly 12–13 cm (5 in) across, woven from rattan with twelve pentagonal holes that make it click when struck — is passed around the circle with acrobatic kicks and volleys (Wikipedia, n.d.). The tradition is about 1,500 years old, and the competitive form is judged on technique and artistry; chinlone has been contested at the Southeast Asian Games in 2013, 2017, 2023 and 2025 (SEA Games Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The spiritual center of the sport is the annual Waso Chinlone Festival at the Mahamuni Pagoda in Mandalay during the Burmese lunar month of Waso — the 98th edition ran for 45 days in 2026 — where teams from across Myanmar perform (Wikipedia, n.d.). Chinlone is played by men, women and children together; women traditionally play a solo, dance-like style called *tapandaing*, and women's teams compete at the SEA Games on the same rules as the men's (Wikipedia, n.d.). ## Rules

In the traditional game, six players form a circle and keep the ball in the air for as long as possible, with no fixed time limit — the game ends only when the ball touches the ground, and in a competitive match the team with the most controlled, artistic passes wins points from the judges (Wikipedia, n.d.). The ball may be struck with any part of the body except the hands and arms; a player who touches the ball with hands or arms loses the rally (Wikipedia, n.d.). Competitive chinlone, as played at the SEA Games and under the Myanmar Traditional Chinlone Federation's rulebook, is judged on technique, difficulty and artistry of the kicks and poses rather than on points scored — there is no goal and no tackling (SEA Games Federation, n.d.). The first official rulebook was written in 1953 by U Ah Yein of the Burma Athletic Association for the first official competition in Yangon (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men's and women's competitions use the same circle size and ball; women additionally perform the solo *tapandaing* style, a graceful individual routine with the ball, which is judged separately (Wikipedia, n.d.). Age and gender differences are thus cultural rather than structural: children, women and men all play together in the traditional form, while formal competition separates men's, women's and mixed team events (Wikipedia, n.d.). There is no world federation; the sport is governed nationally by the Myanmar Traditional Chinlone Federation and regionally appears at the SEA Games (SEA Games Federation, n.d.).

## History

Chinlone's origins are traced back roughly 1,500 years, to the Pyu city-states and early Bagan period of Myanmar, and it was described by foreign travelers for centuries as a unique Burmese pastime in which a circle of players kept a rattan ball aloft with their feet (Wikipedia, n.d.). The sport was formalized in the 20th century: the first official competition was held in Yangon in 1953 under a rulebook written by U Ah Yein of the Burma Athletic Association, and the annual Waso Chinlone Festival at the Mahamuni Pagoda in Mandalay — whose 98th edition ran for 45 days in 2026 — became the spiritual home of the game, with teams competing in marathon sessions before the pagoda's Buddha image (Wikipedia, n.d.). Chinlone entered the Southeast Asian Games program in 2013 in Myanmar, and again in 2017, 2023 and 2025, where women's teams compete alongside men's (SEA Games Federation, n.d.). Women have always played chinlone — the solo *tapandaing* style performed by women is one of the most graceful forms of the game — and female players are a visible part of every festival circle (Wikipedia, n.d.). No single founder is recorded; the shaping figures are the 1953 rulebook's author U Ah Yein and the generations of master players at Mandalay who preserved the art (Wikipedia, n.d.).
