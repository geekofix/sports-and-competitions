## Basic Information

The caber toss is the iconic event of the Scottish Highland Games: a competitor lifts a large tapered tree trunk (the caber, typically Scots pine, about 5-6 m long and 50-80 kg), runs with it, and tosses it so it turns end over end and lands upright in line with the direction of the run — a perfect "12 o'clock" toss. Highland Games heavy events are governed by associations such as the Royal Scottish Highland Games Association (RSHGA) and the Scottish Highland Games Association (SHAG) in North America.

## Rules

Under SHAG rules, the thrower stands behind the trig (throwing line), lifts the caber vertically with the thick end (butt) on the ground, then balances it against the shoulder/upper body while running a few steps and tossing it forward so the small end passes through the vertical and lands first, with the caber falling away from the thrower. A judge behind the thrower scores the landing as on an imaginary clock face: a perfect toss lands at 12 o'clock pointing directly away from the thrower. If the caber does not turn over completely, a side judge awards a degree score (up to 90 degrees). Each competitor gets up to three attempts; because every caber differs and scoring is subjective, there are no official records — only the best tosses.

## History

The caber toss dates back centuries as a test of strength by Scottish Highland warriors and woodsmen; it became a staple of Highland Games in the 19th century alongside the hammer, stone put and sheaf toss. The modern Games spread worldwide with Scottish emigration — the Caledonian Clubs of North America hold the Scottish Heavy Athletics World Championships. The caber itself became symbolic of the Games; the 2026 world champion was crowned at the 160th Scottish Highland Gathering in San Francisco.
