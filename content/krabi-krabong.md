## Basic Information

Krabi–krabong is Thailand's traditional weapon-based martial art, whose name refers to its two principal weapons: the krabi (Thai cutlass) and the krabong (staff); it is practiced alongside the unarmed art of muay boran, the ancestor of modern [muay Thai](muay-thai.html) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The weapons system includes the krabi, the daab (single-edged sword), the krabong, daab song mue (double swords), mae sok (forearm clubs, similar to the Okinawan tonfa), the ngao (bladed staff), the phlong (long staff) and the kean/lo (wood or buffalo-hide shield) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). There is no single international competition federation; the art is taught in Thai military and police academies, at universities such as Ramkhamhaeng, and by private institutes such as the Buddhaisawan Sword Fighting Institute, with graded khan (belt) systems under bodies like the International Muay Thai Federation (The Thaiger, 2023). Women train krabi–krabong in many Thai schools and institutes alongside men, and weapon demonstrations at festivals and the Armed Forces Day parade are performed by mixed groups (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Krabi–krabong has no standardized sport rulebook because it is primarily a martial art rather than a ring sport: training consists of choreographed weapon drills (ram) practiced from both leads, paired attack–defense sequences and the wai khru ram khru pre-fight ceremony, accompanied by music (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Where competitive or demonstration bouts are held — for example at Thai martial arts festivals and in some institute curricula — they use rattan or wooden training weapons, protective gear for contact bouts, and are judged on technique, control and choreography rather than points (The Thaiger, 2023). Each weapon is taught through a set of drills that can be interchanged with the others, and unarmed krabi–krabong (muay boran) adds kicks, pressure-point strikes, joint locks, holds and throws (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Grading systems award khan levels from white sash (beginner) to gold sashes (master), with the highest gold sash reserved for the King of Thailand in the Buddhai Sawan system (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Men and women follow the same syllabus; there are no women-only or men-only divisions, and both sexes train and demonstrate together (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Rule/curriculum sources: the teaching institutes and the International Muay Thai Federation, https://www.imuaythai.org/ (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## History

Krabi–krabong was developed by the warriors of the Ayutthaya Kingdom for battlefield fighting, likely alongside muay boran, and is famously associated with King Naresuan the Great, who defeated the Burmese crown prince Mingyi Swa in a celebrated elephant duel in 1593 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The French diplomat Simon de la Loubère documented both muay Thai and krabi–krabong in his account *Du Royaume de Siam* while visiting the Ayutthaya court in 1688–1689 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Many records were lost when the Burmese sacked Ayutthaya in 1767, but the arts survived through oral transmission and temple schools such as the Buddhaisawan Temple, whose modern Sword Fighting Institute was led by Sumai Mesamana until his death in 1998; his son Pramote Mesamana continued the family tradition (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The name krabi–krabong was standardized in 1936 at the Physical Education Academy, the art entered the Thai school curriculum in 1975, and the Thai Weaponry Club founded at Ramkhamhaeng University in 1971 developed the Daab Chao Ram school (The Thaiger, 2023). Legendary figures of the art include the sword fighter Phraya Phichai Dap Hak and King Naresuan (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
