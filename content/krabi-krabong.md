## Basic Information

Krabi-krabong (literally "saber and staff") is Thailand's traditional weapons-based martial art, developed by ancient Thai warriors for battlefield combat and closely related to the unarmed art muay boran. Its weapons include the krabi (curved cutlass), the daab (single-edged sword, often wielded in pairs as daab song mue), the krabong (staff or cudgel), the phlong (long pole), ngao (bladed staff), mae sok (forearm clubs) and the kean/lo shield. Practitioners train with rhythmic drills and forms, and in modern Thailand the art is part of the national physical education curriculum.

## Rules

Krabi-krabong is taught as a system of forms: each weapon is practiced through a set of drills in which attack and defense alternate, including the characteristic choreographed "six basic moves" — lower, closer, lift, lunge, plunge and lead — performed first with wooden training weapons and then with steel. Competitions and demonstrations are judged on accuracy, form and realism rather than free sparring; traditional bouts use padded or blunt weapons, and the pre-fight wai khru ritual dance is part of the performance. Unarmed krabi-krabong (muay boran) uses kicks, knee and elbow strikes, joint locks and throws, the ancestors of modern muay Thai.

## History

Krabi-krabong was developed during the Ayutthaya period (1351-1767) for Thailand's armies, influenced by Indian silambam and neighboring Southeast Asian arts; many records were lost when the Burmese sacked Ayutthaya in 1767, but the French diplomat Simon de la Loubère already described both muay Thai and krabi-krabong in his account Du Royaume de Siam (1689) after visiting in 1688. Kings Rama IV (1851-1868) and Rama V (1868-1910) promoted the art, it was first taught in a school in 1935 at the Central Physical Education School, and since 1975 it has been included in Thailand's national physical education curriculum. Today it is preserved by clubs such as the Ramkhamhaeng University Thai Weaponry Club and practiced worldwide.
