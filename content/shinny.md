## Basic Information

Shinny (also shinney, pick-up hockey, pond hockey or "outdoor puck") is the informal, unorganized form of hockey played on ice — frozen ponds, lakes, backyard rinks and community outdoor pads — without formal rules, referees, fixed positions and often without goaltenders; goals are marked by nets or simply by stones or blocks of snow (Wikipedia, n.d.). Body checking and lifting or "roofing" the puck are usually forbidden because players do not wear full protective equipment, and teams are traditionally picked by throwing all sticks into a pile at centre ice and splitting the pile in two (Wikipedia, n.d.). There is no governing body: shinny is self-governed, and in the United States the same game is usually called pick-up or pond hockey (Wikipedia, n.d.). Municipal outdoor rinks keep the tradition alive in winter — Toronto alone operates 54 mechanically cooled outdoor rinks, the most of any city in the world, with supervised shinny programs since 2011 (City of Toronto, n.d.).

Shinny is widely seen as the nursery of Canadian hockey: Doug Beardsley argues in *Country on Ice* that most Canadian hockey professionals played some form of shinny in their youth (Wikipedia, n.d.). The game is open to all ages and genders, and women’s and mixed pond-hockey leagues have grown in recent years. During the Great Depression, northern kids played with broomhandles or tree branches as sticks and a tin can, a piece of wood or even a frozen road apple as a puck — any object of about the right size served (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## Rules

Because shinny is informal, there is no single rulebook: the only near-universal customs are no body checking and no high shots without protective gear, goals at each end marked by any available object, and teams chosen by the stick toss (Wikipedia, n.d.). Players joining late are told "which way they are going" — which net to shoot at — based on skill and the score, and games can run for hours with players rotating in and out (Wikipedia, n.d.). Where pond hockey is organized into tournaments, some ice-hockey rules are adopted — the U.S. Pond Hockey Championship, for example, plays on 155-foot ovals with boards — while offsides and icing are typically dropped because they are impractical on small or irregular surfaces (Wikipedia, n.d.). Safety guidance recommends helmets and warns against playing where other people or animals are within the field of play (City of Toronto, n.d.). Men and women play under the same informal rules; there are no separate gender categories.

## History

The name derives from the Scottish game of shinty — shinny was a common name for one of shinty’s regional variations in Scotland — and Scottish and Irish immigrants carried the game to Canada and the northern United States in the 1800s, where it adapted to frozen ponds with improvised sticks and pucks (Wikipedia, n.d.). Sports historian Margaret Ann Hall also documents similar stick-and-ball games among Indigenous peoples of North America, possibly an independent origin, played mainly by women (Wikipedia, n.d.). Shinny is generally believed to be a precursor of organized ice hockey: when modern hockey was codified with rules, face-offs and referees in the late 1800s, the older, looser shinny remained in the language (Wikipedia, n.d.). The earliest photographs show boys picking teams for a game of shinny in Sarnia, Ontario, in 1908.
