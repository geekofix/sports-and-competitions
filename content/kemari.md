## Basic Information

Kemari is an ancient Japanese ball game that became popular among the court nobles of Kyoto during the Heian period (794–1185), having been introduced from China during the Asuka period (6th–8th centuries) (Nippon.com, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Eight players form a circle on a small court of about 15 square meters (roughly 6–7 m per side) and keep a deerskin ball — about 20 cm in diameter and 120–150 g in weight — aloft using only the right foot, with the hands never touching the ball; players call out "Ari," "Ya" and "Oh" as they kick (The Japan News, 2026, January 5; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Unlike modern sports there are no winners or losers: the aim is to kick the ball gracefully so that the next player can receive it, embodying harmony rather than conflict (The Mainichi, 2026, January 5). Today kemari survives as a ceremonial performance at shrines — notably the Kemari Hajime at Shimogamo Shrine in Kyoto every 4 January, which was held on 4 January 2026 with eight members of the Kemari Preservation Society (Shukiku Hozonkai) under chairman Takashi Yamamoto, as well as at Shiramine Jingū and Tanzan Shrine in Nara (The Japan News, 2026, January 5; Kokugakuin University, n.d.). Both men and women played kemari in the late Heian and Kamakura periods, and the preservation society's performers today include the tradition of graceful, cooperative play for all participants (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

In the classical game, eight players stand in a loose circle on a square court of about 6–7 m per side and pass a deerskin ball to one another with kicks of the right foot only — the left foot, hands and arms must not touch the ball (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Nippon.com, n.d.). Each kick is accompanied by a set call — "Ari," "Ya," "Oh" — and a variety of named kicks exists, including the heel kick *ushiro-mari* (The Japan News, 2026, January 5). The objective is not to score but to keep the ball in the air as long as possible and to strike it so beautifully that the next player can control it; dropping the ball ends that rally, and there is no competition or points system (The Mainichi, 2026, January 5; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Formal rules, ranks and court etiquette were established under Emperor Go-Toba (reigned 1180–1239), who was himself a master of the game (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). In the modern ceremonial form performed by the Shukiku Hozonkai preservation society, the same circle formation, right-foot kicks and calls are demonstrated at shrines, with players wearing traditional Heian-period costume (Kokugakuin University, n.d.). Men and women historically both played, and the cooperative, non-competitive format is identical for all (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). There is no international federation; the tradition is preserved by the Kemari Preservation Society and the shrines that host the rituals (Kokugakuin University, n.d.).

## History

Kemari was introduced to Japan from China during the Asuka period and rose to prominence among the aristocracy of the Heian period, spreading later from the court to the samurai class and townspeople (Nippon.com, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Fujiwara no Narimichi (1097–1169), the "sage of kemari," was famed for his heel kick *ushiro-mari*, which he reportedly used at the Kumano shrines (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The game's rules and player ranks were codified under Emperor Go-Toba in the early 13th century, and in the late Heian and Kamakura periods both men and women played (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Kemari declined after the Meiji Restoration in the 19th century; Emperor Meiji, an enthusiast of the game, donated imperial funds so that the Kemari Preservation Society (Shukiku Hozonkai) was established in 1903 (some sources give 1907) to keep the tradition alive (Nippon.com, n.d.; Kokugakuin University, n.d.). Today the society performs the Kemari Hajime at Shimogamo Shrine on 4 January each year — the 2026 ceremony featured eight members on a 15-square-meter court — as well as at Shiramine Jingū in Kyoto (14 April and 7 July) and the Kemari Festival at Tanzan Shrine in Nara (29 April and 3 November) (The Japan News, 2026, January 5; Kokugakuin University, n.d.).
