## Basic Information

Kemari (蹴鞠) is a traditional Japanese ball game from the Heian period (794-1185) in which a group of players keep a hollow deerskin ball (mari) in the air with their feet and bodies, without using their hands, for as long as possible. It is non-competitive and cooperative — closer to keepie-uppie or hacky-sack than to a match — and was refined into a ceremonial art form called kemari-do. Today it is performed as a seasonal ritual, notably at the Kemari Hajime festival at Shimogamo Shrine in Kyoto on January 4.

## Rules

A game is played by about eight players standing in a circle on a square court of roughly 15 m x 15 m marked at the four corners by a pine, cherry, willow and maple tree. Players wear Heian-period court dress (kariginu) with tall black eboshi hats and traditional boots. The ball — two pieces of deerskin sewn together, hollow, about 20 cm in diameter and weighing 100-130 g — is kept aloft with controlled kicks using any part of the body except the hands; if it touches the ground the rally ends. A referee counts the kicks aloud every ten, and graceful or difficult kicks (such as the heel kick ushiro-mari) can earn bonus; matches traditionally ended at a target score such as 100, 300 or 360 kicks, or at sunset with a final ceremonial kick.

## History

Kemari was influenced by the Chinese ball game cuju, and the earliest record is the Nihon Shoki entry for 644 describing a ball game at Asukadera played by Prince Naka-no-Oe (later Emperor Tenji) and Fujiwara no Kamatari — an event that helped trigger the Taika Reform. The word kemari is firmly attested from 701, and the game became popular among the nobility in the late Heian period; famous masters include Fujiwara no Narimichi and Nanba Yorisuke of the 12th century, and the poet-monk Saigyo. It spread to the samurai class in the Kamakura period and to townspeople in the Edo period. After declining in the Sengoku wars, it survives as a Shinto ritual at shrines such as Shimogamo and Tanzan in Kyoto, where preservation societies still play it today.
