## Basic Information

Water ski slalom is the precision discipline of water skiing: on a single ski the athlete is towed by a boat at up to 58 km/h (men) or 55 km/h (women) through a 259 m course of six buoys, weaving alternately around them on a tow rope that starts at 22 m and is shortened in steps to 10.25 m for women and 9.75 m for men as the boat speed is increased; the pass score is the number of buoys rounded, and the world records are ratified by the International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation Tournament Council (Wikipedia, n.d.; International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-d; International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-c).

The approved world records at the end of the 2026 season are 2.5 buoys at 9.75 m for Nate Smith of the USA (Covington, Louisiana, 7 September 2013) in the men's event and five buoys at 10.25 m for Regina Jaquess of the USA (Lymanland, Duncanville, Alabama, 22 September 2023, after her 4.5-buoy record of 2019) in the women's event; Jaquess (pictured at a 2025 pro event via the IWWF EMS) and Smith, a four-time world champion in 2013, 2015, 2021 and 2025, define the modern era, and the 2026 season again featured record-capable skiing at the Swiss Pro Slalom in May 2026 (International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-c; International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-e).

## Rules

The slalom course is 259 m long with six buoys spaced 11.25 m apart, offset 4.25 m from the centreline, and the boat travels at a fixed speed for each pass — up to 58 km/h for men and 55 km/h for women at elite level; the skier starts on a 22 m rope and, after each clean pass, the rope is shortened through 18.25 m, 16 m, 14.25 m, 13 m, 12 m, 11.25 m, 10.75 m, 10.25 m and (men) 9.75 m, with the boat speed raised stepwise to the maximum (International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-d). A pass ends in a fall or a missed buoy; the score is the number of buoys rounded on the best pass, and ties are broken by the fastest time (International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-d).

World records are only approved when set at a homologated event with the required number of IWWF Tournament Council judges and witnesses, video review and a certified boat and speed control; the record is expressed as buoys at a given rope length (International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-c). Slalom is also offered in junior, Over 35 and disabled divisions, and barefoot slalom — without skis — is a separate discipline (International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-d). Men and women compete in separate divisions under identical course and boat rules, differing only in maximum boat speed and final rope length (International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-b).

## History

Slalom as a competitive discipline emerged in the United States in the late 1950s — the first national slalom contests were held around 1959 — and spread internationally after the founding of the water ski federation in 1946; the first world championships in 1949 already included slalom (International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.). The sport's equipment era was defined by the shortening of the tow rope: from 22 m in the 1960s to 9.75 m for men today, a progression that turned slalom into a high-speed, high-balance event, with Cypress Gardens in Florida — home of the pioneering Evie Wolford — at the centre of the early show and slalom scene (Wikipedia, n.d.). Jon Franklin of Australia, pictured, is one of the modern slalom specialists of the AWSA circuit, and the discipline remains the gateway event of the three-event world championships (International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation, n.d.-a).
