## Basic Information

River surfing is the sport of riding waves in rivers — stationary standing waves, tidal bores or upstream waves — instead of in the ocean. It was born in Munich, Germany, where surfers have ridden the standing wave of the Eisbach, a side arm of the Isar in the Englischer Garten, since 1972, and it has since spread to cities such as Graz in Austria and Montreal in Canada. There is no single international federation; the sport is organised locally around individual waves, with Munich's Eisbach wave the world's largest urban surfing spot.

## Rules

There are no universal competition rules; river surfing is governed above all by local wave etiquette and safety practice. On a standing wave only one surfer rides at a time — party waves are unwelcome and dangerous — and surfers queue on the bank, entering the current from the side and exiting downstream after a fall. Because the wave does not move, a rider can stay on indefinitely, facing upstream while the water flows at up to several metres per second beneath the board. A thick wetsuit is essential (the Eisbach never warms above about 15 °C), and standard ocean leashes are not used on the Eisbach — they can trap a surfer underwater — so experienced riders either forgo a leash or use quick-release systems. Wave quality depends on the underlying rock or weir structure and the water level, measured in cubic metres per second, and competitions such as Munich's annual Eisbach contest judge the variety and control of tricks. Standing waves also require caution about the hydraulic jump and recirculating "keeper" hydraulic below them.

## History

River surfing claims forerunners as early as a 1955 ride on the tidal bore of the River Severn in Britain, but the birthplace of organised river surfing is Munich: from 1972 surfers rode the artificial wave on the Eisbach near the Haus der Kunst, first with tow-ropes and door-like planks, before realising that the fast-flowing water carried them without any rope at all. The scene grew through the 1970s and 1980s as an outlaw subculture — surfing was formally illegal at the Eisbach until 2010, when the city of Munich legalised it and took responsibility for the wave — while the pioneers added wooden planks underwater to stabilise and improve the wave's shape. In August 2012 Munich hosted the second European River Surfing Championships on a temporary artificial wave built next to the airport as part of the Surf and Style event. The sport globalised quickly in the 2000s: Corran Addison first surfed the Habitat 67 standing wave on Montreal's Saint Lawrence River in 2002 and founded a river surf school there, engineers built dedicated surf waves on the Mur in Graz in 2001, and purpose-built waves such as the Almwelle near Salzburg in Austria and the beginners' wave at the Floßlände in Munich have brought river surfing to inland communities far from any ocean — today roughly a thousand active surfers live in Munich alone.
