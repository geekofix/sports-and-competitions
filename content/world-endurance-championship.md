## Basic Information

The FIA World Endurance Championship (WEC) is the top level of endurance sports car racing, sanctioned by the Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile (FIA) and organised with the Automobile Club de l'Ouest (ACO): a global season of races from 6 hours to a full 24 hours, contested by Hypercar prototypes (LMH and LMDh) and LMGT3 grand tourers, with world titles for drivers and manufacturers (FIA World Endurance Championship, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The series' marquee round is the 24 Hours of Le Mans, first run in 1923 and the oldest active endurance race in the world (Wikipedia, n.d.).

The modern WEC was first run in 2012 as the successor to the Intercontinental Le Mans Cup and the revival of the World Sportscar Championship that had ended after 1992 (FIA, n.d.). At the 2025 24 Hours of Le Mans, the Ferrari 499P No. 83 of Robert Kubica, Yifei Ye and Phil Hanson won — the first overall Le Mans winners from Poland and China — and the reigning Hypercar world drivers' champions are Sébastien Buemi, Brendon Hartley and Ryo Hirakawa (Wikipedia, n.d.). On the women's side, the Iron Dames project made history at the 2023 8 Hours of Bahrain, when Sarah Bovy, Michelle Gatting and Rahel Frey became the first all-female line-up to win an FIA WEC race (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## Rules

Championship rounds are timed races — 6, 8 or 10 hours, and the 24 Hours of Le Mans — with points awarded to the top ten finishers on a sliding scale (25 for a win, roughly 1.5× at 8–10-hour races and 2× at Le Mans), and world championship titles decided by total points over the season for Hypercar drivers, manufacturers and, since 2024, LMGT3 crews and teams (FIA World Endurance Championship, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Classes compete together but score separately: purpose-built Hypercars (Le Mans Hypercar or LMDh regulations, converged with IMSA's top class from 2023) at the top, LMP2 prototypes (reserved to Le Mans from 2024) and production-based LMGT3 cars, with Balance of Performance keeping multi-manufacturer fields close (Wikipedia, n.d.). Sporting regulations govern driver stints and rest, fuel and energy allocation, safety-car and slow-zone procedures, while technical scrutineering enforces class rules; women and men drive in the same classes and line-ups under identical regulations (FIA, n.d.).

## History

Endurance racing grew out of the interwar sports car boom centred on Le Mans from 1923, joined by classics such as the Mille Miglia, Sebring and Daytona, and the FIA formalised a World Sportscar Championship in 1953 that ran until 1992 (Wikipedia, n.d.). The WEC name was used by the FIA from 1981 to 1985; after a gap, the ACO rebuilt the ecosystem through the American and European Le Mans Series, uniting them in the Intercontinental Le Mans Cup of 2010–2011, which the 2012 WEC replaced with an eight-race season featuring LMP1, LMP2, LMGTE Pro and LMGTE Am classes (FIA, n.d.). Audi, Porsche and Toyota dominated the LMP1 hybrid era — Audi's 13 Le Mans wins between 2000 and 2014 included the first diesel victory in 2006 — before the manufacturers withdrew after 2017; the Hypercar regulations launched in 2021 (LMH) with LMDh added from 2022–23 drew Ferrari, Peugeot, BMW, Porsche, Cadillac, Lamborghini, Alpine, Aston Martin and others back, and LMGT3 replaced the GTE classes from 2024, dropping LMP2 from the full season (Wikipedia, n.d.). Le Mans remains the centrepiece, and its record holder is Denmark's Tom Kristensen, who won a record nine times between 1997 and 2013 (Wikipedia, n.d.).
