## Basic Information

Gilli danda (from Sanskrit ghaṭikā, “tip-cat”) is the ancient stick-and-ball game of the Indian subcontinent and a recognized forerunner of cricket and baseball: a player balances a small wooden peg (the gilli) on a stone or in a shallow hole, flicks it into the air with a longer stick (the danda) and then strikes it as far as possible. It is played across South Asia — as dandi biyo in Nepal, danguli in Bangladesh, gulli danda in Pakistan, viti-dandu in Maharashtra, kitti-pul in Tamil Nadu and gooti-billa in Telugu — and has spread to Cambodia (kon ko), Indonesia (pathel lele), Turkey (çelik çomak), Cuba (quimbumbia) and Italy (lippa). Boys and girls, men and women play it in streets and villages; organized gilli-danda competitions are held in Pakistan and India. Within the folk, festival and novelty games on this site, its closest relatives are [Tipcat](tipcat.html), [Pithoo](pithoo.html), [Kancha](kancha.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Equipment: a danda about 42–60 cm long and a gilli — an oval, spindle-shaped piece of wood 5–13 cm long with pointed ends. The striker stands in a small circle, balances the gilli on a stone so one end is raised, flicks it up with the danda and strikes it while airborne; then runs to touch a pre-agreed point before the fielders return the gilli. If a fielder catches the gilli before it touches the ground, the striker is out; if it lands, the nearest fielder may throw it to hit the danda laid across the circle (a “run out”). Missing the gilli three times is also an out. Points are scored per successful hit and for distance; the player or team with most points wins. House rules vary by region.

## History

The game may date back more than 2,500 years — the Mauryan period (c. 322–185 BC) is often cited — and it was already played on the streets of ancient India; it is documented in South Asian literature and travel accounts for centuries. Through migration and trade it spread across Asia and beyond, giving rise to tip-cat in England, lippa in Italy, quimbumbia in Cuba and many other national variants. Today it survives as a street game across South Asia and is promoted by traditional-sports bodies such as the Traditional Sports & Games Pakistan Association.
