## Basic Information

Cuju — "kick ball" — is an ancient Chinese ball game played with the feet only, in which players propel a ball through openings in a goal or keep it aloft without using their hands; it is regarded as the earliest form of association football (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). On 15 July 2004, FIFA formally recognized Linzi in Zibo, Shandong province, as the birthplace of football at a ceremony marking the game's origins in the Warring States period state of Qi (FIFA, n.d.). Cuju was played by both men and women — women's matches are documented in imperial courts from the Tang dynasty onward — and it is the direct ancestor of the modern game covered on [Football](football.html) on this site (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Today cuju survives as part of China's intangible cultural heritage and is played in cultural demonstrations and competitions in China and among Chinese communities worldwide (FIFA, n.d.; Britannica, n.d.). As the ancient ancestor of the game, cuju stands at the start of the football family that today includes [Football](football.html), [Futsal](futsal.html), [Beach Soccer](beach-soccer.html), [Indoor Soccer](indoor-soccer.html), [Five-a-side Football](five-a-side-football.html), [Seven-a-side Football](seven-a-side-football.html), [Six-man Football](six-man-football.html), [Swamp Soccer](swamp-soccer.html), [Walking Football](walking-football.html), [Fire-ball Soccer](fire-ball-soccer.html), [Amputee Football](amputee-football.html), [Blind Football](blind-football.html), [Cerebral Palsy Football](cerebral-palsy-football.html), [Powerchair Football](powerchair-football.html), [Royal Shrovetide Football](royal-shrovetide-football.html) and [Calcio Fiorentino](calcio-fiorentino.html).

## Rules

The first written mention of cuju appears in the Zhan Guo Ce, compiled during the Warring States period (c. 475–221 BC), describing a leather ball kicked in the state of Qi (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Han dynasty (206 BC–AD 220) descriptions of the game give a rectangular court, the "ju chang", with six crescent-moon goal openings at each end; points were scored by kicking the ball through an opening, and the use of hands was forbidden (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The ball evolved from a leather shell stuffed with feathers or hair to the air-filled eight-panel bladder of the Tang dynasty (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Two forms existed: the team contest "zhuju", a precursor of modern football with goals, and the solo keep-up game "baida" (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The game was banned by the Hongwu Emperor of the Ming dynasty in the fourteenth century and declined further under the Qing, while the Japanese court variant kemari preserves a related kicking game to this day (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

Cuju originated in the state of Qi during the Warring States period and became popular in the Han dynasty, when Emperor Gaozu is said to have played it inside his palace (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women's cuju was played at the Tang court, and by the Song dynasty professional clubs such as the Qi Yun She organized competitions (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). After the Ming ban and the Qing decline, the game survived mainly as a folk and court pastime until FIFA's 2004 recognition at Zibo revived it as a symbol of football's Chinese origins (FIFA, n.d.).
