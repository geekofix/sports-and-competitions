## Basic Information

Mongolian traditional archery (sur kharvaa, “distance shooting”) is one of the three manly games of Naadam alongside wrestling and horse racing, and it is open to men and women — winners receive the titles of national marksman and national markswoman. Three regional styles are contested at Naadam: Khalkh (the most widespread, men from about 75 m, women from 60–65 m), Buriad (30–45 m, mixed) and Uriankhai (30–40 m, traditionally men only). Archers use the traditional composite bow of wood, horn and sinew and blunt arrows, and shoot at the khashaa target — a low wall of small leather cylinders that are knocked down one by one; judges beside the target sing the uukhai praise call on every hit. At the World Nomad Games, traditional archery is contested in Kyrgyz, Turkish and Hungarian styles as well as mounted (horseback) archery. Within the Central Asian nomad games on this site, its closest relatives are [Naadam](naadam.html), [Cirit](cirit.html), [Khuviin Tamir](khuviin-tamir.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Under the National Holiday Naadam rules, each man looses 40 arrows at the khashaa target from 75 m, and women loose 20 arrows from 60 m (sources variously give 60–65 m); teams of about 12 archers compete in rounds, higher-ranked archers shoot first, and archers take turns acting as judges. Hitting a red hasaa (head) cylinder of the target is a celebrated success. Buriad and Uriankhai competitions use shorter distances (30–45 m and 30–40 m) and their own target rhythms; Uriankhai remains men-only by custom. Equipment: a composite recurve bow (wood core, horn belly, sinew backing) of roughly 80–90 kg draw for men, blunt-headed arrows, and the khashaa/sur target of rolled leather cylinders filled with bark or leather.

## History

Archery has been central to Mongol life for millennia — the earliest surviving Mongolian text, the stone inscription of Yisünge (Esukhei) on the Chinggis Khan Stone, records that in 1225 the archer hit a target from 335 ald, more than half a kilometre. Friar John of Plano Carpini noted that Mongols shot from their second year on. The Secret History of the Mongols records the three games. Competitive archery was revived with the 1922 army Naadam and the 1924 Buriat sur-kharvaa games, and women began competing in the 1960s (first among the Buriats and in the 1950s in Inner Mongolia). Naadam — including its archery — was inscribed by UNESCO in 2010.
