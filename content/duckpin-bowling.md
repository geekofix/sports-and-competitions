## Basic Information

Duckpin bowling is a pin-bowling variant played mainly on the US East Coast — Virginia, Maryland, Connecticut, Rhode Island and Massachusetts — governed by the National Duckpin Bowling Congress (NDBC), established 8 September 1927 as the parent and supervising organization of the sport (National Duckpin Bowling Congress, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It uses a palm-sized ball with no finger holes and short, squat pins, giving the game its name (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Like candlepin, duckpin allows three balls per frame, but fallen pins are cleared after every ball, and no perfect 300 game has ever been bowled in sanctioned play — the smaller equipment makes strikes genuinely hard (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Women have always been part of the elite game: the Women's National Duckpin Association (WNDA) was formed in 1982 and the Ladies Professional Duckpin Tournament dates to at least 1974; the NDBC has ranked a top female bowler every season since 1928 and awards the #1 woman the same hand-blown glass National Trophy as the men (National Duckpin Bowling Congress, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The 1936 Library of Congress photograph shows two women bowling by the pins, and legends like Toots Barger — "Queen of Duckpins", thirteen times the NDBC's #1-ranked bowler — defined the women's game (Wikipedia, n.d.-c; Harris & Ewing, 1936).

## Rules

Regulation duckpin is played on the standard 60-foot lane with smaller gutters. The ball is 4+3/4 to 5 inches (12.1–12.7 cm) in diameter, weighs between 3 lb 6 oz and 3 lb 12 oz (1.53–1.70 kg), and has no finger holes — it is palmed, which limits hook and makes accuracy the whole game (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Britannica, n.d.).

Duckpins stand 9.4 inches (23.3 cm) tall with a maximum diameter of about 4.125 inches (104.8 mm) and weigh roughly 1.5 lb (0.68 kg), set in the same equilateral triangle as tenpins. A bowler gets three balls per frame; all ten pins on the first ball is a strike, on the second a spare, on the third a flat "ten" with no bonus — strike and spare bonuses are scored exactly as in tenpin, and a spare or strike in the tenth frame earns extra rolls. The theoretical maximum of 300 has never been achieved under official conditions (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

The NDBC sanctions the annual National Tournament (the 95th edition ran in May 2026) with singles, doubles, team and handicap events open to men and women; regulation play is confined to the nine congress-certified states, while "mini-duckpin" is played elsewhere (National Duckpin Bowling Congress, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

The origin is disputed: it is commonly said that duckpin began around 1900 in a Baltimore bowling, billiards and pool hall owned by future baseball Hall of Famers John McGraw and Wilbert Robinson, but research has uncovered references from the early 1890s in New Haven, Boston and Lowell, Massachusetts, including an 1892 Boston Globe mention (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

The sport became organized when the NDBC was founded on 8 September 1927; the Baltimore Evening Sun sponsored an annual tournament from 1925 to 1968. In 1953 submarine designer Ken Sherman built the first automatic duckpin pinsetter — over 1,000 moving parts — but because he refused to sell the patent to Brunswick, no new machines were manufactured after 1973, and today certified alleys (41 in 2016, down from nearly 450 in 1963) must cannibalize spare parts (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Two women stand out in the history: Toots Barger of Baltimore, ranked the NDBC's number-one bowler thirteen times and winner of the Evening Sun tournament twelve times including six straight from 1946 to 1951, and Maxine Allen, holder of more than 50 duckpin world records and a 1962 NDBC Hall of Fame inductee (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-c).
