## Basic Information

Capoeira is an Afro-Brazilian martial art, game and cultural practice in which two players (capoeiristas) move and fight inside a circle of participants (the roda) while others play the berimbau, atabaque and pandeiro and sing (UNESCO, n.d.). The two main traditions are Capoeira Regional, formalized by Mestre Bimba, and the older Capoeira Angola, preserved by Mestre Pastinha (Wikipedia, n.d.). The capoeira circle was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2014 (UNESCO, n.d.). In its competitive form, sport capoeira is standardized and governed by the World Capoeira Federation (WCF), founded in Tallinn, Estonia, in October 2011, which runs world and continental championships, a world ranking and events such as the 1st Brazil Open International Capoeira Championship 2026 and the Samsun Open 2026 (WCF, n.d.). Under WCF competition rules, senior women (18+) compete in weight classes up to 53 kg, 53–60 kg and over 60 kg, and senior men in classes up to 64 kg, 64–70 kg, 70–76 kg, 76–82 kg and over 82 kg, while children (10–13) and juniors (14–17) have age-only categories open to both sexes; each bout consists of two 90-second games (two halves of 45 seconds), with a 30-second decider if the score is tied (WCF, n.d.). Among today's leading female capoeiristas is Mestra Márcia Cigarra of ABADÁ-Capoeira — one of the top seven mestres among the organization's more than 40,000 members worldwide and the first female student of Mestre Camisa — who taught at the 17th Encontro e Jogos Femininos in Curitiba, Brazil, in May 2026 (ABADÁ-Capoeira, 2026) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b) (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

## Rules

In the traditional roda there is no formal winner: the jogo between two players is shaped by the mestres and the community, and roda etiquette — greeting at the circle and answering the berimbau — governs play (UNESCO, n.d.). Players keep the ginga (the constant swaying step) and use kicks, sweeps, takedowns, hand strikes and acrobatic floreios. In sport capoeira under the WCF Rules of Competition, only movements from the official permitted-movements list are scored; illegal movements or behavior bring a penalty or disqualification; light, controlled, non-injurious contact to the body or head (but not the face or throat) counts as an effective technique at a distance of up to 30 cm; competitors must perform inside the roda line and must heed the referee's gestures and decisions; and bouts are decided by judges' points over the two 90-second games (WCF, n.d.). Men's and women's competitions follow the same rules and scoring; the weight classes listed above apply to senior women and men respectively, while children's and junior categories (ages 10–17) are open to both sexes (WCF, n.d.).

## History

Capoeira emerged in Brazil between the 16th and 19th centuries among enslaved Africans and their descendants, above all in Bahia and Rio de Janeiro, where it was disguised as dance to survive the colonial ban codified in the 1890 Penal Code (Wikipedia, n.d.). A roda in Rio de Janeiro was already being painted in 1825 by the German artist Johann Moritz Rugendas (pictured below). In the early 20th century, Mestre Bimba — Manuel dos Reis Machado (1899–1974) — created Capoeira Regional in Salvador, Bahia, opened his academy in 1932, helped lift the ban on the art, and demonstrated capoeira for President Getúlio Vargas in 1953 (Wikipedia, n.d.). His contemporary Mestre Pastinha — Vicente Ferreira Pastinha (1889–1981) — preserved the older Capoeira Angola from 1941 onward and is pictured below. In 2014 UNESCO inscribed the capoeira circle on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (UNESCO, n.d.). The global organization ABADÁ-Capoeira was founded by Mestre Camisa in 1988 and now counts more than 40,000 members; women have become increasingly visible at the highest levels, with mestras such as Márcia Cigarra leading major international women's events today (ABADÁ-Capoeira, 2026).
