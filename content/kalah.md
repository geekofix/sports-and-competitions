## Basic Information

Kalah is the 1940 American tournament variant of mancala that became the commercially sold “Mancala” game worldwide: a board of two rows of six pits plus a store (kalah) at each end, 72 stones (six per pit). It was invented in 1940 by William Julius Champion Jr. of Minneapolis, first sold in 1944, patented in 1952 (US design patent D165634) and marketed by his Kalah Game Company (founded 1958 in Holbrook, Massachusetts); world kalah championships are held. Men and women, children and adults play; it is a staple of game cafés and schools. Within the mancala and mind games on this site, its closest relatives are [Oware](oware.html), [Mancala](mancala.html), [Bao](bao.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Players sow counterclockwise from one of their six pits, one stone per pit, including their own store but not the opponent's. If the last stone lands in your own store, you move again. If it lands in an empty pit on your own side, you capture that stone plus all stones in the opposite pit into your store. The game ends when one side is empty; the other player sweeps the remaining stones into their store, and the player with more stones wins (the original 1958 rules scored point differences over several games, match to 40 points).

## History

William Julius Champion Jr. (1908–1994) of Minneapolis devised kalah in 1940, began selling sets in 1944, patented the design in 1952 (the board in US design patent D165634 is the classic layout) and founded the Kalah Game Company in 1958; the game became a mass-market classic and is still sold worldwide as “Mancala.” Kalah is essentially a modern commercial cousin of the ancient African and Asian sowing games, and kalah-style rules appear in many traditional variants (for example ayo in Nigeria).
