## Basic Information

Greyhound racing is a speed sport in which muzzled greyhounds chase a mechanical lure (the "hare") around an oval track, with the first dog to cross the finish line winning. In Great Britain and Ireland it is regulated by the Greyhound Board of Great Britain (GBGB, which publishes the Rules of Racing) and the Irish Greyhound Board; in the United States by state racing commissions and the National Greyhound Association. Major events include the English Greyhound Derby at Towcester and the Irish Greyhound Derby.

## Rules

Under the GBGB Rules of Racing, races are run over licensed distances (typically 280-520 m in the UK) with up to six or eight dogs starting from numbered traps that open simultaneously; the mechanical lure runs on a rail about 30 cm ahead of the dogs, and the first greyhound to cross the finish line (judged by photo finish) wins. Dogs wear a racing muzzle and a numbered jacket; doping control, welfare regulations and licensing of trainers and kennels are enforced by the GBGB. Betting is offered on win, each-way, forecasts and tricast markets. Races are graded (A1-A11, S1) or open (feature races like the Derby). In the US, racing is regulated state by state and is now permitted in only a small number of states.

## History

Greyhound racing began in the United Kingdom in 1926-27, when Owen Patrick Smith and the Greyhound Racing Association introduced the mechanical lure and oval track in Manchester (a meet at Belle Vue) and London (White City), inspired by American "coursing with mechanical lure" experiments from the 1910s-20s. The English Greyhound Derby was first run in 1927 (won by Mick the Miller, who became a national celebrity), and the Irish Derby dates to 1932. The sport peaked in mid-20th-century Britain with dozens of tracks; many closed after 2000 amid welfare concerns, and the industry is now consolidated (e.g., Entain-owned tracks). Ireland remains a stronghold (Curraheen Park, Dublin). Greyhound racing was phased out in most US states after 2018-2021, with only a small number of states still permitting it under state regulation.
