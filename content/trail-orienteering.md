## Basic Information

Trail orienteering (TrailO) is the discipline of orienteering that tests map-reading judgment rather than speed: competitors move along a marked, accessible course and, at each control point along the trail, must decide which of several visible flags matches a position marked on the map (or whether none does). Developed to be fully inclusive (it is contested by athletes with and without physical disabilities, including wheelchair users), it is part of the international orienteering federation's championship programme.

## Rules

At each control site on the course, several flags hang visible from the observation point, and the competitor studies the map and terrain to choose which flag lies in the correct position (or the answer none of them): answers are recorded without time pressure at most controls (accuracy, not pace, decides the standings), with a small number of timed controls breaking ties. Elite course (E) and paralympic-open categories run the same challenges (the paralympic class competed at the Paralympic games in the past), and organization rules govern course lengths, the number of controls, fairness of flag placements and the eligibility classes that keep the sport accessible.

## History

Trail orienteering was formalized in the 1990s as an orienteering discipline emphasizing pure map interpretation and removing running speed from the equation: the format proved ideally accessible for wheelchair users, and with its roots in European orienteering culture it joined the world championship programme early in the 2000s. World championships have been staged alongside the classic orienteering championships ever since, and the discipline has grown as an inclusion showcase for the sport: at its best, the world's able-bodied elite and para athletes compete on identical terms in the same forests.
