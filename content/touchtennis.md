## Basic Information

Touchtennis is the miniaturized version of tennis: played on a compact court roughly a quarter the size of a full court with short rackets, foam-based lower-bouncing balls and a lower net, it preserves tennis's strokes and tactics while fitting into driveways, gardens and play areas. Invented in London as a garden game, it has grown into an organized sport with standardized court dimensions, national tournaments and a world tour.

## Rules

Courts are laid out to a standardized touchtennis specification (in metres roughly 8 by 4 for singles), nets are lower than a tennis net, and rackets are smaller and lighter than tennis frames, while the ball is a modified foam-based construction that bounces lower and slower, defining the sport's touch-oriented game. Scoring follows tennis: points as 15, 30, 40, game, sets with a tiebreak at 6-6, abbreviated in some tournament formats; serves may be underarm or overarm in social play, and code rules govern the court markings, ball specs and player conduct for ranked competition. The shortened dimensions disguise a tactical game: rallies are longer and placement beats power, so the sport is as much about angles and patience as its big sibling.

## History

Touchtennis began in London in the early 2000s, traditionally credited to a group of friends' garden experiment in the Wimbledon area that formalized into regular play and dedicated equipment; tournaments began in the 2010s across the United Kingdom and abroad, with a standardized court and ruleset, a world ranking and a World Tour circuit that has staged events in Europe and beyond. The sport has found a niche with tennis players seeking gentler hours, families and older players (the smaller court eases movement demands), and has been promoted as a genuine standalone sport rather than a drill, with national associations and championships now part of its structure.
