## Basic Information

Street luge (also land luge or road luge) is the gravity sport of riding a stretched, sled-like luge board feet-first down paved roads, steering by leaning and braking with the soles of the shoes. A cousin of downhill skateboarding contested at speeds above 150 km/h, it moved from Californian hills to the X Games and now races under international gravity-sports federations.

## Rules

Riders lie supine on an aluminium or steel-frame board equipped with hard wheels, trucks and padding, steering by shifting body weight and swinging the rear of the board, and braking with special sole pads or skate shoes pressed to the road; full leather suits, motorcycle-style helmets and gloves are mandatory. Races are timed downhill sprints or side-by-side heats of four to six riders on closed mountain roads, with a push or roll start and disqualifications for leaving the racing line into another rider. Equipment rules govern board dimensions (limited length and width, no fairings over the rider in standard classes), wheel and truck specifications, and safety gear, with classes for classic luge (simple steel boards, no suspension) and modern streamlined designs.

## History

Street luge grew out of southern California skateboard culture of the 1970s, when riders first lay down on their boards to bomb hills; the Signal Hill Speed Runs of 1975 to 1978 made prone skateboarding famous, with Sam Puccio recording timed runs near 90 km/h, and pioneers such as Roger Hickey pushed record speeds and purpose-built luge designs ever further through the 1980s. The sport's breakthrough came in the mid-1990s, when the ESPN X Games showcased street luge to a worldwide audience from 1997 to 2001 under the International Gravity Sports Association (IGSA), and NBC's Gravity Games followed under Extreme Downhill International. The sport's modern governance is shared between the IGSA and the International Downhill Federation (IDF), which sanction world cup-style events in Europe, North America, South America and Australia, where top-speed venues such as Quebec's L'Ultime Descente have produced certified runs beyond 160 km/h.
