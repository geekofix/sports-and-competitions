## Basic Information

Newcomb ball (also called Newcomb, Mamanet or catchball) is a team ball game played on a court divided by a net or rope, in which the ball is caught and then thrown — never hit or batted — over to the opposing side (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It was invented in 1895 by Clara Gregory Baer, a physical education instructor at Sophie Newcomb College of Tulane University in New Orleans, and is one of the few sports created by women for women; it was the second team sport played by women in the United States after basketball (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Newcomb rivaled [volleyball](volleyball.html) in popularity through the 1920s and may have influenced volleyball's development (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Today the game survives as a teaching game for volleyball fundamentals, as a cooperative game, and as Mamanet, which has its own world federation and world championships played by women and men (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Two teams of six to nine players line up on opposite sides of a net or rope, with a neutral "base" area behind the centre line that players may not enter (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The objective is to throw the ball into the opponents' area so that it hits the ground without being caught — originally called a "touch-down" and worth a point; the receiving team catches the ball and throws it back from the spot where it was caught, and every player throws rather than hits (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Court size, net height and team size varied historically by gender, age and available space, so women's and men's games could use different court dimensions (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In the modern competitive form Mamanet, teams of six play on a court 12 m by 6 m per side with a 1.6 m net, the ball may be caught and thrown (one or two hands) but not carried, and matches are played as best-of-three sets to 25 points with a two-point lead (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).


## History

Clara Gregory Baer devised Newcomb in 1895 while waiting for basketball baskets to arrive at Newcomb College, designing a game women could play in long skirts without the physical contact of basketball (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The game spread through women's colleges and the YMCA in the United States and to England and the Commonwealth in the early 20th century, where it became a staple of women's physical education; it was played at the 1900s-1920s alongside basketball and volleyball before volleyball's hitting rules won out (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In Israel, the catchball variant Mamanet was formalized in the 1960s-1970s and grew into an organized competitive sport with a world federation, world championships and hundreds of thousands of recreational players (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
