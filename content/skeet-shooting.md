## Basic Information

Skeet shooting is a shotgun discipline in which athletes fire at clay targets launched from two fixed houses — the high house and the low house — on a semicircular field of eight stations; it has been an Olympic event since Mexico City 1968 for men and since Sydney 2000 for women, with a mixed team event on the programme since Tokyo 2020, and the world governing body is the International Shooting Sport Federation (ISSF) (ISSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). At Paris 2024, held at the Châteauroux National Shooting Centre from 2 to 4 August 2024, Vincent Hancock of the United States won a historic fourth individual Olympic gold in men's skeet, beating teammate Conner Prince 58–57 in the final, while Chile's Francisca Crovetto Chadid won the women's gold over Britain's Amber Rutter in a shoot-off, with America's Austen Smith taking bronze (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Olympics, n.d.).

Among today's elite, the women's event is led by Chile's Francisca Crovetto Chadid — Olympic champion at Paris 2024 and the first Chilean woman to win an Olympic shooting gold — and by Britain's Amber Rutter, the 2024 silver medallist and a multiple ISSF World Cup medallist, while the men's event is headed by Vincent Hancock of the United States, who at Paris 2024 became only the sixth athlete in history to win the same individual Olympic event four times, with the next American generation, Conner Prince and Austen Smith, right behind him (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; ISSF, n.d.). Men and women shoot identical qualification and final formats; there are no gender-specific rule differences, and the mixed team event pairs one man and one woman per nation (ISSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Skeet is governed by the ISSF (https://www.issf-sports.org/), whose shotgun regulations define the field: eight stations arranged in a semicircle between the high house (station 1) and the low house (station 7), plus a midway station 8; two throwing machines release clay targets in a fixed sequence of singles and doubles, and in Olympic skeet the shooter calls for the target with the gun mounted at the hip and may receive the clay immediately or up to three seconds later (ISSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). In Olympic qualification each shooter fires 125 targets — five series of 25 — spread over two days, and the top six advance to the final; the final is shot over up to 60 targets with progressive eliminations after each stage until the gold and silver medals are decided, with shoot-offs breaking ties for places (ISSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Ammunition is limited to 12-gauge or smaller shells with a maximum shot charge of 24 grams; a broken target scores and a miss does not, and a shooter who fails to fire at a called target is penalized (ISSF, n.d.). Men and women compete in separate individual events under identical rules, and since Tokyo 2020 a mixed team skeet event has been on the programme — at Paris 2024 it replaced the mixed team trap event (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; ISSF, n.d.).

## History

Modern skeet shooting was formalized in the United States in the 1920s — the National Skeet Shooting Association (NSSA) was founded in 1925 and published standard rules in 1926 — and the name derives from the Norwegian word *skyte*, "to shoot"; the discipline spread internationally and was added to the Olympic programme at Mexico City 1968 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The women's Olympic event debuted at Sydney 2000, won by Azerbaijan's Zemfira Meftahatdinova (born 1963), who had already won the mixed event at Barcelona 1992, when women competed together with men in the last mixed Olympic shooting final until the mixed team format returned in 2020 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Among the icons of the men's game is Italy's Ennio Falco (born 1968), Olympic champion at Athens 2004 and a multiple world champion who retired after the London 2012 Games, and on the women's side Italy's Diana Bacosi (born 1983), Olympic champion at Rio 2016 and world champion in 2018 and 2019, who retired in 2022 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). A major rules reform in 2005 cut the Olympic qualification from 150 to 125 targets, ending the era of perfect 150 scores such as Mykola Milchev's Olympic record at Sydney 2000 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
