## Basic Information

Seega (seejeh, seeza, siga, sidjah) is an ancient Egyptian two-phase placement-and-capture game played on square boards of 5×5, 7×7 or 9×9 cells (khamsáwee, seb'áwee and tis'áwee), each player commanding 12, 24 or 40 pieces. In the modern game it is still played in Egypt and by Bedouin, often in holes dug in the sand with pebbles; men and women play together. Its boards and scenes are among the standard entertainments depicted in Egyptian tombs. Within the mind games on this site, its closest relatives are [Latrones](latrones.html), [Dash Guti](dash-guti.html), [Morabaraba](morabaraba.html), [Draughts](draughts.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

The board starts empty. Phase one (positioning): players alternately place two of their own pieces on any empty cells, never the center cell, until all pieces are placed. Phase two (movement): pieces move one cell horizontally or vertically (never diagonally, never jumping); the first move of the game must be a piece moving into the center cell. A piece is captured when a move bounds an opponent piece between two of the mover's pieces horizontally or vertically (custodial capture); placing a piece between two enemies captures nothing. After a capture the player moves again if another capture is possible. A player who cannot move loses (variants allow passing or force the opponent to free a move); some versions end the game when one side is reduced to one piece.

## History

Edward William Lane described seega in An Account of the Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians (1st ed. 1836), including the predetermined first placements on the 5×5 board; H. Carrington Bolton saw Egyptians and Bedouin playing it in sand holes near Mount Sinai and published an account in the Journal of American Folklore in 1890. The game is ancient — board-game scenes in Egyptian tombs (such as the lintel of Ankh-ef-en-sekhmet) show game players — and seega is often compared with the Roman ludus latrunculorum, to which it may be related. A commercial 7×7 variant, Seejeh, was marketed in 1975.
