## Basic Information

Surfing is the sport of riding ocean waves on a board: the surfer paddles into a breaking wave, stands and rides along its face, using balance, wave-reading and board control. From its Hawaiian roots to a global culture with a professional world tour and an Olympic debut at Tokyo 2020, it is one of the world's most popular and photographed board sports.

## Rules

Competitive surfing is judged: a heat of two to four surfers takes turns catching waves, and a panel scores each ride from 0.1 to 10 on committed maneuvers, degree of difficulty, combinations and speed, power and flow "+E+" the best waves counting to a heat total. Surfing etiquette (priority rules) governs who has the right of way on a wave and penalizes interference; contest formats include rounds of seeding heats, repercharge rounds and finals, and the World Surf League runs the Championship Tour on the world's best breaks. Equipment spans shortboards for high-performance maneuvers, longboards for trim and style, and the modern subdivisions of big-wave, foiling and stand-up paddle surfing, all scored under federation rules standardized for the Olympic Games by the International Surfing Association and WSL.

## History

Surfing descends from the Hawaiian wave-riding tradition, where riding waves on shaped wooden boards was embedded in island culture, witnessed by European visitors in the late eighteenth century. The revival of wave-riding began in the early twentieth century in Hawaii and California: Duke Kahanamoku, the Hawaiian Olympic swimmer, demonstrated surfing worldwide, and the 1950s-60s saw shorter foam-and-fiberglass boards produce a style and counterculture boom. Professional surfing organized in the 1970s and matured into the world tour of today's World Surf League, while big-wave spots like Waimea Bay, Mavericks and Nazare pushed the boundaries of the rideable; the sport returned to its roots on the world stage with its Olympic debut at Tokyo 2020 (won by the Hawaiians and Japan's stars), and its culture reaches from local shapers to a global industry of millions of recreational surfers.
