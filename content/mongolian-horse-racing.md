## Basic Information

Mongolian horse racing (morin khur, “horse race”) is the second of Naadam's three manly games and a cross-country endurance race quite unlike Western track racing: races run 15–30 km across open steppe, with the distance set by the horse's age class (about 15–16 km for two-year-old daaga, up to 27–30 km for the oldest classes), and up to about 1,000 horses may start together at Khui Doloon Khudag. Riders are child jockeys, usually aged 5–13, chosen for their light weight and fearlessness; both boys and girls ride. The top five horses in each class earn the title airgiyn tav, the winning jockey is hailed as tumny ekh (“leader of ten thousand”), gold, silver and bronze medals go to the top three, and even the last horse (bayan khodood) is honored with a song. Crossbred horses race separately (13 July). Within the Central Asian nomad games on this site, its closest relatives are [Naadam](naadam.html), [Kunan Chabysh](kunan-chabysh.html), [Zhorgo Salysh](zhorgo-salysh.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Six age-class races are held at the national Naadam (daaga two-year-olds through the oldest azarga/ikh nas classes), each over its own 15–30 km course starting and finishing at Khui Doloon Khudag; local Naadams run the same classes over shorter schedules. Before the races the audience sings traditional songs and the jockeys sing the gingo song. Only Mongolian-breed horses race together (crossbreds in a separate race), the races are timed, and prizes and the airgiyn tav titles are awarded to the top five. Races are also held at Tsagaan Sar (Lunar New Year), in spring and at the Ikh Hurd.

## History

Mongol horsemanship and racing go back over two millennia — Chinese sources record Naadam-like contests of the Xiongnu in 209 BC, and the Secret History of the Mongols records the three games. The breed is purported to be little changed since Chinggis Khan's time, and nomads still keep more than 3 million horses. The first recorded Naadam in 1639 saw Bonkhor Donir's horse win the racing. Mongolian races test the horse's endurance above all: foreign breeds are usually faster but exhausted at the finish, while Mongolian horses still have wind — though horses have occasionally died of exhaustion, which is why welfare rules and veterinary checks now apply at the national race.
