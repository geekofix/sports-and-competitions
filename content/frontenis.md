## Basic Information

Frontenis is a racket sport of the Basque pelota family, played with a tennis-style racket and a pressurized rubber ball on a 30 m x 10 m x 10 m court (frontón), in pairs (doubles) for international competition. It was created in Mexico around 1900 and is one of the official specialties of the International Federation of Basque Pelota (FIPV), which runs the Basque Pelota World Championships including frontenis.

## Rules

In Olympic frontenis (the only FIPV-recognized modality, using a rubber "racquet ball"), pairs play on a 30 m court: every served or returned ball must strike the front wall (frontis) before bouncing on the floor, and the opposing pair must hit it back before its second bounce. Points are scored when a side fails to return the ball, hits it out of the court (the lower 60 cm of the front wall and areas above the coping are out), or commits a fault. Matches at the World Championships are played in sets to 15 points, best of five. The serve must land in the designated service area; a "blackguard" (serve hitting the black line on the front wall) is a let. Pre-Olympic frontenis (a tennis-like ball) is played in Spain and France with European club championships but is not FIPV-official.

## History

Frontenis was created around 1900 in Mexico by tennis players (among them Buttlin, Sharp, Crowle, Pérez Verdia and Maldonado) who hit a tennis ball with rackets against the wall of Fernando Torreblanca's house in Mexico City; the name comes from "frontón" (pelota court) and "tenis" (tennis), shortened to "frontenis." It spread through Latin America and reached Spain via the Canary Islands in the 1940s, then the rest of Europe in the 1960s-70s. The first World Championship in frontenis was held in Donostia-San Sebastián, Spain, in 1952. Mexico remains the dominant nation (José "El Poison" Becerra revolutionized the racket in the mid-20th century), with Spain, France, Argentina and the US also strong. Olympic frontenis was a demonstration sport at the 1968 Mexico City and 1992 Barcelona Olympics.
