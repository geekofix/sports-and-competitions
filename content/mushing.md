## Basic Information

Mushing is the sport and transport method in which one or more dogs pull a sled (on snow) or a wheeled rig (dryland) with a driver, the musher. Competitive sled dog racing is governed worldwide by the International Federation of Sleddog Sports (IFSS), which runs on-snow and dryland world championships. The most famous races are the Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race from Anchorage to Nome, Alaska, and the Yukon Quest between Canada and Alaska. Related disciplines include [skijoring](skijoring.html), canicross, bikejoring and dog scootering.

## Rules

In IFSS races, classes are divided by team size and distance: sprint (up to 8 dogs, unlimited), middle distance (6 or 12 dogs) and long distance (8 dogs or unlimited), plus dryland classes such as canicross, bikejoring, scooter and wheel rigs. Each dog must be vaccinated and identified, dog abuse is prohibited and punishable by disqualification, and veterinarians check teams at checkpoints. In the Iditarod, a musher starts with a maximum of 16 dogs and must finish with at least 5 on the towline; three mandatory rests are required, including one 24-hour layover, and mandatory gear such as a sleeping bag, axe and eight booties per dog must be carried. The first team to the finish wins.

## History

Sled dogs have been used for transport in the Arctic for thousands of years, and competitive racing grew out of gold rushes and mail delivery routes in Alaska and Canada in the late 19th and early 20th centuries; the famous 1925 serum run to Nome, when dog teams relayed diphtheria antitoxin, made mushing internationally known. The Iditarod was first run in 1973, the Yukon Quest in 1984, and the IFSS was founded in 1985 to unify national sled dog associations; its on-snow World Championships have been held since 1990, starting in St. Moritz, Switzerland. Legendary mushers include Rick Swenson, Joe Redington Sr., Jeff King, Martin Buser, Robert Sorlie and Lance Mackey, who won both the Iditarod and the Yukon Quest in the same year.
