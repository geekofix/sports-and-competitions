## Basic Information

Sidecar road racing is motorcycle road racing's two-up discipline, in which a driver and a passenger race a three-wheeled motorcycle-and-sidecar outfit around closed road and circuit courses, the passenger moving dynamically around the rear platform to balance the machine through corners. It is the only surviving original FIM road racing class from the founding world championship of 1949, and its spiritual home remains Britain's road circuits — above all the Isle of Man TT — alongside a European circuit championship.

## Rules

An outfit is a special chassis with the sidecar wheel offset to one side (nearside in Britain, offside in much of continental Europe), low-slung and streamline-faired, with the driver kneeling in front of the engine — a layout sometimes called the "kneeler" — and the passenger crouched on a rear platform, using their body as ballast. Under FIM regulations "rider" applies equally to driver and passenger: the two work as a team, the passenger shifting weight from side to side in corners, forward for front-wheel grip and backward for traction, and even helping the driver drift. Traditional Formula Two sidecars (600 cc engines) are the common national racing class in the United Kingdom, competing in road racing events such as the Isle of Man TT, while the FIM Sidecar World Championship classes race larger engines at circuits across Europe. Races are mass-start, and the slipstreaming, three-abreast spectacle is decided by overall race time.

## History

Sidecar racing began in the 1920s\u20131930s with passengers riding civilian-style on sidecars attached to solo machines, outfits famously described as "scaffolding on wheels"; the FIM world championship for sidecars was part of the inaugural Grand Prix world championship in 1949, dominated in its early decades by Eric Oliver, a pioneer of streamlining who took the first four titles, and later by British, Italian and German greats. As the outfits evolved into low, long, specially-built racing chassis with the passenger lying flat behind the driver, the F1-style European scene grew, and the class continued even as the more glamorous solo classes evolved; the championship ran under the Superside banner from 2001 and returned to FIM management in 2010 as the FIM Sidecar World Championship. In Britain, Formula Two sidecar racing remained a fixture of the short-circuit and pure road-racing calendars, with the Sidecar TT at the Isle of Man — won a record fourteen times by Dave Molyneux — the discipline's most coveted prize; the sport retains devoted followings in Britain, France, Germany, the Netherlands, Hungary and the Czech Republic.
