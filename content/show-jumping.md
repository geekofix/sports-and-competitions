## Basic Information

Show jumping is an equestrian discipline in which horse-and-rider pairs negotiate a course of fences, walls, water jumps and combinations inside an arena, with the winner determined by penalties and time. Alongside dressage and eventing it is one of the three Olympic equestrian disciplines, governed internationally by the F\u00e9d\u00e9ration Equestre Internationale (FEI), and its prestige events \u2014 the Olympic Games, the FEI World Championships and the Nations Cup \u2014 crown the best partnerships in world horse sport.

## Rules

A course consists of around 10\u201313 numbered obstacles \u2014 at championship level up to roughly 1.60 m high \u2014 including uprights, spreads, triple bars, combinations (double and triple) and a water jump, all of which must be jumped in order within the time allowed. Penalties are scored at 4 faults for a knockdown (when any part of an obstacle is displaced through the fault of the horse or rider) and at 4 faults for a first refusal or run-out; a second refusal at the same obstacle means elimination, as do falls of horse or rider, error of course and exceeding the time limit \u2014 while exceeding the time allowed adds one time fault per commenced second. The winner is the combination with the fewest penalties, and in most competitions riders equal on zero penalties return for a jump-off against the clock over a shortened course \u2014 where the decisive factors become faults and raw speed. In the Olympic team competition, placing is decided over two rounds, with the best three of four team scores counting.

## History

Jumping came to horse sport after the English Enclosure Acts \u2014 riders who once galloped freely across open country suddenly faced fenced fields to jump \u2014 and the early contest form called "lepping" spread through Irish and British shows in the nineteenth century. The first major show jumping competition in England took place in 1907 at Olympia, and the sport entered the Olympics at Paris 1900 with a single jumping competition before reappearing as a full standard at Stockholm 1912, when the modern format was codified \u2014 15 jumps of up to 1.4 m with a 13-foot water jump, scored on faults. The British Show Jumping Association (1923) and other national bodies standardised rules nationally, while the FEI, founded in 1921, unified the international format and today governs the Olympic and championship programme. Women have ridden on equal terms with men since Helsinki 1952; the sport's great names \u2014 from Hans G\u00fcnter Winkler and his legendary Halla to later champions such as Nick Skelton, Ludger Beerbaum, Steve Guerdat and Scott Brash \u2014 are the product of the Nations Cup, World Equestrian Games and the global Longines FEI Jumping World Cup circuit.
