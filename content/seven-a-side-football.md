## Basic Information

Seven-a-side football is the reduced-team form of association football played with seven players per side on a smaller pitch — common in youth and veterans' leagues and, under modified laws for athletes with cerebral palsy, staged as Paralympic football 7-a-side from 1984 to 2016 under the International Federation of Cerebral Palsy Football (IFCPF) (IFCPF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). At the Rio 2016 Paralympics, Ukraine won the gold medal 2–1 after extra time against Iran, and hosts Brazil took bronze with a 3–1 win over the Netherlands at Deodoro Stadium (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Today the organised elite form of the seven-a-side game is CP football, run by the IFCPF with biennial World Cup and World Championships plus a Women's World Cup since 2022, and the 2026 IFCPF World Cup is staged in the United States in October 2026 (IFCPF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-c). Seven-a-side sits between the 11-a-side [Football](football.html) and the five-a-side formats [Five-a-side Football](five-a-side-football.html), [Futsal](futsal.html) and [Blind Football](blind-football.html), and shares the reduced-team idea with [Six-man Football](six-man-football.html); the wider football family is listed under Important Links.

Among the current leading teams, the women's game is headed by Australia's ParaMatildas, champions of the 2024 IFCPF Women's World Cup, ahead of the United States, winners of the inaugural 2022 edition in Salou, Spain, and Ireland, who finished third in 2024; on the men's side Ireland were ranked eighth in the IFCPF world rankings in February 2026 and the 2026 World Cup is being played in the United States (Wikipedia, n.d.-c; IFCPF, n.d.).

## Rules

CP football 7-a-side applies the Laws of the Game with specific modifications under IFCPF rules: seven players per side including the goalkeeper on a reduced pitch of at most about 75 m × 56 m with 5 m × 2 m goals, no offside, two 30-minute halves, and one-handed throw-ins permitted to accommodate impaired arm function (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; IFCPF, n.d.). Players are classified FT1 to FT8 according to their level of impairment from cerebral palsy, stroke or traumatic brain injury: at least one FT1 or FT2 player (formerly C5/C6) must be on the field at all times and no more than two FT8 (formerly C8) players may play simultaneously; FT1 denotes the most severe impairment (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In ordinary youth and veterans' seven-a-side football, leagues typically use seven-a-side teams, smaller goals and often no offside, with match length scaled to age group (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women's CP football follows identical laws and classifications, and the IFCPF Women's World Cup has been held since 2022 (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). Football 7-a-side was on the Paralympic programme from 1984 (wheelchair and standing events in 1984, standing/CP only from 1988) until Rio 2016 and was dropped from Tokyo 2020 onwards; a bid to reinstate it for Paris 2024 was rejected citing insufficient development of the women's game (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

The first international CP football match was played at the 1978 Cerebral Palsy Games in Edinburgh, Scotland, and the first World Championship followed in 1982 in Denmark, won by Ireland (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The sport debuted at the Paralympics in 1984 and ran through Rio 2016, where Ukraine claimed a third gold; the Netherlands won three consecutive standing titles (1988 Seoul, 1992 Barcelona, 1996 Atlanta) with a golden generation including Carlo Dengerink, Jaap de Vries and Paul Heersink, each a three-time gold medallist, while Ukraine's Volodymyr Antoniuk won gold in 2004 and 2008 and Russia took the titles in 2000 and 2012 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Women never competed in Paralympic 7-a-side, which contributed to the sport's removal after 2016; the IFCPF answered by launching a Women's World Cup in 2022 in Salou, Spain, won by the United States, followed by the 2024 edition won by Australia (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-c).
