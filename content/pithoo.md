## Basic Information

Pithoo (pittu, pithu garam, gol garam — “pitcher and stones”) is the Pakistani and North Indian name for the seven-stones game: one team knocks over a pile of seven flat stones with a ball while the other team tries to hit them before the pile is rebuilt. It is played by boys and girls across Pakistan and the Punjab, and organized pithoo matches are held in schools and clubs. Within the folk, festival and novelty games on this site, its closest relatives are [Seven Stones](seven-stones.html), [Gilli Danda](gilli-danda.html), [Maram Pitti](maram-pitti.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Seven flat stones are stacked in a pile at the centre of the playing area. A seeker (attacker) throws a ball from behind a marked line to scatter the pile — three throws are allowed; if the ball is caught by a hitter after one bounce, the thrower is out. The seekers then try to rebuild the pile while the hitters (defenders) throw the ball to hit them; a seeker hit by the ball is out. Points go to the seekers for rebuilding the pile (announced with the game's name) and to the hitters for each seeker eliminated. The round ends when the pile is rebuilt or all seekers are out; teams then swap roles.

## History

Seven-stones games are among the most ancient games of the subcontinent — the Bhagavata Purana describes Krishna playing the game with his friends — and the same game is known under dozens of names across the region (lagori, sitoliya, satodiyu, dabba kali, ezhu kallu, haft sang in Iran, sat chara in Bangladesh, game ball in Nepal). The British spread it to Africa, the Caribbean and Southeast Asia, and it is now played in more than 30 countries. Modern organization includes the International Lagori Federation's rules (12 players per team, six on court, three-minute sets) and Lagori World Cups since 2015.
