## Basic Information

Kushti — known internationally as pehlwani — is the traditional form of wrestling of the Indian subcontinent, contested in a mud pit (kushti kakad) by pehlwans wearing only a langot (loincloth), where the aim is to throw the opponent so that both shoulders touch the ground (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It developed during the Mughal era from the older Indian malla-yuddha and the Persian kushti, and is today organized in India by the Wrestling Federation of India, which runs dangal tournaments and national championships (Wrestling Federation of India, n.d.-a). While traditional akharas (wrestling schools) have historically been male-only, women's wrestling in India is strong at the international level in the freestyle and (since 2023) the Greco-Roman discipline — see [freestyle wrestling](freestyle-wrestling.html) — and women's kushti is increasingly practiced in modern Indian wrestling academies (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

Kushti is fought in a circular mud pit of clay mixed with oil, ghee and turmeric, about 10–14 m across, prepared in an akhara; competitors wear the langot and no shoes (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Strikes, kicks and joint locks are forbidden — the contest is a pure throwing and grappling match in which the first pehlwan to force the opponent down so that both shoulder blades touch the pit is the winner (Wrestling Federation of India, n.d.-a). Bouts at dangal tournaments are usually elimination or best-of format with a time limit, and professional dangals award prizes for wins; there are weight categories in organized competition, and men's and women's events are run separately (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Etiquette rules are strict: wrestlers bow to the akhara and their guru before and after the bout, and the referee (ustad) controls the start and the end of each bout (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Rule source: Wrestling Federation of India, https://wrestlingfederationofindia.org/ (Wrestling Federation of India, n.d.-a). See also [wrestling](wrestling.html) and [kabaddi](kabaddi.html) on this site.

## History

Wrestling in India dates back millennia — malla-yuddha is described in ancient texts — and kushti as a distinct style emerged in the 16th–17th centuries under Mughal rule, blending Indian and Persian traditions at imperial courts such as that of Akbar (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The sport reached its golden age in the 19th and early 20th centuries, when pehlwans like the Great Gama (Ghulam Muhammad, 1878–1963), the "Rustam-e-Zamana" who remained undefeated for more than 50 years, became national heroes, alongside later icons Dara Singh (1928–2012) and Kartar Singh (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). During the colonial era and after independence, akharas across Punjab, Haryana and Maharashtra kept the tradition alive, and the Wrestling Federation of India was set up to organize modern dangal competition (Wrestling Federation of India, n.d.-a).
