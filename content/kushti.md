## Basic Information

Kushti (also called pehlwani) is the traditional wrestling of the Indian subcontinent, practiced in Pakistan, India and Bangladesh. Wrestlers, called pehlwans, train and live together in a traditional gymnasium called an akhara — a dug pit filled with soft clay soil mixed with oil and turmeric — wear a loincloth (langot), and win a bout by forcing the opponent's back to the ground. Tournaments are known as dangals, and the system influenced catch wrestling and, through the Mughal era, evolved from the ancient malla-yuddha described in the Mahabharata.

## Rules

Traditional kushti is a no-points, pin-fall sport: two pehlwans grapple barefoot in the akhara until one wrestler's back touches the ground; there are no strikes, kicks or time limits in the traditional format. Wrestlers may use locks, throws, pins and submission holds, and bouts are officiated by the guru and elders of the akhara. Wrestlers follow strict codes of conduct and diet, train with traditional exercises such as dands and baithaks (Hindu squats), maces (gada) and clubs, and owe loyalty to their akhara. Village dangals award cash prizes and the winner parades around the arena with the loser following.

## History

Wrestling in South Asia dates to the ancient malla-yuddha, described in the Mahabharata (Bhima, Karna, Jarasandha) and codified in the 13th-century Malla Purana. During the Mughal Empire in the 16th century, Persian and Mongol wrestling merged with malla-yuddha to form modern pehlwani/kushti; the Mughal emperor Babur was himself a wrestler. Rajput princes patronized wrestling champions, and the greatest center was Punjab and Uttar Pradesh. The legendary Ghulam Muhammad, "the Great Gama", remained undefeated in over 5,000 bouts in the early 20th century, and the Hind Kesari championship has crowned India's top traditional wrestlers since 1958. Today kushti survives in akharas across Punjab and Haryana while Indian athletes also win Olympic medals in modern freestyle wrestling.
