## Basic Information

Tag rugby is the non-contact form of rugby football in which players wear a belt with two detachable tags: instead of tackles, defenders stop the ball carrier by pulling off a tag. Played by mixed teams of all ages and abilities, it is the world's most accessible rugby format, with leagues and international tournaments from grassroots festivals to national cups.

## Rules

Teams (commonly six or seven a side) run, pass and kick toward the try line exactly as in rugby league-derived forms, but the ball carrier is halted when a defender removes one of their tags and holds it up; the attacker must then play the ball back to a teammate (a roll or tap restart) within a fixed count, usually six tags per possession before turnover. Rules prohibit contact beyond the tag, fending, and kicking ahead in some social formats, and simplify line play (defenders retire a set distance from each tag restart). Standardized codes govern belt and tag specifications, pitch sizes, mixed-game requirements for both genders on the field, and tag counts; festivals and leagues run round robins with finals, and the format's safety and simplicity make it the entry point into rugby worldwide.

## History

Tag rugby grew out of rugby league's and rugby union's efforts to create safe, inclusive versions of the game in the late twentieth century: tag-style belt games appeared in the 1990s in Australia, Great Britain and New Zealand (tag itself borrowed from American flag football's belt format), and codified codes such as OzTag in Australia, Tag Rugby in the United Kingdom and Ireland's tag scene turned the idea into organized local sport. The International Tag Federation and national bodies now run world cups in men's, women's and mixed categories, with strong tag communities in Ireland, Britain, Australia and New Zealand and a fast-growing player base that has made tag the recreational face of the rugby family: everything the handling game offers with the collision and season-ending injuries removed.
