## Basic Information

Fencing is a combat sport in which two fencers duel with blunted swords (foil, épée or sabre) on a 14 m x 1.5-2 m piste, scoring touches on the opponent's valid target area. It is governed by the Fédération Internationale d'Escrime (FIE), which runs the FIE World Championships and the Olympic fencing program, where fencing has been contested at every modern Olympic Games since Athens 1896.

## Rules

At FIE World Championships and the Olympics, individual bouts are fought to 15 touches (or time limit of 3 periods of 3 minutes with a 1-minute break; first to lead at the end wins). Team events are relay-style bouts to 45 touches. Electronic scoring machines register valid touches; in foil and épée there is a "right of way" (foil) or simultaneous-touch rule (épée, 1/40 s) that decides priority, while sabre awards the touch to the fencer who attacks first with right of way. The valid target is the torso (foil, épée) or the whole body above the waist including arms and head (sabre). Fencers must wear a mask, jacket, breeches, glove and a conductive lame (foil/sabre), and weapons are checked before bouts. Puncturing, corps-à-corps to avoid a touch and turning the back are fouls penalized with a yellow/red card.

## History

Fencing is the only sport to appear at every modern Olympic Games, from Athens 1896, when only foil and sabre for amateurs were contested (épée added 1900). The FIE was founded in Paris in 1913 and governs the sport, running World Championships since 1921 (men) and 1929 (women). The sport evolved from dueling and military swordsmanship in 16th-18th century Europe; the electric scoring apparatus was introduced in the 1930s. Italy, France, Hungary, Russia and the United States have dominated historically; legendary fencers include Italy's Edoardo Mangiarotti (13 Olympic medals), Hungary's Aladár Gerevich (7 Olympic golds), and modern stars such as Romain Cannone (épée gold Tokyo 2020) and Oh Sang-uk (sabre, multiple Olympic golds).
