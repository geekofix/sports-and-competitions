## Basic Information

Fencing is a combat sport in which two fencers duel with one of three weapons — foil, épée or sabre — on a 14 m × 1.5–2 m conductive metal piste, scoring touches with an electric scoring apparatus on the opponent's valid target area (FIE, n.d.-b). It is governed worldwide by the Fédération Internationale d'Escrime (FIE), founded on 29 November 1913 in Paris and headquartered in Lausanne, Switzerland, with more than 150 national member federations (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Fencing is one of only four sports contested at every modern Olympic Games since Athens 1896 (Olympics.com, n.d.). The pinnacle competition is the FIE World Championships: the 2026 edition was held in Hong Kong from 22 to 30 July 2026 with twelve events — individual and team for each of the three weapons, for men and for women — and athletes from more than 100 nations (FIE, n.d.-a) (Fédération Internationale d'Escrime, n.d.-a) (Fédération Internationale d'Escrime, n.d.-b).

At the end of 2026 the leading fencers of the 2026/27 season include, on the women's side, the American Lee Kiefer, individual and team foil champion at the Paris 2024 Olympics, together with Italy's Martina Favaretto and Anna Cristino in foil, France's Toscane Tori in sabre and Italy's Roberta Marzani in épée; on the men's side the Czech Alexander Choupenitch tops the FIE world rankings in men's foil, France's Sébastien Patrice leads men's sabre and Hungary's Gergely Siklósi is among the leading épée fencers (FIE, n.d.-b).

## Rules

The field of play, the piste, is 14 m long and 1.5–2 m wide with a conductive surface; the en-garde lines are 2 m on each side of the center line and the rear limits 7 m from the center line, with warning lines 2 m before each rear limit (FIE, n.d.-b). Fencers wear a fencing mask, jacket, breeches, glove and — at foil and sabre — a conductive lamé covering the valid target; a chest protector is compulsory for women at all three weapons and optional for men (FIE, n.d.-b). Weapons are checked before bouts: foil maximum 500 g, total length 110 cm, blade 90 cm; épée maximum 770 g, blade 90 cm; sabre maximum 500 g, total length 105 cm, blade 88 cm; the foil and épée tip must register a pressure of at least 500 g and 750 g respectively (FIE, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Individual direct-elimination bouts are fenced in three periods of three minutes with one-minute breaks, the first fencer to score 15 touches wins; if the score is tied at 15, a one-minute priority period decides, with a pre-drawn winner if no touch is scored (FIE, n.d.-b). Pool bouts last three minutes and end at 5 touches; team events are relays of nine bouts of three minutes each, with the cumulative score counting up to 45 touches (FIE, n.d.-b). At the World Championships and the Olympic Games, fencers first compete in pools and then in a direct-elimination table up to the final; the World Championships comprise twelve events and the Olympic program has comprised all six individual and six team events since Tokyo 2020, achieving full gender parity (FIE, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

In foil and sabre the rules of right of way (priority) decide which fencer scores when both land a touch in the same action: the fencer who attacks, the defender who parries and immediately ripostes, the fencer who remises, or the counter-attacker holds priority (FIE, n.d.-b). In épée there is no right of way — the first valid touch scores, and two touches landing within about 40 milliseconds count as a double touch for both fencers (FIE, n.d.-b). The valid target is the torso covered by the lamé at foil, the whole body at épée, and everything above the waist including head and arms at sabre (FIE, n.d.-b).

The referee penalizes infringements with cards: a yellow card is a warning, a red card awards a penalty touch to the opponent, and a black card excludes the fencer from the competition; a P-black may follow a red card for violent or brutal acts and is decided with video assistance at FIE events (FIE, n.d.-b). Typical fouls are corps-à-corps to avoid a touch, covering the valid target, turning the back, leaving the piste with both feet — which awards a touch to the opponent — and disturbing the lamé with the weapon hand (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Men and women fence under identical rules; the only equipment difference is the compulsory chest protector for women (FIE, n.d.-b). Women's foil entered the Olympic program in 1924, women's épée in 1996 and women's sabre in 2004 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Age classifications are cadet (under 17), junior (under 20), senior (20 and over) and veteran (40 and over), each with its own World Championships and World Cup circuits (FIE, n.d.-a). Para fencing for wheelchair athletes is governed by World Abilitysport with the classes A, B and C (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

Modern fencing grew out of the dueling and military swordsmanship of 16th- to 19th-century Europe, codified in the fencing schools of Italy and France: the foil developed in 18th-century France as the blunted training weapon of the smallsword, the épée as the dueling weapon, and the sabre from the cavalry saber (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Fencing was on the program of the first modern Olympic Games in Athens 1896 — men's foil and sabre plus a masters' foil — and épée was added in 1900 (Olympics.com, n.d.).

The Fédération Internationale d'Escrime was founded on 29 November 1913 in the conference rooms of the Automobile Club de France in Paris; the Belgian Albert Feyerick was elected first president, French rules were adopted for foil and épée and Hungarian rules for sabre (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The FIE has organized World Championships since 1921 for men and women's foil from 1929, officially under the name World Championships from 1937 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Electric scoring replaced the side judges: for épée at the Berlin 1936 Olympics, for foil at Melbourne 1956, and for sabre in 1988 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Italy and Hungary dominated the early decades: Edoardo Mangiarotti (1919–2012) won 13 Olympic medals, the most of any fencer; Aladár Gerevich (1910–1991) took seven Olympic gold medals between 1932 and 1960, six of them in team sabre; Nedo Nadi (1894–1940) won individual and team foil gold at Antwerp 1920; and Christian d'Oriola (1928–2007) of France won four gold medals at London 1948 and Melbourne 1952 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). On the women's side, fencing entered the Olympics at Paris 1924 with women's foil: Helene Mayer (1900–1982) of Germany won individual foil gold in Amsterdam 1928 and silver medals in 1932 and 1936, while Britain's Judy Guinness (1910–2008) took the silver beside her in 1932 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Later icons include Hungary's Ildikó Rejtő (1937–2014, seven Olympic medals), Italy's Valentina Vezzali (b. 1974, nine Olympic medals including three consecutive individual foil titles from 2004 to 2012) and France's Laura Flessel (b. 1971, two épée gold medals at Atlanta 1996) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
