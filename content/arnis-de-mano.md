## Basic Information

Arnis de Mano ("the harnessed/protected hand") is the empty-hand division of Filipino martial arts — the hand techniques that underlie stick and blade work, derived from fighting Spanish fencers during 330 years of colonial rule. It uses open-hand strikes, trapping, joint locks and disarms, and is the basis of panantukan (dirty boxing). It is part of the national sport arnis (RA 9850, 2009) and is taught worldwide; men and women train, and empty-hand events are contested at FMA tournaments. Within the combat sports and martial arts on this site, its closest relatives are [Eskrima](eskrima.html), [Larnis](larnis.html), [Pekiti-Tirsia](pekiti-tirsia.html), [Kuntao](kuntao.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links (Martial Arts Culture and History, n.d.).

## Rules

In sport, arnis de mano follows modern arnis rules: the empty-hand (panuntukan) events use padded gloves and chest protectors, three two-minute rounds of point sparring; disarms and throws score. Traditional arnis de mano has no ring rules — it is a self-defence system assessed by forms and controlled sparring. The 2009 Philippine law (RA 9850) made arnis the national martial art and sport, with DepEd including it in the school curriculum since 2010 (Martial Arts Culture and History, n.d.).

## History

The name comes from the decorated gauntlets used in the Moro-warrior plays (moro-moro) that rehearsed battles against the Muslim south. During Spanish rule (1521–1898) weapon arts were banned, so Filipinos emphasized empty-hand and improvised weapons; after independence the arts were systematized (Doce Pares 1932 Cebu, Balintawak 1950s, Modern Arnis by Remy Presas 1966). Arnis was at the 1936 Berlin Olympics as a demonstration and in the Palarong Pambansa since 2006 (Martial Arts Culture and History, n.d.).
