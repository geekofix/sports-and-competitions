## Basic Information

Surf lifesaving is both a volunteer rescue service and the competitive sport built from its skills: swimming, board paddling, surf ski racing, beach sprints and inflatable rescue boat (IRB) events contested by surf lifesaving clubs. Born on Australian beaches in the early twentieth century, it has grown into an international movement with national associations on every continent and world championships staged by the International Life Saving Federation.

## Rules

Surf sport carnivals contest events that mirror patrol rescue skills: surf swims through the break, board races on foam rescue boards, surf ski and paddle events, beach sprints and beach flags (a turn-and-grab duel for bats buried in the sand), iron person races combining swim-board-ski legs, and team relays such as the Taplin. IRB racing stages simulated rescues "+E+" surf rescue, mass rescue, tube rescue, assembly and team relay events "+E+" in which a driver and crewperson pick up patients in the surf under strict safety rules, with craft scrutineered and standardized. Competition rules set minimum awards for entrants (competitors typically hold the Bronze Medallion or national equivalent), age categories from nippers to masters, and strict safety controls on surf conditions, with scoring by heat and final placements at state, national and world championship carnivals.

## History

Surf lifesaving began in Australia in 1907, when bathers drowned at Sydney beaches prompted the formation of the first surf life saving clubs (Bondi, recognized by Surf Life Saving Australia as the first) and a national body; volunteer patrols using the reel, line and belt became an Australian institution. Competition evolved alongside the patrols, with early recordings of club sport dating to around 1912; board and ski events grew from rescue equipment, IRB racing was formalized by Surf Life Saving Australia from the mid-1980s, and the International Life Saving Federation staged the first World Surf Lifesaving Championships in 1974. The movement spread to New Zealand, South Africa, the United Kingdom, Ireland, Japan, the United States and beyond, and surf sport remains the movement's engine: a calendar of club, state and national championships (the Australians among them) plus world titles contested by national teams keeps the rescue skills race-sharp.
