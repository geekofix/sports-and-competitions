## Basic Information

Aikido is a Japanese martial art (budo) created by Morihei Ueshiba in the 1920s–1940s that resolves conflict by blending with an attacker's movement and energy (ai ki) instead of meeting force with force, using throws (nage-waza), joint locks and pins (katame-waza) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-e). Internationally the art is represented by the International Aikido Federation (IAF), founded in 1976, which counted 87 member countries as of April 2024 and exclusively represents the Aikikai, the parent organisation headquartered at Hombu Dojo in Tokyo; aikido is established in over 130 countries and is open to everyone regardless of gender or age (International Aikido Federation, n.d.-c; International Aikido Federation, n.d.-a; International Aikido Federation, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-d).

Aikido is overwhelmingly non-competitive: the IAF presents it at the World Games and the World Combat Games purely as a demonstration sport, and training centres on cooperative free practice (randori) rather than bouts; only a few styles, notably Shodokan, hold formal competitions (International Aikido Federation, n.d.-c; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Because there are no professional bouts, there are no professional athletes to rank — instead the art's most visible current figures include women at the very top of the organisation, such as Yoko Okamoto of Japan, IAF chairperson since 2024, alongside the many women shihan (8th-dan masters) teaching worldwide; the photograph shows a female practitioner executing a throw in a class (International Aikido Federation, n.d.-c; Wikipedia, n.d.-d).

## Rules

Training takes place in a dojo on tatami mats; beginners wear a white keikogi and advanced students add the black hakama, and etiquette (bowing, cleanliness, respect for the teacher) is part of the art (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Gradings run from 6th to 1st kyū (pupil grades) up to 1st–8th dan, with 8th dan the highest rank awarded within the Aikikai; the technical syllabus covers throws, pins, joint locks, falling (ukemi) and, in many schools, weapons practice with the wooden sword (bokken), staff (jō) and knife (tantō) (International Aikido Federation, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

There are no weight classes and no separate men's and women's curricula — men and women train together under identical gradings, and children's classes typically start around age 6, with adult and senior groups alongside; competitive rules exist only in the Shodokan branch, which contests tantō-style bouts with a wooden knife (International Aikido Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

## History

Morihei Ueshiba (1883–1969), known posthumously as O-Sensei (“great teacher”), pictured in 1939, had studied jujutsu — including Daito-ryū aiki-jūjutsu under Takeda Sōkaku — and was a senior disciple of the Ōmoto-kyō movement of Onisaburo Deguchi before opening his Kobukan Dojo in Tokyo in April 1931; he named the art aikido in 1942 and the Aikikai Foundation has headed it since, moving into the purpose-built Hombu Dojo in Shinjuku in 1968 (Wikipedia, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-c; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

The leadership (doshu) passed from the founder to his son Kisshomaru Ueshiba (1921–1999) and in 1999 to Moriteru Ueshiba (b. 1951); major offshoot styles include Yoseikan (Minoru Mochizuki, 1931), Yoshinkan (Gozō Shioda, 1955) and the competitive Shodokan (Kenji Tomiki, 1967) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-c). The art spread from Koichi Tohei's move to Hawaii in 1953 to Britain (1955), France (1961, Norihiko Noro), Italy (1964, Hiroshi Tada) and Germany (1965, Mitsugi Asai), and the IAF was founded in Tokyo in 1976, becoming a full GAISF member in 1984 and an AIMS member, with aikido demonstrated at the World Games (Wikipedia, n.d.-c; International Aikido Federation, n.d.-c; International Aikido Federation, n.d.-a).
