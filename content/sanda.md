## Basic Information

Sanda, formerly known as Sanshou, is the full-contact kickboxing combat sport of Chinese wushu, combining boxing-style punches and kicks with wrestling throws, takedowns and sweeps — and, in some competitions, elbow and knee strikes. It was developed in the Chinese military from traditional Chinese martial arts and modern combat methods, standardised as a sport alongside taolu (forms), and is governed internationally by the International Wushu Federation (IWUF), which organises world championship and Asian Games competition.

## Rules

Competitive bouts are fought on the leitai, an elevated platform 80 cm high and 8 by 8 metres, with foam mats around it. Adult matches comprise three two-minute rounds with a one-minute rest between them; a fighter wins by taking two of three rounds, by knockout, or when the opponent is disqualified or his corner throws in the towel. Valid techniques drawn from any wushu style include punching, kicking and throwing, with valid striking areas the head, trunk and legs; blows to the back of the head, throat, spine or groin are illegal, and ground fighting is discontinued as soon as a fighter falls — the sport is fought standing, with sweeps, kick catches and the fast, high-amplitude throws (such as the scissor takedown) that distinguish it from Western kickboxing. Fighters wear gloves, protective gear and, at world level, compete in a range of weight categories; forceful clean throws that project the opponent off the platform, an expression of the "throw off the leitai" tradition, score among the highest points of the sport.

## History

Sanda's competitive roots lie in the rule-free lei tai challenge matches of traditional Chinese martial arts, and in the barehanded fighting systems taught to soldiers: it was first systematised in the 1920s at the Whampoa military academy, and in 1928 the Central Kuoshu Academy sponsored a full-contact national tournament that carried the lei tai tradition forward. The People's Liberation Army adopted the method for hand-to-hand combat, and when the People's Republic developed modern wushu in the 1950s through the Chinese Wushu Association (founded 1958), sanda was formalised as the sport's fighting half, with competition rules, gloves and scoring introduced for civilian competition — a curriculum often applied with restrictions for safety that remain the standard today. The 1988 international invitational championships in China opened the discipline to the world, and the International Wushu Federation, founded in 1990, has governed it since, staging sanda competition at the World Wushu Championships and including the discipline in the Asian Games, where it has been a medal sport since the 1990 Beijing Games; sanda fighters from China, Iran, Vietnam, Russia and Egypt have dominated recent world championships, and the sport's profile rises with each multi-sport games appearance.
