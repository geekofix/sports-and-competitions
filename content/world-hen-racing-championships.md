## Basic Information

Hen racing is the eccentric rural novelty sport in which hens sprint a short course, with the first bird home winning the heat — most famously contested at the World Hen Racing Championships in the car park of the Barley Mow Inn in Bonsall, Derbyshire, England, traditionally on the first Saturday of August (BBC, n.d.). Handlers may not touch, carry or feed their hen to induce it to run, women and men compete on equal terms as handlers and owners, and any breed of hen may enter — competitors bring their own bird or rent one on the day for a small fee (Derbyshire Times, n.d.). The event is staged for charity, with collections supporting animal-welfare causes, which keeps it firmly on the humane side of animal sport (BBC, n.d.).

## Rules

At the Bonsall championship, heats of four hens race a short sprint course of roughly 30 feet across the pub car park under a time limit of three to four minutes, and the first hen across the finish line wins the heat (Wikipedia, n.d.). Hens that peck or fight are disqualified, lifting the bird is forbidden, and using food or other inducements is against the spirit of the rules, so the race is decided by the hen's own pace (BBC, n.d.). After racing, every hen is returned to its owner or released unharmed, and entry fees plus spectator collections go to animal charities rather than to betting (Derbyshire Times, n.d.). Local copycat races elsewhere in Britain follow the same sprint-heat format with adjusted course lengths and prize structures.

## History

Organised hen racing in Bonsall stretches back more than a century in the village, where earlier contests had judged hens on egg-laying rather than speed, and the modern sprint "World Championship" was formally launched at the Barley Mow Inn in 1992 (Wikipedia, n.d.). The event grew into an annual August fixture covered by national and international press — BBC cameras reported the 20th annual championship in 2011 — and it set the template for hen races across Britain and overseas (BBC, n.d.). The light-hearted rivalry between Bonsall and other villages that host similar races remains part of the event's charm.
