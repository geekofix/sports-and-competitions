## Basic Information

Dara is the Senegalese (Wolof) name for the mancala sowing game played across the Senegambia region on a board of two rows of six pits with 48 seeds — the same game known elsewhere in West Africa as wari, awale or ouril. It is played casually in streets and markets and in organized tournaments; men and women play on equal terms, and girls' play is a common sight in Senegal. (Note: a different West African mill game is also called dara — the three-in-a-row game; this entry covers the sowing game.) Within the mancala and mind games on this site, its closest relatives are [Oware](oware.html), [Ayoayo](ayoayo.html), [Kalah](kalah.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Players sit opposite and sow counterclockwise from one of their six pits, one seed per pit, skipping the pit they picked up. If the last seed lands in an opponent's pit and makes it two or three seeds, those seeds are captured; captures chain backward as long as the preceding pits also end on two or three. A grand-slam move that would empty the opponent's side is legal but captures nothing, and if the opponent would be left with no seeds you must make a feeding move if possible. The game ends when one side has no seeds to move; the remaining seeds go to the other player, and more than 24 seeds wins.

## History

Sowing games of the oware family have been played across West Africa for centuries under many names — wari in Mali, awale in Côte d'Ivoire, ouril in Cape Verde, ayoayo among the Yoruba — and were carried to the Americas by enslaved Africans (warri in the Caribbean). Carved wooden boards from Sierra Leone in the British Museum collection show that elaborate boards were made in the early 20th century. The name dara is simply the Wolof word for the game; Robert Sutherland Rattray, who documented West African games for the Royal Anthropological Institute, recorded the Senegalese form under related names.
