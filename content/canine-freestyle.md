## Basic Information

Canine freestyle — also called musical canine freestyle, freestyle dance or, in the UK, *heelwork to music* (HTM) — is a dog sport in which a handler and dog perform a choreographed routine to music, combining obedience, tricks and dance. The two main competition styles are heelwork to music (the dog stays in heel position for at least two-thirds of the routine) and freestyle (at least two-thirds free movement, with distance work, jumps, spins and props allowed in some divisions) (World Canine Freestyle Organization [WCFO], n.d.; The Kennel Club, n.d.). Routines are performed off-leash without training aids, and judging scores technical merit (precision and difficulty), artistic interpretation (choreography to the music) and teamwork. The sport is recognized by the UK Kennel Club, which hosts the Heelwork to Music and Freestyle finals at Crufts every March (Crufts 2026 at the NEC Birmingham; the 2026 judges included Louise Ballard, Gina Pink and Carmen Schmid), and internationally by the FCI, which held the Dog Dancing World Championship (HTM and Freestyle) within the World Dog Show from 4 to 7 June 2026 at BolognaFiere, Italy (The Kennel Club, n.d.; World Dog Show 2026, n.d.). Major governing/organizing bodies include the WCFO, the Canine Freestyle Federation, the Musical Dog Sport Association (North America), Canine Freestyle GB and Paws 2 Dance (Canada); the first FCI world titles were awarded at the 2010 FCI World Dog Show in Herning, Denmark (WCFO, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). ## Rules

In heelwork to music, the dog must remain in heel position — close to the handler on either side or in face-to-face and other close positions — for at least two-thirds of the routine; judges deduct 0.1–1.0 points from the technical-merit score for each loss of heelwork proximity. In freestyle, at least two-thirds of the routine is free movement: leg weaving, distance work, jumps, spins, bowing, rolling and finishing tricks such as jumping into the handler's arms are allowed. Routines typically last 1.5–2.5 minutes, are performed off-leash with no food or training aids, and are judged on technical content, artistic interpretation of the music and teamwork; musicality matters — routines that do not follow the rhythm score poorly (WCFO, n.d.). Competitions run in classes (novice, intermediate, advanced; HTM and freestyle divisions), with qualification for the Crufts finals through premier shows during the year; single dog-and-handler, pairs and teams of three or more may compete. At the FCI Dog Dancing World Championship the same HTM/freestyle split is used with national teams and individual titles (The Kennel Club, n.d.; World Dog Show 2026, n.d.). Exhibition freestyle (with props, cues and costumes) is allowed only outside championship classes (WCFO, n.d.).

## History

Musical canine freestyle began almost simultaneously around 1989 in Canada, England, the United States and the Netherlands, inspired by the equestrian sport of musical kur; the first official organization, Musical Canine Sports International, was founded in British Columbia in 1991. In the UK, obedience handler Mary Ray first demonstrated heelwork to music at Crufts in the early 1990s, Rugby Dog Club held the first show in 1996, and the Kennel Club made HTM an officially recognized sport in 2005–2007, with the first Crufts competitions in 2005–2006; Tina Humphrey and her Border Collie "Chandi" famously won all three Crufts finals in 2009. The World Canine Freestyle Organization was founded in 1999, and the first FCI World Championships in HTM and Freestyle were held at the 2010 FCI World Dog Show in Herning, won by Kath Hardman (HTM) and Thierry Thomas (freestyle) (Wikipedia, n.d.). Today the sport has dozens of national organizations, world championships under the FCI, and a growing para/adaptive scene (WCFO, n.d.).
