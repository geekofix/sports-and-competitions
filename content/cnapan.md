## Basic Information

Cnapan is the medieval mass ball game of southwest Wales (Carmarthenshire, Ceredigion, Pembrokeshire), described in detail by George Owen of Henllys in 1603: two parishes' teams — up to 2,000 players — fight to carry a small ball (boiled in animal fat for slickness) to their own parish church door, by any means, chasing for miles; the game ends only when the ball is carried far enough that return is hopeless. It has been cited as a possible antecedent of rugby union. Modern reenactments (Newport–Nevern 1985, Traeth Mawr 2012, England–Wales 1990) used modified rules with a referee and leather ball. Within the football (soccer) family and its variants on this site, its closest relatives are [La Soule](la-soule.html), [Football](football.html), [Rugby Union](rugby-union.html), [Calcio Fiorentino](calcio-fiorentino.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## Rules

George Owen's 1603 description: the ball (cnapan) is hurled up by a company member; whoever catches it hurls it toward his side's goal (the parish church); there is no fixed goal beyond that, and play continues until the ball is two miles or more away and cannot be returned that night. 19th-century Shrove Tuesday matches (Laugharne 1838, Neath 1884) led to court cases over disorder. Revived matches use teams of any size, players joining freely, a leather ball and a referee (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

The 9th-century Historia Brittonum (Nennius) mentions boys "playing at ball" (pilae ludus); George Owen believed cnapan was played by the Celtic Britons. Church doors served as goals for centuries (St Mary's Haverfordwest damaged by 1914). The modern game was reenacted Newport vs Nevern (1985, annual for a decade, ended over insurance), England vs Wales at Gateshead (1990), and on BBC's Britain's Lost Routes (2012) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
