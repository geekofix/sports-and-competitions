## Basic Information

Xiangqi (Chinese chess) is a two-player strategic board game played on a board of nine columns by ten rows in which each side commands an army of sixteen pieces including a general, advisors, a unique pair of cannons and chariots — pieces move on the intersections of lines, and the central "river" separates opposing halves with the palace box restricting the general and advisors. It is one of the most played board games in the world, the dominant chess variant in China with millions of players and a professional title system.

Games are decided by checkmate of the general, stalemate-like scoring rules, and positional skill in which open-file play, river crossings and material trades decide most outcomes.

## Rules

Each side has a general, two advisors, two elephants, two horses, two chariots, two cannons and five soldiers; the chariots move like rooks, horses like knights but leg-blocked at the move's pivot, elephants cannot cross the river, and cannons capture only by leaping exactly one intervening piece — the game's signature piece. The general moves one intersection within his palace and faces the opposing general across an open file; soldiers that cross the river gain sideways movement. Checkmate ends the game, and unlike Western chess, a stalemated player (with no legal non-losing move) loses the game.

## History

Xiangqi descends from the shared ancestor of all chess games — early Chinese games of the chaturanga family documented from the seventh to ninth centuries, with the game’s board, pieces and rules standardising over the following dynasties and reliably described by the Song dynasty (ninth to thirteenth centuries). It developed in China as the scholarly and popular board game par excellence, with classic manuals from the Ming and Qing dynasties, and in the twentieth century the game gained national associations from 1962, professional leagues in China, and international body the World Xiangqi Federation, holding world championships since the 1990s. It remains the most played chess variant after international chess, with strong communities across China, Vietnam, and the worldwide Chinese diaspora and online platforms reinforcing play.
