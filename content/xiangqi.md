## Basic Information

Xiangqi (Chinese chess) is a two-player strategy game played on a board of nine vertical files and ten horizontal ranks, where each side commands sixteen pieces — a general, two advisors, two elephants, two horses, two chariots, two cannons and five soldiers — and wins by checkmating the enemy general (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is governed worldwide by the World Xiangqi Federation (WXF), founded on 6 April 1993 in Beijing, which runs the biennial World Xiangqi Championship — the first edition was held in Singapore in 1990, and the 19th edition in Shanghai in 2025 drew about 183 players from 25 countries, with separate women's and open categories (World Xiangqi Federation, n.d.). A distinctive rule sets xiangqi apart from Western chess: stalemate is a loss for the stalemated player, and perpetual check or chase also loses, so the game always rewards the attacker (Wikipedia, n.d.). At the end of this section, the current leading players, women first, are the reigning women's world champion and the open world champion crowned at the 19th World Xiangqi Championship in Shanghai in 2025 (World Xiangqi Federation, n.d.).

## Rules

The board's nine files and ten ranks are split by a blank "river", and each general is confined to the 3 × 3 palace at its end (Wikipedia, n.d.). The general moves one step orthogonally inside the palace, advisors move one step diagonally inside the palace, elephants move exactly two points diagonally but may never cross the river and are blocked by any piece between, and horses move one point orthogonally followed by one point diagonally outward but are "leg-blocked" when a piece stands on the adjacent orthogonal point (Wikipedia, n.d.). Chariots move like rooks in Western chess, while cannons move like rooks but capture only by leaping over exactly one screen piece of either colour (Wikipedia, n.d.). Soldiers advance one point forward and, after crossing the river, may also move one point sideways; the two generals may never face each other on the same file without a screen between them — the "flying general" rule (World Xiangqi Federation, n.d.). Checkmate wins; stalemate loses for the stalemated side, and perpetual check or perpetual chase by one side is also a loss for the chasing side, so games are decided decisively (Wikipedia, n.d.). In competition, clocks with increments are used, and men's (open) and women's categories are contested separately at world championships, with junior categories for younger players (World Xiangqi Federation, n.d.).

## History

Xiangqi's origins go back to the Warring States period (around 475–221 BC), with early forms of the game played in China, and the modern rules were largely standardised during the Song dynasty; related games spread across East Asia, giving rise to Korean janggi and Japanese shogi (Wikipedia, n.d.). The international governing body was prepared by a committee set up in October 1988 at the 4th Seven-Star Tournament and formally founded as the World Xiangqi Federation on 6 April 1993 in Beijing (World Xiangqi Federation, n.d.). The first World Xiangqi Championship was held in Singapore in 1990 and the event has been biennial since 1991, with women's categories included from the start of the modern era; the 19th edition took place in Shanghai in 2025 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Domestically, Hu Ronghua dominated Chinese chess by winning ten national titles between 1980 and 2000, while the 2024–25 match-fixing scandal — which led to lifetime bans for leading players including Wang Tianyi and Zhao Xinxin in January 2025 — showed the federation enforcing its rules at the very top of the women's and men's games (Wikipedia, n.d.).
