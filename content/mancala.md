## Basic Information

Mancala is the name of a family of more than 800 count-and-capture board games played across Africa and beyond, from the Arabic naqala, “to move”: players scoop all the seeds, stones or shells from one pit and sow them one by one into the following pits, aiming to capture more than half of the pieces (Wikipedia, n.d.-d; Wikipedia, n.d.-c). The best-known variants are oware (West Africa and the Caribbean, where it is called warri), kalah (the commercial 2×6 game with stores), bao (4×8, East Africa) and toguzkumalak (Kazakhstan), and competitive play is organised for oware by The Oware Society, the first body to promote international oware tournaments, which lists world championships held since 2010 (Wikipedia, n.d.-e; The Oware Society, n.d.-b; The Oware Society, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-f).

The games are open to everyone — men and women, children and adults play together socially, and at tournament level men's and women's titles are contested separately in oware and toguzkumalak, with junior events at the Mind Sports Olympiad and online on platforms such as PlayOK (Wikipedia, n.d.-e; The Oware Society, n.d.-a).

## Rules

All mancala games share the sowing mechanic — counterclockwise, one piece per pit, skipping the opponent's store in some variants — but boards differ: two-row games use 2×6 pits plus stores (kalah, started with four seeds per pit and the store on the right), while four-row games such as bao and omweso use 4×8 pits (Wikipedia, n.d.-d; Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

In oware (abapa rules), the board holds 48 seeds, four per pit, with no stores; if the last seed of a move lands in an opponent's pit and makes it hold exactly two or three seeds, those are captured, with chain captures possible, and a player must make a move that feeds the opponent if the opponent's side is empty; the game ends when one side is empty or a player cannot move, and the winner is the one with more seeds — more than half of the 48 (Wikipedia, n.d.-e).

## History

Archaeological evidence places mancala-style sowing games in Eritrea and Ethiopia by the 6th–7th century AD, with Roman-era boards also found, and the games spread along trade routes to the Caribbean (warri), the Middle East and Asia; the classic 1896 study by Stewart Culin, “Mancala, the National Game of Africa”, first documented the family systematically, and carved boards such as the late-19th-century example in the Brooklyn Museum, pictured here, show the craftsmanship of the tradition (Wikipedia, n.d.-d; Wikipedia, n.d.-e).

In the 20th century the games reached a global audience through commercial sets — kalah was patented in the United States in the 1940s–1950s — and through the digital era, when the mancala variant bantumi appeared on Nokia phones such as the 3310, while today's competitive scene centres on oware, toguzkumalak, omweso and bao, with international tournaments organised by the Oware Society and world championships in several variants (Wikipedia, n.d.-d; Wikipedia, n.d.-b; The Oware Society, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-f).
