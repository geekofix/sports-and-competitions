## Basic Information

Kite fighting is competitive cutting of the opponent's kite line: fighters fly paper- or plastic-covered kites coated with abrasive glass powder (called manja or 'glass line') and try to saw through the rival's line so their kite falls; it is the traditional sport of the Basant festival in Lahore, Pakistan, and of Makar Sankranti in India and of kite festivals in Afghanistan and Bangladesh (Wikipedia, n.d.). Matches are one-on-one; the last kite in the air wins, and festivals draw thousands of participants; men and women both fly at festivals (women's sections exist in Pakistan).

## Rules

Traditional rules: each fighter gets one kite and a length of coated line; the bout ends when one line is cut (the fallen kite's owner loses) or, in festival rounds, by points for cuts made; line coating (manja: glass powder on cotton or synthetic line) is regulated — in Pakistan only cotton line with approved coating is legal after court rulings, to protect bystanders and birds; age and gender categories at organized events (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

Kite fighting has centuries of history in South Asia — the Mughal-era tradition of 'patang bazi' in Lahore, formalized into the Basant festival in the 19th century; modern organized fighting with safety rules runs at the Lahore Kite Flying Festival and in India (Ahmedabad, Gujarat) around Makar Sankranti (14 January) (Wikipedia, n.d.).
