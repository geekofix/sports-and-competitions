## Basic Information

Gouren (Breton for "wrestling"), also known as Breton wrestling (*lutte bretonne*), is a traditional standing jacket-wrestling style of Brittany in which two wrestlers grip each other's jacket on a mat — sawdust outdoors in summer, an indoor mat in winter — and try to throw the opponent to the ground; it is practiced mainly in Brittany but also in Cornwall, Scotland and beyond through the international meetings of the FILC (Fédération Internationale des Luttes Celtiques), founded in 1985 (Wikipedia, n.d.; Fédération Internationale des Luttes Celtiques, n.d.). In France the sport is governed by the Fédération de Gouren, founded in 1980, which today counts about 1,700 licensed wrestlers — roughly a quarter of them women — in some fifty *skolioù* (clubs) across Brittany, and which has been affiliated with the French Wrestling Federation (FFL) since 1995; gouren has been an optional subject in the French baccalauréat since 1998 (Fédération Française de Lutte, n.d.; Fédération de Gouren, n.d.-a). The European Championships of Celtic Wrestling organized by the FILC bring together gouren and Scottish backhold wrestlers from Brittany, Scotland, Cornwall, Austria, Sardinia, Spain, Iceland and other nations — the 2026 edition is held in Carlisle, England, in November 2026 (Fédération Internationale des Luttes Celtiques, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).

At the end of 2026 the leading women are the top Breton wrestlers of the women's categories — including the all-women Coupe des Lutteuses — with women making up roughly a quarter of the federation's licensees, while on the men's side the reigning champions of the Breton and European championships compete in eight weight categories (Fédération Française de Lutte, n.d.; Fédération de Gouren, n.d.-a; Fédération Internationale des Luttes Celtiques, n.d.).

## Rules

Gouren is fought standing up, barefoot, on the *pallen* — a mat of sawdust in summer or an indoor wrestling mat in winter (Fédération de Gouren, n.d.-a). Wrestlers wear a strong white shirt, the *roched*, tied with a belt, and black trousers, the *bragoù*; holds are taken only on the opponent's *roched*, above the belt, the belt itself included, while the legs may hook and entangle freely — giving rise to techniques such as the barrage, the sweep (*taol biz troad*) and the emblematic *kliked*, a leg coil around the opponent's calf (Fédération Française de Lutte, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). As soon as one wrestler touches the ground with any part of the body other than the feet, the bout is stopped, both wrestlers rise, shake hands and resume (Wikipedia, n.d.). The perfect result, the *lamm*, wins the bout immediately: the loser is thrown flat on the back so that both shoulder blades touch the ground simultaneously before any other part of the body (Wikipedia, n.d.; Fédération de Gouren, n.d.-a). If no *lamm* is scored within the time limit — bouts can last up to seven minutes, and in international competition five minutes plus two minutes thirty of extra time — the winner is decided by the intermediate results: *kostin* (one shoulder blade touching the ground) and *kein* (the lower back), with an advantage system preventing draws (Fédération Internationale des Luttes Celtiques, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Before each competition the wrestlers recite the oath of fair play, and the referee opens the bout with "Prest oc'h" ("Are you ready?") and then "Krogit!" ("Wrestle!"); passivity and brutality are fouls, and deliberately dropping to a knee or a hand to escape a throw is cautioned first and disqualifying on repetition (Fédération Internationale des Luttes Celtiques, n.d.). Men and women compete in separate weight categories — eight at the European Championships — under identical rules, and children's and youth divisions are common at Breton festivals (Fédération Internationale des Luttes Celtiques, n.d.; Fédération de Gouren, n.d.-a).

## History

Wrestling has been practiced in Armorica since the 4th century, when Breton settlers brought martial techniques that merged with local ones; gouren was long the exercise of nobles and men-at-arms, spreading to the common people after the Renaissance, and the term *gouren* appears as early as 1464 in the *Catholicon*, the first trilingual dictionary, while the royal surgeon Ambroise Paré described Breton wrestling as practiced in western Brittany in 1543 (Wikipedia, n.d.; Fédération de Gouren, n.d.-b). In 1930 Dr Charles Cotonnec of Quimperlé founded the FALSAB to bring gouren into the modern sporting era — codifying the written rules, introducing the time limit, the intermediate results that can decide a bout (*kostin*, *kein*) and the sawdust mat to limit injuries (Fédération de Gouren, n.d.-b). The Fédération de Gouren was created in 1980, the FILC in 1985, the European Championships of Celtic Wrestling in 1986, the affiliation with the French Wrestling Federation followed in 1995, and gouren became an optional baccalauréat subject in 1998 (PCI Bretagne, n.d.; Fédération de Gouren, n.d.-b). The Breton diaspora even opened a *skol* of gouren in New Orleans, and today the federation runs competitions year-round — the Breton championship in summer on sawdust and indoor tournaments on the Olympic mat in winter (Wikipedia, n.d.; Fédération de Gouren, n.d.-a).
