## Basic Information

Prisonball is a variation of [dodgeball](dodgeball.html) in which players who are hit are not eliminated but sent to a "prison", "jail" or "doghouse" behind the opposing team's back line, from which they can be released and rejoin the game (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). It is one of the most widely played schoolyard dodgeball variants, known by many names — prison dodgeball, jailball, jailbreak, battleball, trench, king's court in Canada, queimada in Brazil and balle au prisonnier in France — and it is played by girls and boys, women and men alike in schools, camps and recreational leagues (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## Rules

Play starts like standard dodgeball: two teams face each other across a centre line, and a player hit by a thrown ball, or whose throw is caught, goes to a marked prison area behind the opposing team instead of leaving the game (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The release rules vary by local ruleset and are the heart of the game: a common standard frees a prisoner when a teammate throws a ball into the prison and a prisoner catches it, sometimes freeing all prisoners at once; other versions let a prisoner earn release by hitting an opponent, who then goes to prison in turn; in the "ghost" version prisoners stay in play behind the back line and may attack with collected balls. In basketball-court versions, sinking a dodgeball into the opposing basket frees all of a team's prisoners. The game ends when all members of one team are in prison; headshots are commonly banned and hits do not count if the ball touched the ground first (PE Scholar, 2014). Open to all ages and both genders with no separate classes.


## History

Prisonball descends from the folk tradition of dodgeball-type schoolyard and gym games that spread through American and European physical-education programs in the 20th century; the German Völkerball ("nation ball"), played with a single ball and a jailed "king", is essentially the same game known in continental Europe since the 19th century, and French balle au prisonnier is its standard school form (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In the United States the variant thrives in schoolyards and summer camps under countless regional names, while formalized dodgeball organizations such as the World Dodgeball Federation play standardized rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
