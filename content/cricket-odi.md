## Basic Information

One Day International (ODI) cricket is the 50-over format of international cricket: each team bats for a maximum of 50 overs and bowls 50 overs, and the side with more runs wins. ODIs are played under the ICC Men's or Women's ODI Playing Conditions, and the flagship tournament is the ICC Cricket World Cup, held every four years (the 2027 edition is co-hosted by South Africa, Zimbabwe and Namibia). The ICC governs the format from Dubai.

## Rules

Under the ICC ODI Playing Conditions, each innings lasts at most 50 six-ball overs. The bowling side may have at most two fielders outside the 30-yard circle during the first ten overs (powerplay), a maximum of four between overs 11 and 40, and five from over 41; each bowler may bowl a maximum of ten overs. A delivery outside the batter's reach is a wide (extra run plus the ball), an illegal delivery is a no-ball (one run, plus the ball), and at most two bouncers per over are allowed. The team batting first sets a target; the chasing side wins by wickets in hand (up to ten wickets) or by runs if scores level (a tie). Rain-affected matches are decided by the Duckworth-Lewis-Stern method.

## History

Limited-overs cricket was pioneered in England in the 1960s, and the first ODI was played in 1971 at Melbourne (Australia beat England in a rain-shortened 40-over match, later adjusted). The ICC Cricket World Cup began in 1975 in England and has been held every four years; Australia is the most successful team with six titles, followed by India and the West Indies with two each. The Women's Cricket World Cup started in 1973, before the men's. ODI cricket became the dominant international format in the 1990s with colored clothing and day-night matches.
