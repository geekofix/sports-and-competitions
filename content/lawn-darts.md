## Basic Information

Lawn darts (also Jarts, javelin darts, lawn jarts or yard darts) are thrown at a horizontal target ring on the ground, combining horseshoes and darts: a set typically has four 12-inch (30 cm) darts with a weighted tip and plastic fins plus two plastic ring targets, tossed underhand so the weighted end sticks into the ground, with a point for landing anywhere within the ring (Wikipedia, n.d.). The game debuted in the United Kingdom in the late 1950s under the name "flying horseshoes" and became a backyard craze in North America in the 1970s and 1980s (Wikipedia, n.d.). After thousands of injuries and at least three children’s deaths — 6,100 Americans visited emergency rooms between 1980 and 1988, 81% of them aged 15 or younger — the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission banned the sale of sharp metal-tipped lawn darts effective 19 December 1988, and Canada banned them in July 1989 under the Hazardous Products Act (Wikipedia, n.d.; CPSC, 1988). Today only soft-tip plastic versions (blunt-tip "Jarts") may be sold in the U.S. and Canada, while the EU permits rounded, often magnetic tips (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Modern lawn darts are played with the legal soft-tip sets: two players or teams alternate tossing darts at the opponent’s target ring, scoring for darts landing inside the ring, with the closest dart to the centre breaking ties in many house rules — much like horseshoes (Wikipedia, n.d.). Organized play uses set pitching distances and safety rules: nobody may stand in the field of play while a dart is thrown (Wikipedia, n.d.). The game is open to all ages and genders and remains a staple of barbecues and family gatherings, now with the safe plastic-tipped darts (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## Rules

A standard set has four darts and two rings; players or teams take turns throwing underhand from behind a throwing line toward the opponent’s ring, which lies on the ground a set distance away (Wikipedia, n.d.). A dart landing anywhere inside the ring scores a point; some rulesets award extra points for the centre, and ties are broken by the dart closest to the centre (Wikipedia, n.d.). Safety rules are mandatory in organized play: only soft-tip darts may be used in the U.S. and Canada, no person or animal may be within the field of play during a throw, and darts are retrieved only when all players are behind the line (Wikipedia, n.d.; CPSC, 1988). The original sharp metal-tipped darts are a banned hazardous product in the U.S. (CPSC ban effective 19 December 1988) and their resale is illegal in Canada (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men and women play under the same rules (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

Lawn darts first appeared in the United Kingdom in the late 1950s as "flying horseshoes" and were marketed in North America from the 1970s as the ultimate backyard game (Wikipedia, n.d.). In 1970 the U.S. FDA classified sharp-pointed darts as a "mechanical hazard", forcing warning labels and banning sales in toy stores (Wikipedia, n.d.). The death of seven-year-old Michele Snow in April 1987 — a dart thrown by a playmate penetrated her skull — galvanized her father David Snow’s campaign for an outright ban, and on 25 May 1988 the CPSC voted to ban sharp-tipped lawn darts, effective 19 December 1988, with a consent decree against Sears earlier that year (CPSC, 1988; Wikipedia, n.d.). Canada followed with a ban in July 1989 (Wikipedia, n.d.). In 1997 the CPSC renewed warnings after another child was gravely injured by an old set (Wikipedia, n.d.). The ban left safer soft-tip versions on the market, and the game continues today with plastic-tipped darts (Wikipedia, n.d.).
