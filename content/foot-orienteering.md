## Basic Information

Foot orienteering is the running discipline of orienteering: on a map-and-compass course through forest, park or urban terrain, athletes must visit a sequence of control points marked on the map in the shortest possible time, punching each control electronically (IOF, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It is governed worldwide by the International Orienteering Federation (IOF), founded in 1961 in Copenhagen, which today unites member federations from more than 70 countries and organizes the World Orienteering Championships (WOC), the World Cup and the orienteering world ranking (IOF, n.d.-a). Since 2019 the WOC alternates between sprint-only editions in even years and forest editions in odd years; WOC 2026 was the sprint championship in Genova, Italy, July 7–11, 2026, with the sprint final in an arena at Piazza Baracca in Sestri Ponente, the knock-out sprint qualification on July 9 and the finals on July 10, followed by the sprint relay on July 11 at Piazza Matteotti — won by Kasper Fosser of Norway (his second sprint title after 2022) and Simona Aebersold of Switzerland (her first sprint gold), while Switzerland defended the sprint relay title (IOF, n.d.-c). WOC 2027 will be the forest edition in Hungary and WOC 2028 in Spain; the European Orienteering Championships 2026 took place in Lithuania (IOF, n.d.-d) (International Orienteering Federation, n.d.-a) (International Orienteering Federation, n.d.-b) (International Orienteering Federation, n.d.-c) (International Orienteering Federation, n.d.-d).

At the end of 2026 the leading women are Simona Aebersold (SUI), the 2026 sprint world champion, and the Swedes Tove Alexandersson — the most decorated athlete in WOC history and reigning knock-out sprint champion — and Hanna Lundberg, one of the world's best sprinters, while on the men's side Kasper Fosser (NOR) and Riccardo Rancan (SUI) head the sprint rankings ahead of Matthias Kyburz and the 2026 World Cup leader (IOF, n.d.-d; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## Rules

The IOF Competition Rules define four individual disciplines: sprint (winning times of about 12–15 minutes on a 1:4,000 or 1:5,000 map in urban or park terrain), middle distance (30–35 minutes, 1:10,000), long distance (60–90 minutes, 1:15,000 or 1:10,000 for elite classes) and relay (IOF, n.d.-b). At every event each competitor receives the map only at the start and must navigate alone — any outside assistance leads to disqualification — and every control must be visited in order and punched with the electronic SI card; a missing or mispunched control means disqualification (IOF, n.d.-b). Start systems differ by discipline: mass start for long distance, interval start for middle distance, and a qualification system for sprint, where the knock-out sprint (introduced in 2001, full sprint program since 2019) runs heats of six athletes, of whom the top two plus the two fastest overall advance to the next round (IOF, n.d.-b). Relays are run by teams of four (men's and women's) or as a mixed sprint relay in a woman–man–man–woman leg order (IOF, n.d.-b). Men and women compete in the same elite classes on identical courses; age classes exist for juniors (U20 at the Junior World Orienteering Championships), youth (U18 at EYOF) and masters (World Masters Orienteering Championships), and there are separate disciplines for mountain bike orienteering (MTBO), ski orienteering and trail orienteering (IOF, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The official IOF world ranking is published continuously from World Cup and championship results (IOF, n.d.-d).

## History

Orienteering as a competitive sport began in Sweden in the late 19th century — the first public competition is usually dated to 1897 near Stockholm, organized under Major Ernst Killander — and spread to Norway and the other Nordic countries in the early 20th century (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The International Orienteering Federation was founded in Copenhagen in 1961 by ten nations — Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, Finland, Hungary, Norway, Poland, Sweden, Switzerland and West Germany — and the first World Orienteering Championships were held in 1966 in Outokumpu, Finland (IOF, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The sport's most celebrated early champion was Sweden's Annichen Kringstad, who won six individual world titles between 1978 and 1989, followed by the Swiss Simone Niggli-Luder with 23 WOC gold medals — a record she shares with compatriot Tove Alexandersson's 23 titles — while on the men's side France's Thierry Gueorgiou (ten world titles), Norway's Olav Lundanes and the Swiss Daniel Hubmann and Matthias Kyburz defined the modern era; Hubmann became the oldest WOC gold medalist at age 40 in 2023 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The sprint discipline was added to the WOC program in 2001 and the mixed sprint relay in 2022, transforming the championship into the alternating sprint/forest format used since 2019 (IOF, n.d.-c).
