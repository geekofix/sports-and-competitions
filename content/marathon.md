## Basic Information

The marathon is the classic road race over 42.195 km (26 miles 385 yards), run on city streets or country roads, governed and record-ratified by World Athletics (World Athletics, n.d.-c). The men's world record is 1:59:30, set by Sabastian Sawe of Kenya at the 2026 London Marathon — the first officially ratified sub-two-hour marathon, with Yomif Kejelcha second in 1:59:41 at the same race; the previous record was the late Kelvin Kiptum's 2:00:35 from Chicago 2023 (Olympics, n.d.; World Athletics, n.d.-b). The women's world record is 2:11:53, set by Tigst Assefa of Ethiopia (pictured) at Berlin 2023; Assefa also holds the women-only record, which she set at the 2026 London Marathon, and she won the 2026 Berlin Marathon in 2:11:04 despite an Achilles rupture (World Athletics, n.d.-c; Wikipedia, n.d.). At the end of the 2026 season the women's marathon was led by Assefa, Sifan Hassan of the Netherlands — the Paris 2024 Olympic champion — and Amane Beriso, while the men's race featured Sawe (pictured) and Kejelcha (World Athletics, n.d.-b).

## Rules

The marathon course must be measured with a Jones counter along the shortest possible route, with start and finish areas, water and refreshment stations approximately every 5 km, and electronic chip timing; pacemakers are permitted under strict World Athletics rules and non-compliant pacing voids record ratification (World Athletics, n.d.-a). World records are ratified by World Athletics; elite marathons carry World Athletics Label status (Platinum, Gold, Silver, Bronze), the six Abbott World Marathon Majors form the top series, and the Olympic marathon is held every four years (World Athletics, n.d.-b; World Athletics, n.d.-c). Men and women race separately or together — mixed and women-only records are kept — and elite races include wheelchair and age-group divisions (World Athletics, n.d.-a).

## History

The marathon takes its name from the legend of Pheidippides, who ran from Marathon to Athens in 490 BC; the modern race was fixed at 42.195 km at the 1908 London Olympics, when the start was set at Windsor Castle so the royal children could watch (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Abebe Bikila of Ethiopia (pictured) won barefoot at Rome 1960 and repeated in Tokyo 1964; Emil Zátopek of Czechoslovakia won the 1952 Helsinki hat-trick of 5000 m, 10,000 m and marathon (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Eliud Kipchoge of Kenya set the world record of 2:01:09 at Berlin 2018 and ran 1:59:40.2 in Vienna 2019 — the first sub-two, though not record-eligible — before Kelvin Kiptum's 2:00:35 in Chicago 2023 and finally Sawe's 1:59:30 at London 2026 (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). On the women's side, Joan Benoit won the first Olympic women's marathon at Los Angeles 1984, Paula Radcliffe set 2:15:25 at London 2003, Brigid Kosgei ran 2:14:04 at Chicago 2019, and Assefa broke the record with 2:11:53 in Berlin 2023 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
