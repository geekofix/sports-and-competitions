## Basic Information

Ski ballet \u2014 known in its later competitive years as acroski \u2014 was a freestyle skiing discipline in which athletes performed a choreographed routine of spins, jumps, flips and pole plants on a smooth slope, set to music and scored by judges like figure skating on skis. It was part of the professional freestyle tours of the 1970s and 1980s, an official FIS discipline and an Olympic demonstration sport at Calgary 1988 and Albertville 1992, before disappearing from international competition by 2000.

## Rules

A routine lasted about 90 seconds to two minutes, performed on a groomed gentle slope to music of the skier's choice, combining spins (including 360s and more), pole plants and flips, rolls, leg crossings and jumps \u2014 with poles used both as props and as pivot points for spins. A panel of judges scored the performance on criteria similar to figure skating: difficulty, composition and choreography, style and execution, with deductions for breaks in rhythm or falls; for a time in the 1980s pair ballet events existed, adding lifts and synchronised movement to the repertoire. Skiers wore standard freestyle equipment of the era \u2014 short twin-tipped skis did not yet exist, and most ballet skis were short, soft and symmetrical \u2014 and the discipline was treated as the artistic third of professional freestyle skiing, alongside aerials and moguls.

## History

Ski ballet grew out of the "hot-dogging" era of freestyle skiing in the late 1960s and early 1970s, a counterculture revolt against alpine racing in which style \u2014 not gates \u2014 was the point; Waterville Valley in New Hampshire, site of the first freestyle instruction programme in 1969 and the first National Open Championships of Freestyle Skiing in 1970, was its cradle, and performers such as Suzy "Chapstick" Chaffee became its first stars, with Genia Fuller winning all four disciplines at the 1974 Freestyle Championship. The International Ski Federation (FIS) recognised freestyle skiing in 1979 and ran ballet as an official discipline in the Freestyle World Cup and the 1986 first Freestyle World Ski Championships; the sport was a demonstration event at the Calgary 1988 and Albertville 1992 Winter Olympics \u2014 Lane Spina medalled at both \u2014 but its failure to be upgraded to medal status, together with the rise of snowboarding among young skiers, caused a sharp decline after 1992. Renamed acroski in the 1990s in an effort to legitimise the discipline, it nonetheless stopped on the World Cup circuit after 1999 and vanished when the FIS ceased formal competition in 2000 \u2014 though its DNA survives in the spins, flips and butters of modern slopestyle and freestyle skiing, and in Norwegian champions like Rune Kristiansen it had a final generation of true virtuosos.
