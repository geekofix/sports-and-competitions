## Basic Information

Matkot (the name comes from the Hebrew matka, "paddle") is a beach paddleball game played mainly in Israel, where it is often called the national beach sport (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; CBC News, n.d.). Two or more players — men and women alike — stand on the sand and hit a small rubber ball back and forth with solid wooden paddles, trying to keep the rally going for as many shots as possible (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The exact origin of the word is debated (Haaretz, n.d.). Closely related games are played elsewhere: frescobol in Brazil and kadima in the United States use similar paddles and rally rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Because the classic game has no winners or losers, it is as much a social ritual as a sport — on Tel Aviv beaches the sound of the paddle on the ball is ubiquitous in summer, and women play as often as men (CBC News, n.d.; Haaretz, n.d.) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Matkot is the beach paddle-rally game of the wall/paddle family — [Paddleball](paddleball.html), [American Handball](american-handball.html), [Beach Tennis](beach-tennis.html), [Basque Pelota](basque-pelota.html) — listed under Important Links.

## Rules

Matkot has no formal rulebook, points or winners in its classic form: the cooperative goal is to keep the ball rallying as long as possible, and play stops only to chase missed balls (Haaretz, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Equipment is simple and standardized by custom rather than regulation: paddles are traditionally hollow wood with a short handle and a round head about 30 cm in diameter, though modern paddles are also made of carbon fiber, and some makers even built paddles from aircraft alloys (Haaretz, n.d.). The standard ball is the same small rubber ball used in squash; beginners sometimes use a lighter, bouncier ball (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Players must keep a safe distance — by beach etiquette about 40 m — from lifeguard stations (Haaretz, n.d.). Since 2000, informal Israeli competitions have been organized, capped by a national championship in Tel Aviv held each summer, where rally-count formats are used (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men's and women's players use identical equipment and play by the same rally rules (CBC News, n.d.).

## History

Matkot has been played on the beaches of Tel Aviv since the 1920s, during the British Mandate period; a 1932 painting by the Israeli artist Nahum Gutman shows two young men with rounded paddles hitting a ball on a Tel Aviv beach, the earliest visual documentation (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Times of Israel, n.d.). No one knows who invented the game: some players trace it to Jewish immigrants from Libya, others to Poland — one story derives the word matkot from the Polish matka, "mother," as in the smack a mother gives (Haaretz, n.d.). The game became a fixture of Israeli beach culture, with unofficial leagues at every beach, nicknames for playing styles such as Bat Yam power-hitting, and a small matkot museum in Tel Aviv run by veteran player Morris Zadok, who teaches the game at the Wingate Institute (Haaretz, n.d.; Hadassah Magazine, n.d.). The Brazilian cousin frescobol emerged independently on the beaches of Rio de Janeiro in the 1940s–1950s, differing mainly in using teardrop-shaped rackets and a louder, solid ball (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
