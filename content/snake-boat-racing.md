## Basic Information

Snake boat racing is the great regatta tradition of Kerala in India: crews of about 100 rowers per chundan vallam (snake boat) race long cedar war canoes to the beat of the boat's drummer and songs of the oarsmen, during the monsoon festival season from July to September. The boats, up to 43 metres long and carrying over a hundred crew including singers and helmsmen, are treated as village icons.

The most famous meetings include the Champakulam Moolam Boat Race, the Aranmula Uthrittathi regatta and the Nehru Trophy Boat Race at Alappuzha, alongside the government-backed Champions Boat League series.

## Rules

Races are held over set distances (commonly about 1,100 to 1,400 metres on the Punnamada lake course for the Nehru Trophy) between boats of defined classes — the chundan snake boats being the largest — with lanes and heats according to each regatta's rules. The crew combines rowers in two rows with the amarakkaran (helmsman) at the stern, the nilakkar (oarsmen's timekeepers calling rhythm) and vanchipattu singers setting stroke; a false start or lane breach is penalized by the regatta committees, and trophies carry prestige and prize money for the winning villages, whose boats are rebuilt and worshipped between seasons.

## History

The snake boat tradition is tied to Kerala's river life and temple festivals, with the Champakulam Moolam race tracing its origin story to the transport of an idol in the 16th century, and the chundan Vallanghi boats facing off at village regattas for generations. The Nehru Trophy, visited by Jawaharlal Nehru in 1952 and awarded from 1954 onwards, made Alappuzha the racing capital, and television coverage turned the August final into a national spectacle. With the Champions Boat League the sport now has a league season besides the classic regattas, keeping the vallam kali alive as both festival and competition.
