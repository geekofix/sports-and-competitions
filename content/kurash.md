## Basic Information

Kurash is the traditional belt-wrestling style of Uzbekistan, practiced for more than 3,500 years and the national sport of Uzbekistan. Two wrestlers (kurashchi) in the yakhtak — a blue or green jacket, white trousers and a red belt — fight standing on a mat (gilam) and try to throw each other to the ground; the winner is the one whose opponent touches the mat with his back, shoulders and part of the hip or blade. The International Kurash Association (IKA) governs the sport internationally, and kurash has been part of the Asian Games program since 2007.

## Rules

Under IKA rules, a bout is fought standing, using grips on the opponent's jacket and belt (belbogli kurash); strikes, kicks and holds below the belt are prohibited. A wrestler wins by throwing the opponent so that he lands on his back with full contact (the "tush" — back, shoulder blades and part of the hip touching the mat), or by the referee's decision for a clean, decisive technique. Matches are held in weight categories on the gilam, judged by a panel of three referees; the bout time is typically a few minutes of active fighting, and a contestant may also win by the opponent's disqualification. Competitors bow (tazim) before and after the bout.

## History

Wrestling has been part of Uzbek life since antiquity — Bronze Age finds in ancient Bactria depict wrestlers — and the belt form of kurash developed over centuries, with legendary champions such as Pahlavon Mahmud and Sadik Polvon of the 14th-16th centuries celebrated in epic poetry. During the Soviet era the sport was suppressed, but after independence in 1991 the International Kurash Association was founded and new international rules, uniforms and refereeing were standardized. Kurash was added to the Asian Games program and the Asian Indoor Games (2007), and World Kurash Championships are held regularly, with Uzbekistan, Iran, Mongolia and Central Asian nations among the strongest.
