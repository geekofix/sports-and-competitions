## Basic Information

Kayaking is paddling a narrow, closed-deck boat (a kayak, from the Inuit qajaq) with a double-bladed paddle, and covers several competitive disciplines: sprint (flatwater racing over 200 m to 5,000 m), slalom (racing down a whitewater course through hanging gates), kayak cross (mass-start down a course with obstacles), marathon and canoe polo. The International Canoe Federation (ICF), founded in 1924, is the world governing body, and sprint and slalom have been Olympic disciplines since 1936 and 1972 respectively.

## Rules

In sprint kayaking, athletes race in single (K1), double (K2) or four-person (K4) kayaks over 200, 500, 1,000 or 5,000 m in marked lanes; false starts are penalized and the fastest boat wins, with heats, semifinals and finals at major championships. In canoe slalom, paddlers negotiate a course of 18-25 hanging gates on a whitewater river or artificial course; touching a gate adds a 2-second penalty and missing a gate adds 50 seconds, and the lowest total time wins — the race includes a timed penalty system and can involve a semifinal and final. Kayak cross is a mass-start race down a course with gates, jumps and obstacles, first across the line wins. All athletes wear buoyancy aids and helmets in whitewater disciplines.

## History

The kayak was developed by Inuit hunters of the Arctic for hunting on the sea. Modern recreational canoeing and kayaking were popularized in the 19th century by the Scottish solicitor John MacGregor, who founded the London Canoe Club in 1866 and toured Europe in his kayak Rob Roy, writing about his journeys. Sprint canoeing debuted at the 1936 Berlin Olympics, and slalom joined the program at Munich 1972. The ICF, founded in 1924, now governs canoe sprint, slalom, marathon, polo, dragon boat and wildwater racing worldwide. Olympic and world champions include Hungary's Danuta Kozák and Katie Ledecky of the US in longer distances.
