## Basic Information

Trap shooting — officially ISSF Olympic trap, also called international trap or bunker trap — is the clay-target shotgun discipline in which shooters take turns on five stations in front of a single "trap house" and engage clay pigeons launched away from them at an unknown horizontal angle, with one shot allowed per target and a hit decided by a visible break of the target (International Shooting Sport Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The discipline is governed worldwide by the International Shooting Sport Federation (ISSF), founded on 17 July 1907 in Zurich by seven national federations and headquartered in Munich; ISSF world championships date back to 1897, and the ISSF runs a World Cup series plus the ISSF World Ranking (International Shooting Sport Federation, n.d.). Trap has been on the Olympic programme for men since 1900 (absent in 1904, 1928 and 1932–1948, contested at every Games since 1952); it was nominally open to women from 1968 to 1992, men-only in 1996, and a separate women's event was added at Sydney 2000 (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The current leading women include Guatemala's Adriana Ruano, who won Olympic gold at Paris 2024 with a new Olympic record and Guatemala's first-ever Olympic gold medal, and the leading men include Britain's Nathan Hales, the Paris 2024 Olympic champion with an Olympic-record 48 of 50 (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).

## Rules

Under the ISSF Shotgun Rules, a trap qualification consists of 125 targets shot in five series of 25, with squads rotating through the five shooting stations and each shooter firing five shots per station (International Shooting Sport Federation, n.d.). The trap machine releases a 110 mm clay target at an unpredictable angle within a fixed horizontal arc, travelling at over 100 km/h, and the shooter may mount the gun and fire only once per target; a target visibly broken scores a hit, and all shooters use shotguns of no more than 12 gauge with shell shot loads limited by the rules (International Shooting Sport Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Since the 2018 format change, the six best from qualification advance to a 25-target semifinal, followed by medal matches for gold/silver and bronze (Wikipedia, n.d.). Safety rules require eye and ear protection, and the gun must be held in the prescribed low "hold point" before the call for the target (International Shooting Sport Federation, n.d.). Men and women shoot the same 125-target qualification at the Olympic Games — the ISSF equalized the number of targets for both genders — and women have had their own Olympic trap event since Sydney 2000 (Olympics, n.d.; International Shooting Sport Federation, n.d.). At world cups, world championships and the Olympics the ISSF also stages mixed team trap events (International Shooting Sport Federation, n.d.).

## History

Trap shooting descends from live-pigeon shooting: glass-ball targets appeared in the 1870s and the first clay targets replaced live birds around 1875–1880, with the Inanimate Bird Shooting Association founded in England in 1893 and renamed the Clay Bird Shooting Association in 1903; a 1921 British act made it illegal to shoot birds released from traps (Wikipedia, n.d.). Trap was contested with live pigeons at the 1900 Paris Olympics and remained on the programme through 1924, returning in 1952 with standardized clay targets, squads and the fixed trap house (Wikipedia, n.d.). The ISSF was founded on 17 July 1907 in Zurich and took the name International Shooting Sport Federation at its 1998 Barcelona General Assembly; the first world championships were held in 1897 in Lyon and are counted as the start of a continuous series (International Shooting Sport Federation, n.d.). Historic icons include Italy's Angelo Scalzone, Olympic trap champion at Melbourne 1956, and America's Donald Haldeman, who won Olympic trap gold at Munich 1972 and Montreal 1976 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Among the women, Italy's Jessica Rossi won Olympic gold at London 2012 plus multiple ISSF world titles before retiring from elite competition (Wikipedia, n.d.). The separate women's Olympic event was decided at the 1998 ISSF General Assembly in Barcelona and debuted at Sydney 2000 (International Shooting Sport Federation, n.d.).
