## Basic Information

Pitz is the Classical Maya (epigraphic) name of the Mesoamerican ballgame, popularly called pok-ta-pok in Yucatán: teams on an I-shaped stone ballcourt strike a heavy solid rubber ball (3–4 kg, from Castilla elastica latex) with hips, forearms, elbows, knees — hands and feet forbidden in the classic form — trying to pass it through a high stone ring (tlachtemalacatl) on the court wall or keep it in play. Played since at least 1650 BC, it was a ritual contest tied to cosmology (the Hero Twins of the Popol Vuh); FIFA recognizes it as the oldest historical precursor of football. Modern revivals (pok-ta-pok tournaments since 2015, Chichén Itzá) and the Sinaloan ulama keep the rubber-ball tradition alive. Within the pelota, wall-ball and handball games on this site, its closest relatives are [Ulama](ulama.html), [Pelota Mixteca](pelota-mixteca.html), [Pelota Tarasca](pelota-tarasca.html), [Mesoamerican Ballgame](mesoamerican-ballgame.html); the related pages are listed under Important Links (Maya Archaeologist, n.d.).

## Rules

Ancient rules are not fully known; the modern pok-ta-pok revival (ACCDAPM World Cup since 2015 in Yucatán/Guatemala/Belize) plays on a court or flat ground with a rubber ball: hip/forearm strikes only (hands/feet barred in classic rules), a point for passing the ball through the stone ring or into the end zone; teams of 3–7, substitution allowed. Ulama (Sinaloa) uses end zones (rayas) instead of rings; ulama de cadera (hip, 3–4 kg ball, 5+ players), de antebrazo (forearm, 1–3 players, women often play) and de mazo (6–7 kg two-handed paddle, 500 g ball) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

Ballcourts and rubber balls date to the Olmec Gulf (rubber balls c. 1600 BC, ballplayer figures c. 1200 BC, ~1,500 courts found from Mexico to Nicaragua). The Classic Maya called it pitz (pokolpok in Colonial Yucatec; Frans Blom coined pok-ta-pok in 1932 from the sound). Spanish Catholic authorities suppressed it after the conquest; it survived in Sinaloa as ulama (AJUPEME association; INAH seeks UNESCO intangible-heritage status). The Great Ball Court at Chichén Itzá is the largest surviving (168 × 70 m) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
