## Basic Information

Skiing is the family of snow travel and competition sports using long runners — skis — attached to the boots, spanning everything from piste recreation to the Olympic disciplines of alpine skiing, cross-country skiing, ski jumping, freestyle skiing and snowboarding's skiing equivalents. As organized sport it is governed internationally by the International Ski and Snowboard Federation (FIS), founded in 1924, which oversees racing, freestyle, jumping and nordic competition through World Cup circuits and world championships; as recreation, skiing is one of the world's most popular winter pastimes, with ski areas on every continent.

## Rules

Competitive skiing splits broadly in two: nordic skiing — cross-country racing (skating and classic technique over sprint and distance courses, from sprints to 50 km), ski jumping and nordic combined — and alpine racing's disciplines: slalom and giant slalom (two-run "technical" events over tightly or moderately set gates), super-G and downhill (single-run "speed" events on courses reaching 130\u2013160 km/h in downhill), plus alpine combined (one speed run plus one slalom run). Freestyle skiing adds moguls, aerials, halfpipe, slopestyle, big air and ski cross; equipment is regulated discipline by discipline — ski lengths and radius minimums, helmet standards, and suit rules — and race scoring is by hundredths of a second. Recreational skiing is graded by piste difficulty (green/blue/red-black slope systems), and ski touring and off-piste skiing extend the sport beyond the lifts into unpatrolled terrain.

## History

Skiing began as transport: rock carvings and archaeological finds — including a 4,000-plus-year-old ski from Hoting in Sweden — show Scandinavians and northern peoples hunting and travelling on skis for millennia, with the word "ski" itself from Old Norse. Modern sport began in Norway in the mid-nineteenth century, where Sondre Norheim's willow-and-birch bindings from Telemark allowed the first true turns, and where the first public competitions of the 1860s\u20131880s (Huseby 1875, Holmenkollen 1892) cemented jumping and cross-country racing; Norwegian emigrants carried skiing across Europe and the Americas — the word slalom itself comes from the Norwegian for a gently sloping track. Alpine skiing developed in the 1890s\u20131920s in the Alps as mountain railways opened the high slopes to tourists, with British pioneers running the first slalom at M\u00fcrren in 1922, and the sport's institutions followed quickly: the FIS was founded in 1924, the first Olympic nordic events were staged at Chamonix 1924 (with alpine events added at Garmisch-Partenkirchen 1936), the Alpine World Cup began in 1966\u20131967, and freestyle, snowboarding, ski cross, halfpipe, slopestyle and women's ski jumping joined the Olympic programme across the decades since. Today skiing is a global industry as well as sport — hundreds of millions of skier days per year — and its professional racing stars, from Ingemar Stenmark's slalom record 86 wins to Mikaela Shiffrin's all-time mark, are among winter sport's most celebrated athletes.
