## Basic Information

Lifesaving (lifesaving sport) is the competitive side of lifeguarding: pool and open-water disciplines that test the swimming, rescue and first-aid skills used in real rescues (International Life Saving Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It is governed worldwide by the International Life Saving Federation (ILS), founded on 27 March 1910 in Paris, which organizes the Lifesaving World Championships (LWC) every two years — the LWC2026 is scheduled for 25 November to 13 December 2026 in Gqeberha (Port Elizabeth), South Africa, with pool events at Newton Park and surf events at Kings Beach (International Life Saving Federation, n.d.-b). Lifesaving has been contested at the World Games since 2001, and in April 2024 the ILS together with Surf Life Saving Australia announced a campaign to include beach lifesaving as an optional sport at the Brisbane 2032 Olympics (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Pool events include the 50 m manikin carry, 100 m rescue medley, 200 m obstacle swim, 100 m manikin tow with fins, 100 m manikin carry with fins, the 200 m super lifesaver and the 4 × 25 m manikin relay; surf events include the 200 m surf race, the 2 km run-swim-run, board and ski races, the beach sprint, beach flags and the ironman/oceanman medley (International Life Saving Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The fastest women in the world hold the open world records — Ying Lu of China swam the 200 m obstacle in 2:01.88 (2009) and Germany's Nina Holt the 100 m rescue medley in 1:03.69 at Chengdu in August 2025 — and women compete in every pool and surf discipline at the World Championships (International Life Saving Federation, n.d.-c).

## Rules

Pool events are timed races in lanes: the swimmer dives to retrieve a submerged manikin, tows it with a rescue tube or carries it, and swims under immersed obstacles in the 200 m obstacle swim; the 200 m super lifesaver combines a 75 m swim to a submerged manikin, a tow of one manikin and a tow of a second (International Life Saving Federation, n.d.-a). Surf events are timed on the beach and in the ocean: the surf race is a 200 m course around buoys, beach flags is a 400 m zigzag sprint to a flag in the sand, the beach sprint is 100 m, and the ironman/oceanman is a medley of surf ski, board, swimming and a beach run (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Relays combine four athletes, and the line throw is judged on accuracy of a thrown rescue line to a target in the water (International Life Saving Federation, n.d.-a). All events are gender-categorized men/women with identical distances and equipment; records can only be set at ILS-sanctioned events, and open, youth (15–18) and masters (30+) record lists are published by the ILS (International Life Saving Federation, n.d.-c). Penalties apply for false starts, incorrect manikin handling and lane infringements (International Life Saving Federation, n.d.-a).

## History

The lifesaving movement began in the late 19th century as volunteer lifesaving societies formed on busy beaches and rivers — the first lifesaving organization is usually dated to the Royal National Lifeboat Institution era in Britain and the American lifesaving services of the 1890s — and competitive lifesaving emerged from the skills contests of those clubs (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The ILS was founded in Paris on 27 March 1910 to coordinate international lifesaving, and the first international competitions followed in the 1920s–1930s (International Life Saving Federation, n.d.-b). Among the movement's icons is George Freeth, the Hawaiian-American "father of modern lifesaving," who demonstrated rescue techniques on the US mainland from 1907, and Duke Kahanamoku, the Hawaiian Olympic swimming champion who popularized lifesaving demonstrations worldwide in the 1910s–1920s (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Lifesaving entered the World Games program in 2001 at Akita, Japan, and women have been world champions and record holders since the early international meets — Karla Gilbert of Australia won the women's surf race at the 2001 World Games (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
