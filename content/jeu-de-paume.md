## Basic Information

Jeu de paume ("palm game", known in English as real tennis or court tennis) is the ancestor of modern tennis: a ball game played in an indoor court with a net, sloping roofs (the penthouse), a tambour and galleries, using a small ball and a wooden racket. Played in France since the Middle Ages and at its height from the 14th to the 18th century, it was the "game of kings" — Henry VIII and Charles V were keen players — and remains alive today in a handful of historic courts. The modern game is governed in France by the Fédération Française de Jeu de Paume and internationally by the International Real Tennis Association (IRTA).

## Rules

Real tennis is played on an asymmetric court about 29 m long and 10 m wide, divided by a net, with a sloping penthouse along one side and a cylindrical tambour at one end. Serves must land in the service box, and a let cord (net) can be won by hitting the ball so it touches the net tape and falls back. Points are scored as in tennis — 15, 30, 40, deuce and advantage — but with the special "chase" system: a line marked across the court, and when the serve or a shot lands beyond it, the chase moves and play continues from there. A set is won by the first to six games (with a tie-break at five all), and matches are usually best of five sets. Handicap scoring is also used in informal play.

## History

Jeu de paume was played with the bare hand in medieval France, later with gloves, then paddle bats, and from the late 17th century with strung racquets, after which the name "tennis" was adopted in England. It was a medal sport at the 1908 London Olympics, won by American Jay Gould II. The Real Tennis World Championship has been held every year since 1740, making it the oldest active trophy in international sport. Famous courts include the Royal Tennis Court at Hampton Court Palace in England and the Jeu de Paume in Paris, where on June 20, 1789 the deputies of the Third Estate took the Tennis Court Oath, a founding act of the French Revolution. Today fewer than 50 real tennis courts survive, and the sport is kept alive by clubs in France, the UK, the US and Australia.
