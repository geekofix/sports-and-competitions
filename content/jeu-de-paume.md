## Basic Information

Jeu de paume — real tennis in English — is the original racket-and-ball game from which modern lawn tennis developed: two or four players hit a ball over a net in a walled court about 30 × 12 m (100 × 40 ft), using the walls, floor and roof features to keep the ball in play in complex rallies (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The game originated in 12th-century France, where monks played with the palm of the hand, and rackets appeared in the late 17th century; it was an Olympic medal sport at the 1908 London Games, won by the American Jay Gould II (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Today only about 45 real tennis courts remain worldwide — roughly 27 in the United Kingdom, 10 in the United States, 3 in France and 6 in Australia — and the Real Tennis World Championship has been contested every year since 1740, making it the oldest active championship trophy in sport (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The sport is governed by the Comité Français de Courte Paume in France, the Tennis and Rackets Association in the UK and the International Real Tennis Professionals Association (IRTPA), and a new court opened in Pau, France, in 2026 (Tennis and Rackets Association, n.d.). At the top of the women's game in 2026, Claire Fahey of the United Kingdom — a multiple world champion — and Australia's Judith Clarke, the first Australian woman to win the world title, lead the field, while on the men's side Robert Fahey, world champion twelve times between 1994 and 2016, remains the most decorated player in history (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

## Rules

A real tennis court is about 30 × 12 m (100 × 40 ft) with high walls, a net across the middle, and distinctive features: the penthouse (a sloping roof on the long walls), the dedans (end-wall openings), the tambour (a sloped stone at the service end), the grille and the galleries (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Players may use the walls and roof: the ball may bounce once on any surface, but a second bounce on the floor ends the rally — except on the dedans and tambour, where a second bounce is allowed (the "chase") (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Scoring is unique: points are love, 15, 30, 40, deuce and advantage; a set is six games, and because of the chase system a game can theoretically never end if both players keep chasing (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The serve must land in the diagonal service box and is taken alternately from behind each end; a serve that hits the net cord and lands in ("a let") is replayed, and a serve fault gives the receiver the choice of taking the serve again or the point (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Matches are best of five sets for men and best of three for women at the world championship (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Men and women compete in separate world championships; the women's world championship has been held since the 1980s, with Claire Fahey and Judith Clarke among its leading champions (Tennis and Rackets Association, n.d.). Players wear white clothing on court (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## History

Jeu de paume originated in 12th-century French monasteries, where monks hit a ball with the palm of the hand; rackets came into use in the late 17th century, and enclosed courts with nets, galleries and the characteristic penthouse were built for royalty (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Henry VIII played at his palace at Hampton Court, whose court, built around 1530, still exists, and James V of Scotland played at Falkland Palace in the 1530s (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The game was an Olympic medal sport at the 1908 London Olympics, won by Jay Gould II of the USA, and was a demonstration sport again in 1924 and 1968 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The Real Tennis World Championship has been played annually since 1740 at rotating venues, making its trophy the oldest in continuous competition in world sport (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Among the icons are Pierre Etchebaster, the great French champion who held the world title from 1928 to 1954, Robert Fahey, twelve-time world champion from 1994 to 2016, and on the women's side Claire Fahey, multiple world champion, and Judith Clarke of Australia, the first Australian woman to win the title (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
