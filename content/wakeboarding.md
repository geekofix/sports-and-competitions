## Basic Information

Wakeboarding is the water sport of riding a buoyant board behind a tow boat: riders are pulled at roughly 18 to 24 mph, carving wake-to-wake, launching airs and inverts off the boat's wake to land tricks that mix surfing, snowboarding and gymnastics. The dominant towed board sport worldwide (and an X Games and world-championship staple with cable-park and boat disciplines), it grew out of the surfer-behind-a-boat experiments of the 1980s into today's professional circuits.

## Rules

Competition formats split into boat and cable wakeboarding: boat events score a rider's two passes behind the boat (judged on execution, intensity and composition of a run of tricks with higher difficulty scoring more), with riders selecting lines across the wake to land spins, inverts and mobes; the cable format (a seated winch and tower system pulling riders around a lake) adds obstacles (kickers and rails) that the rider hits, slides and inverts over the features. Judging rewards difficulty, amplitude and style per trick, with falls terminating a pass; rules govern rope length, boat speed and wake shape in competition, and events run divisions by age and gender under the international federation's world championship structure.

## History

Wakeboarding emerged in the 1980s as surfers (notably in Australia, New Zealand and the United States) began riding boards behind boats, with the first purpose-built skurfer boards blending surf and ski; the sport was branded wakeboarding in the early 1990s (with double-ended boards and the first world championships in the mid-1990s), and the X Games era made its stars international figures. The 2000s and 2010s saw cable parks boom worldwide (making the sport far more accessible and urban, with millions of towed wake sessions logged by amateur riders), the professional circuits consolidated under the international federation, and today's riding pushes the trick count toward snowboarding's freestyle vocabulary while wake surfing has grown as its towed-adjacent sibling.
