## Basic Information

Cornish hurling — *Hurlian* in Cornish — is an ancient outdoor team game played only in Cornwall, England, with a small silver ball; it is completely different from the Irish game of hurling despite the shared name, and is considered by many Cornish people as their national game alongside Cornish wrestling (BBC News, 2018; Wikipedia, n.d.). Today the game survives in two communities: at St Columb Major, where the traditional matches are played on Shrove Tuesday and the second Saturday following — in 2026 on 17 February, starting at 4:30 pm — between the Townsmen and the Countrymen of the parish; and at St Ives, where children play on Feast Monday, with a further version in the beating of the bounds at Bodmin roughly every five years (Wikipedia, n.d.). The two goals at St Columb lie about two miles (3 km) apart at either end of the town — the country goal is a granite trough in a field (pictured below), the town goal an ancient Celtic cross — and because a side can also win by carrying the ball across the parish boundary, the pitch is effectively the whole parish, some twenty square miles, the largest playing field of any ball game in the world (Visit Newquay, n.d.). The ball itself is a cricket-ball-sized sphere of applewood hammered into two hemispheres of sterling silver, weighing 19–21 ounces (about 570 g) with a circumference of about 9 inches; the current St Columb ball bears the inscription "Town and Country, do your best", the winner keeps the ball for the year but must commission a replacement costing around £1,000, and since the 1990s new balls have been made by the local craftsman Colin Rescorla, who took over from John Turver (Wikipedia, n.d.). After the match the winning side visits every pub in town and the ball is dipped in gallon jugs of beer — the "silver beer" ceremony. The St Columb game is played by men; the St Ives children's game is mixed, and women participate in the associated festivities (Wikipedia, n.d.; Visit Newquay, n.d.).

## Rules

There is no written code — the game is governed by custom handed down for centuries. At St Columb Major the two sides, the Townsmen (men of the town) and the Countrymen (men of the rural parish), assemble in the Market Square, where, after three cheers, the ball is thrown up in the air to start play (Cornish Times, 2025; Wikipedia, n.d.). Players may carry, pass, throw, snatch and tackle for the ball through the streets — still open to traffic — and across private property such as gardens, fields, houses and pubs, with shopkeepers barricading doors and windows against damage; spectators may touch the ball, which is said to bring luck or fertility, and play often pauses to let younger players join in (Visit Newquay, n.d.). After about an hour the ball is hurled toward the respective goals about two miles apart; a side wins by placing the ball in its own goal — for the Countrymen the granite trough, for the Townsmen the Celtic cross — or simply by carrying it across the parish boundary (Visit Newquay, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The individual who touches the goal with the ball, the "winner of the ball", keeps the silver ball until the next hurling day, when he throws it up to start the new game; customary calls and terms such as "stand" (to tackle), "shuffle the ball" (to hide it) and "silver beer" form part of the ritual, and rough play is restrained by community custom rather than by officials (Cornish Times, 2025). Because the ball is hard and heavy, players and spectators alike maintain intense vigilance to avoid serious injury from a long or poorly aimed throw (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

Hurling is of Celtic origin and was once played across Cornwall on feast and fair days; by the 17th century it was already documented — Richard Carew's *Survey of Cornwall* (1602) describes "hurling to the country" at St Columb, where the ball was of applewood with a thick coating of silver (Cornish Times, 2025). Games between St Columb Major and Newquay survived into the early 1900s, and today St Columb Major and St Ives are the last two communities where the custom continues unchanged, alongside the Bodmin beating of the bounds (Wikipedia, n.d.). The annual St Columb matches have been held on Shrove Tuesday for some 500 years, starting with the throw-up in the Market Place beside the town hall, and hold the same place in the Cornish calendar as the 'Obby 'Oss at Padstow and the Furry Dance at Helston (Wikipedia, n.d.; Visit Newquay, n.d.). The Cornish saying "hyrlïan yw gen gwaré nyi" — "hurling is our sport" — reflects the game's status as a marker of Cornish identity (Visit Newquay, n.d.). Local historians have preserved the tradition in print: Ivan Rabey (1931–2008) of St Columb Major wrote *Hurling at St Columb and in Cornwall* (1972) and *The Silver Ball: the story of hurling at St Columb* (1984), and the historian Barry West traced the origins of the sport for the Cornish Times in 2025 (Wikipedia, n.d.; Cornish Times, 2025). Historic silver balls are on display at Truro Museum, Lanhydrock House, St Ives Museum, St Agnes Museum and St Columb Major Town Hall; one at Penzance Museum bears a 1704 Cornish inscription that translates roughly as "fair play is good play", and a 1990s St Columb ball is held by the National Football Museum in Manchester as part of the story of football's medieval ancestors (Wikipedia, n.d.).
