## Basic Information

Combat sambo is the full-contact, MMA-like variant of sambo in which, unlike sport sambo, striking is allowed in addition to throws, takedowns, joint locks and chokes (FIAS, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The sport is governed worldwide by FIAS — the International Sambo Federation, founded in Madrid on 13 June 1984, which grew out of the Soviet sambo movement that was officially recognized as a sport in the USSR on 16 November 1938 (FIAS, n.d.). The pinnacle event is the World SAMBO Championships: the landmark 50th edition was held from 6 to 8 November 2026 in Tashkent, Uzbekistan, with a record 101 countries competing and medals awarded in sport sambo, combat sambo and blind/vision-impaired sambo, for men and women (FIAS, 2026a). The cadet, youth and junior world championships followed in Thessaloniki, Greece, in October 2026 (FIAS, 2026a). A sambo athlete in competition gear is pictured below. In 2026 the reigning world champions are the 2026 Tashkent gold medallists in each combat sambo weight class, with the women's champions — competing in their own weight classes under slightly modified rules — equally prominent (FIAS, 2026a).

## Rules

Combat sambo is governed by the FIAS Combat Sambo Rules 2026, approved on 31 March 2026, the official rulebook of the world federation FIAS (sambo.sport) (FIAS, 2026b). In a combat sambo bout of five minutes, fighters may punch, kick, knee, elbow and headbutt, apply chokes and standing submissions, and score throws; a choke that renders the opponent unconscious is an immediate total victory (the choke must be completed within one minute of being locked in), and the bout also ends early on a total throw, a submission, a knockout, two knockdowns or a lead of eight points or more (FIAS, 2026b). Scoring awards four points for a total throw, two for a partial throw and one for a minor technique, plus points for a 20-second hold-down; the fighter with the higher score at the bell wins, with tie-breaks on activity (FIAS, 2026b). Athletes wear headgear, boxing-style gloves, shin guards, a mouthguard and a groin protector, and compete in weight classes separately for men and women; in the women's division knee strikes to the head are prohibited, the explicit gender difference in the rules, while sport sambo — the Olympic-recognized discipline — bans all strikes and chokes (FIAS, 2026b; FIAS, n.d.). Age classifications run cadet, youth, junior and senior, each with its own world championships (FIAS, 2026b).

## History

Sambo was developed in the 1920s–1930s in the Soviet Red Army as a military combatives system, and its three founding figures are Viktor Spiridonov (1883–1956), who codified the self-defense system in the 1920s; Vasili Oshchepkov (1892–1938), a judo student of Jigoro Kano who built the sport-sambo rules; and Viktor Kharlampiyev (1902–1979?), who synthesized the competitive sport in the 1930s (FIAS, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Sambo was officially recognized as a sport in the USSR on 16 November 1938, the first national championships were held in 1939, and after World War II it spread through the Eastern Bloc and worldwide (FIAS, n.d.). FIAS was founded in Madrid on 13 June 1984 and has governed the World SAMBO Championships since; sambo was demonstrated at the Olympics and is recognized by the IOC, though it has never been on the Olympic program (FIAS, n.d.). Combat sambo, with strikes and chokes, developed alongside sport sambo from the 1990s and became the format closest to modern MMA; the 2026 50th World Championships in Tashkent with 101 nations mark the sport's biggest world event to date (FIAS, 2026a). The women's division has grown since the 1980s and now contests full world championship weight classes with the modified (no knee-to-head) ruleset (FIAS, 2026a).
