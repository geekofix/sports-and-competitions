## Basic Information

Triathlon is the multisport race of swim, bike and run completed back to back with the transitions timed in: athletes swim in open water, transition (T1) to the bicycle, ride, transition again (T2) and run to the finish, with the fastest total time winning (World Triathlon, n.d.). The classic distances are the sprint (750 m swim / 20 km bike / 5 km run), the standard or Olympic distance (1.5 km / 40 km / 10 km), half Ironman (1.9 km / 90 km / 21.1 km) and the full Ironman (3.8 km / 180.2 km / 42.2 km marathon), and the sport is governed worldwide by World Triathlon, founded as the International Triathlon Union (ITU) on 1 April 1989 in Avignon (Wikipedia, n.d.; World Triathlon, n.d.). Triathlon made its Olympic debut at Sydney 2000 and the mixed team relay joined the programme at Tokyo 2020 (Olympics, n.d.). Today's leading women include Germany's Lisa Tertsch, who won the 2025 World Triathlon Championship Series at the Grand Final in Wollongong on 19 October 2025, and France's Cassandre Beaugrand, the Paris 2024 Olympic champion; among the men, Australia's Matthew Hauser won the 2025 series title and Britain's Alex Yee is the Paris 2024 Olympic champion (Wikipedia, n.d.; Olympics, n.d.).

## Rules

World Triathlon's Competition Rules define the race distances (sprint 750 m/20 km/5 km, standard 1.5 km/40 km/10 km, and the longer 70.3 and Ironman distances under their own rule sets) and the two drafting regimes: elite Olympic-distance racing is draft-legal within a defined draft zone behind another rider, while long-course and most age-group races are non-drafting, with penalties for riding inside the draft zone (World Triathlon, n.d.). Transitions T1 and T2 are part of the race clock; rules govern racked bikes, helmet use on the bike leg, no nudity in transition, and wetsuit use according to water-temperature thresholds set in the rules (prohibited above the applicable threshold, mandatory in colder water) (World Triathlon, n.d.). Infringements draw penalties from warnings to stop-and-go time penalties to disqualification, enforced by referees and race marshals; bike and helmet checks happen before the start (World Triathlon, n.d.). Formats include individual races, the super-sprint eliminator, the mixed team relay (an Olympic event since Tokyo 2020), age-group and para triathlon; the World Triathlon Championship Series (WTCS) awards the annual world title on cumulative points across the series plus the Grand Final (World Triathlon, n.d.). Men and women race the same courses and distances at the Olympic Games, with equal quota places (Olympics, n.d.).

## History

Versions of swim-bike-run racing existed in France in the 1920s, but the modern sport was born on 25 September 1974 at Mission Bay, San Diego, when Jack Johnstone and Don Shanahan of the San Diego Track Club organized a race over a 5.3-mile run, 5-mile bike and 600-yard swim for 46 entrants — the first men's winner was Bill Phillips and the first women's winner Judy Collins (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The Hawaii Ironman, founded by John and Judy Collins on 18 February 1978 on Oahu with 15 starters (won by Gordon Perry) and moved to Kailua-Kona in 1981, created the sport's legendary long-course distance, and ABC's 1980 broadcast brought it to a mass audience (Wikipedia, n.d.). The European Triathlon Union was founded in 1984, and the International Triathlon Union was founded on 1 April 1989 at its first Congress in Avignon with 30 national federations, electing Canada's Les McDonald as its first president; the first ITU World Championships followed in Avignon in August 1989 with more than 800 athletes from 40 countries, and the Olympic distance was fixed at 1.5 km/40 km/10 km (Wikipedia, n.d.). The IOC added triathlon to the Olympic programme in 1994, and it debuted at Sydney 2000 with gold medals for Canada's Simon Whitfield and Switzerland's Brigitte McMahon (Olympics, n.d.). The World Cup series began in 1991 and the WTCS in 2009; the mixed relay debuted at Tokyo 2020, won by Great Britain (Wikipedia, n.d.). Retired icons include Americans Mark Allen (six Ironman world titles 1989–1995) and Paula Newby-Fraser (eight), Britain's Chrissie Wellington (four Ironman titles 2007–2011) and Alistair Brownlee (Olympic gold 2012 and 2016) (Wikipedia, n.d.).
