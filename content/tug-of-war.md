## Basic Information

Tug of war is the ancient team strength contest in which two teams of eight pull on opposite ends of a rope about 33.5 m (110 ft) long, marked at the centre and 4 m to either side; the pull is won when a team's 4 m mark crosses the centre line (Tug of War International Federation, n.d.). The sport is governed worldwide by the Tug of War International Federation (TWIF), founded in 1960 on the initiative of George Hutton of the Great Britain association together with the Swedish association's Tage Andersson and Rudolf Ullmark (Tug of War International Federation, n.d.). Tug of war was an Olympic sport at five Summer Games from Paris 1900 to Antwerp 1920, has been on the World Games programme since 1981, received IOC recognition in 2002, and today runs biennial world and European championships plus club championships — the 2025 World Tug of War Championships in Nottingham, England, drew over 1,200 athletes from 20 nations, with England winning the Senior Men's 640 kg international title and Chinese Taipei the Senior Women's 540 kg (Wikipedia, n.d.). Women compete in their own weight classes (500 kg and 540 kg outdoors, 480 kg at junior level) and in mixed classes, alongside the men's 560–720 kg classes (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## Rules

Under the TWIF Rules Manual, an outdoor pull is contested by teams of eight pullers plus a coach on a rope at least 33.5 m long with a centre mark and marks 4 m on each side of it; the rope starts with its centre mark over the centre line, and the pull is won when the 4 m mark of the winning team crosses that line (Tug of War International Federation, n.d.). Fouls — sitting on the ground, "locking" the rope against the body, improper grip or improper footing — draw a caution, and a repeated foul disqualifies the team; referees also penalize receiving outside assistance (Tug of War International Federation, n.d.). Finals are decided best-of-three pulls (Wikipedia, n.d.). Competition is organized in weight classes that cap the combined weight of the eight pullers: outdoors men's 560, 640, 680 and 720 kg, women's 500 and 540 kg, mixed 580 and 600 kg, and junior classes at 480 and 560 kg; indoor club championships run 560–680 kg for men, 500–540 kg for women and 580–600 kg mixed (Wikipedia, n.d.). Footwear rules allow cleated boots on grass and smooth-soled shoes on mats, and the anchor man at the back sets the drive with the team pulling in unison behind the coach's call (Tug of War International Federation, n.d.). Women's and men's teams follow identical technical rules — the differences are only the weight-class limits (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

Rope-pulling contests are attested in ancient cultures worldwide — as *helkustinda* in Greece around 500 BC and in Egyptian, Chinese and Indian ritual and training tradition (Tug of War International Federation, n.d.). In nineteenth-century Britain the game received codified rules and club competition, and tug of war entered the Olympic Games at Paris 1900 — where the Danish/Swedish team beat France — and was contested at five Games: 1900, St. Louis 1904 (Milwaukee Athletic Club), London 1908 (City of London Police over Liverpool Police), Stockholm 1912 (Great Britain over Sweden) and Antwerp 1920, after which the IOC removed it from the programme (Wikipedia, n.d.). National associations then formed — Sweden's in 1933, Great Britain's in 1958, the Netherlands' in 1959 — and in 1960 George Hutton of the British association, with Sweden's Tage Andersson and Rudolf Ullmark, founded the TWIF; its first international competition was staged at the Baltic Games in Malmö in 1964 with Great Britain, Sweden, the Netherlands and Denmark (Tug of War International Federation, n.d.). The first European Championship followed in 1965 at Crystal Palace in London, the first World Championship was held in the Netherlands in 1975, the first women's international competition took place in 1986, and tug of war has appeared at every World Games since 1981 (Tug of War International Federation, n.d.). The TWIF received provisional IOC recognition in 1999, confirmed in 2002 under Olympic Charter rule 29, and is a member of ARISF and a founder member of the International World Games Association (Tug of War International Federation, n.d.).
