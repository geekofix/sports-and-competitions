## Basic Information

Model rocketry is the sport and hobby of launching small rockets powered by commercially manufactured solid motors, from palm-sized Estes rockets to multi-metre competition models (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is governed in the US by the National Association of Rocketry (NAR), founded in 1957 by Orville Carlisle and G. Harry Stine, and internationally through the FAI’s spacemodelling classes (NAR, n.d.).

Competition (NAR "sport rocketry" / FAI spacemodelling) covers altitude events (S1), payload (S2), duration with parachute/streamer (S3, S6), boost-glider duration (S4, S8), scale (S5), scale altitude (S7), gyrocopter and flex-wing duration (S9, S10) and helicopter duration (S9) (FAI, n.d.). Rockets are classed by motor impulse from 1/4A (small) up to high-power O motors, and the NAR runs national performance records per motor class (NAR, n.d.).

## Rules

Safety is codified in the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code (and NFPA 1122): launch from a rod or rail with an electric ignition, a clear launch area, spectator distances, and no recovery over crowds; high-power rockets follow NFPA 1127 and require NAR/Tripoli certification levels 1–3 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Motors are commercially tested and certified; homemade motors are banned at NAR launches (Wikipedia, n.d.). All ages fly — youth programs start at the A-motor level; men and women compete together.

## History

Modern model rocketry began with G. Harry Stine’s founding of the NAR in 1957 and Vernon Estes’s Estes Industries (1958), which made safe, reliable commercial motors and democratized the hobby (Wikipedia, n.d.). The FAI added spacemodelling (S classes) to its records in the 20th century (FAI, n.d.). Competition today ranges from the NAR National Championships (Nats) to the Team America Rocketry Challenge (TARC) for school teams.
