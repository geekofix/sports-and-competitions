## Basic Information

Kyūdō is the Japanese art of archery, practiced with the long asymmetrical yumi bow (about 2.21 m) at a 28 m distance toward a 36 cm target, and organized in Japan by the All Nippon Kyudo Federation (ANKF), which was formed in 1949 and counted 135,660 members in 2021 across 47 prefectural federations (All Nippon Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a). Internationally the sport is governed by the International Kyudo Federation (IKYF), founded in May 2006, which now has 32 member nations and organizes the World Kyudo Taikai — held in Tokyo 2010, Paris 2014, Tokyo 2018 and Tokyo 2022 (International Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a). Women are a large and growing share of ANKF membership and compete in every competition, from club championships to the All Japan Kyudo Taikai and the World Taikai, on equal terms with men in both individual and team events (All Nippon Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a). The 2014 Kyudo World Cup in Paris, pictured below, was the second edition of that international team competition (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

## Rules

In the standard 28 m individual format (kinteki), each archer shoots four arrows per turn — two pairs — from the shooting line (shōmen) toward the 36 cm hōsha target, and each arrow that hits the target scores one point; in team matches the team with more hits wins, and in the near-target format (enteki) the closest arrow to the center decides ties (All Nippon Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a). The shooting form is codified in the hassetsu, the eight stages of the shot from the bowing (yugamae) to the release (hanare) and the follow-through (zan Shin) (All Nippon Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a). Equipment is strictly regulated: the yumi is a laminated bamboo-and-wood bow of more than 2.21 m (nominal 221 cm), and arrows (ya) are matched to the archer's draw length and have bamboo shafts with feathers (All Nippon Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a). Gradings run from kyū (student grades) through dan to the titles hanshi, renshi and kyōshi, awarded by ANKF examinations; women hold dan and teaching titles in large numbers and shoot in the same divisions as men, with team events mixed (All Nippon Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Junior and high-school divisions use shorter distances for younger archers (International Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a). Rule source: ANKF Kyudo Kyohon (training manual, first issued 1953), https://www.kyudo.jp/ (All Nippon Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a).

## History

Kyūdō descends from kyūjutsu, the archery of the samurai, which was practiced as a battlefield skill and later as a ceremonial and spiritual discipline; the name kyūdō ("the way of the bow") was officially adopted in 1919 in place of kyūjutsu (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). After World War II the Dai Nippon Butoku Kai's archery section was reorganized, and the All Nippon Kyudo Federation was formed in 1949, publishing the Kyudo Kyohon in 1953 as the standard training text (All Nippon Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a). The International Kyudo Federation was founded in May 2006 with the aim of spreading kyūdō worldwide and bidding for Olympic inclusion, and has since organized the World Kyudo Taikai in Tokyo (2010), Paris (2014), Tokyo (2018) and Tokyo (2022), plus the Kyudo World Cup beginning in 2013 — the second edition was held in Paris in July 2014 (International Kyudo Federation, n.d.-a).
