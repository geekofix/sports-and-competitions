## Basic Information

Gas ballooning is ballooning with hydrogen- or coal-gas-filled balloons — lighter-than-air craft that, unlike hot-air balloons, need no burner and can fly for days; competitive gas ballooning is a distance race (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is governed by the FAI’s ballooning commission (CIA) and is the basis of the Gordon Bennett Cup.

*Figure: Sumitsurai. (2016-12-22). Gas balloon at the Jal Mahotsav festival, India [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. CC BY-SA 3.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Gas_balloon_Jal_Mahotsav.jpg*

FAI gas balloon classes (BA-1 to BA-10) are defined by envelope volume, from under 250 m³ to over 100,000 m³; records for distance, duration and altitude in each class are ratified by the FAI (FAI, n.d.). Competition racing uses small (about 1,000 m³) racing balloons, as in the Gordon Bennett Cup (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## Rules

Pilots need a balloon pilot licence with a gas rating; flight planning must consider airspace, weather and ballast, and night flying is allowed with the required experience (FAI, n.d.). Men and women compete and hold records equally — the 1784 first manned flight was by Jacques Charles himself, and women have held FAI gas balloon records since the early 20th century (Wikipedia, n.d.).

## History

The gas balloon was invented by Professor Jacques Charles in Paris in 1783: on 1 December 1783 he and the Robert brothers flew 35 km from the Tuileries to Nesles-la-Vallée, and Charles then flew alone to 3,000 m (Wikipedia, n.d.). Coal gas from city mains was the lifting gas of the 19th-century "balloonmania", and hydrogen took over for sport and record flying; the Gordon Bennett Cup (1906) made gas balloon racing international (FAI, n.d.). Today gas ballooning survives in a handful of clubs and in festival events such as the Jal Mahotsav in Hanuwantiya, India, where gas balloons fly alongside hot-air balloons (photo above).
