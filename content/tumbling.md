## Basic Information

Tumbling — also called power tumbling — is the acrobatic gymnastics discipline in which an athlete sprints down a 25.6 m (84 ft) sprung track and performs one continuous pass of eight (or five) skills, placing only hands and feet on the track: whip-throughs, back flicks and twisting somersaults that must end in a controlled, stuck landing (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The discipline is governed by World Gymnastics (FIG) together with trampoline and double mini trampoline, and it is contested at the annual FIG World Championships, at the European Games and at the World Games, where it appears as power tumbling (The World Games, n.d.). Tumbling was included in the Olympic programme only once, as a men's event at Los Angeles 1932, and returned as a demonstration sport in 1996 and 2000 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Women and men compete in identical individual and team events on the same apparatus with the same passes and judging; at the most recent FIG World Championships in Pamplona in 2025, Great Britain won the women's team title and France the men's team title (USA Gymnastics, n.d.).

## Rules

Under the FIG Code of Points, the tumbling track is a 25.6 m long, roughly 2 m wide sprung rod floor (or air track) preceded by a run-up of about 10 m, and each pass comprises eight elements at senior elite level (five elements in some age-group categories); only the hands and feet may touch the track, and any other contact, a pause between skills or travel off the track line ends the pass's scoring (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Judges award a difficulty score for the declared skills plus an execution score out of 10 for form, rotation, height and the final stuck landing, and the combined total ranks the athletes; qualification consists of two passes — a compulsory and a voluntary pass — while finals are decided over two voluntary passes, and team events aggregate the passes of three athletes per nation (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.). Competitions are organized in men's and women's individual and team events across senior and junior age groups — the technical rules, apparatus dimensions, pass length and judging are identical for both genders, with no separate women's apparatus or scoring (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.). The world federation is the Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique (FIG), which publishes the Code of Points and runs the world championships alongside the trampoline disciplines (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.).

## History

Acrobatic tumbling is attested in ancient China, India, Japan, Egypt and Iran, entered the education system of ancient Greece and Roman military training, and survived into medieval Europe as minstrel and court entertainment — the 13th-century poem *Our Lady's Tumbler* depicts a tumbling performer, and 1303 is the first attestation of the verb "tumble" in English (Wikipedia, n.d.). In the United States the Amateur Athletic Union included tumbling in its championships from 1886 and added women's tumbling in 1938; the NCAA dropped the event in 1962 but reinstated it as an Emerging Sport for Women in the 2020–21 school year (Wikipedia, n.d.). The modern sprung rod floor was developed by the American coach and performer Randy Mulkey, whose track turned circus tumbling into a standardized competitive apparatus (Wikipedia, n.d.). Internationally, the International Trampoline Federation (FIT), founded in 1964, governed tumbling and held the first world championships from 1964 onward; the FIT merged into the FIG on 1 January 1999, which has governed the discipline ever since (Wikipedia, n.d.). Tumbling was an official Olympic event only at Los Angeles 1932 (men only), a demonstration sport at Atlanta 1996 and Sydney 2000, and has been on the World Games programme since the first Games in 1981 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Among the early internationals, Peter Davies of Wales won bronze in men's tumbling at the 1965 world championships, and China has dominated the women's event since the 2010s, winning four consecutive women's team titles by 2017 (Wikipedia, n.d.).
