## Basic Information

Tumbling is the acrobatic gymnastics discipline of continuous flips and twists performed along a sprung tumble track: athletes race a 25-metre rod floor, launching into long series of whips, flicks, somersaults and twists that finish with a stuck landing. Governed alongside trampoline and double mini trampoline as part of the FIG's acrobatic gymnastics-and-trampoline family, it contests world championships and is staged at the World Games in power tumbling form.

## Rules

A competition routine runs eight skills along a 25-metre sprung track: athletes begin at the run-up and execute continuous elements (whip through to tucked and twisting somersaults) without pause, with difficulty values assigned per skill and execution judges deducting for form faults, travel off the track mat line, or incomplete rotation. The last skill must land in control on both feet, routines are performed twice (a compulsory pass and a voluntary pass) in qualification, finals run two voluntary passes, and the trampolining-family scoring system (difficulty plus execution, with landing bonuses for held landing) decides results, with categories by age and gender under the FIG code of points.

## History

Tumbling's roots are ancient (acrobatic tumbling was practiced in Chinese and Greek antiquity), but its modern sport took shape through gymnastics clubs in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as the artistic gymnastics-floor tradition. The International Trampoline Federation absorbed international tumbling championships in the 1970s (world championships from 1974), and the discipline joined the FIG when the trampoline bodies merged in 1999, contesting world championships alongside trampoline, double mini and acrobatic gymnastics today. Russia and China (with successors like Great Britain building podiums) have been the sport's strongest nations, and its modern disciplines continue to push difficulty: routines of eight skills that would finish an old circus act now lead the way in the acrobatic sports' progression.
