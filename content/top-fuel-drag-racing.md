## Basic Information

Top Fuel drag racing is the fastest class of drag racing and the most powerful category in motorsport: nitromethane-fueled dragsters producing more than 10,000 horsepower launch to cover the 1,000-foot distance in under four seconds at speeds beyond 330 mph (530 km/h). Sanctioned by the NHRA in North America and by similar bodies worldwide, it is a spectacle of acceleration that no other racing machinery approaches.

## Rules

A Top Fuel run covers the modern professional standard distance of 1,000 feet, timed to thousandths of a second from a standing start: two cars run side by side down the straight drag strip with the first to legally cross the finish line winning the round, with red-light starts (leaving before the green) automatically losing. The cars run 90 percent nitromethane fuel, supercharged V8 engines of roughly 500 cubic inches, parachutes to stop the car after the run, and weekly rulebooks govern engine displacement, supercharger, tire and chassis specifications, and safety systems including crash containment. Race weekends run qualifying sessions and elimination ladders, with the field's quickest in pairing order and race-day winners advancing round by round to the final.

## History

Drag racing grew from the postwar dry-lakes and airport sprints of Southern California, and the NHRA organized the sport (and the Top Fuel class by extension) from 1951, with the Top Fuel class becoming the origin of quarter-mile legend: Top Fuelers ran the full quarter mile in increasingly quick times until the sport shortened the professional distance to 1,000 feet for engine and chassis safety in 2008. The class's great eras include the front-engine dragster age, the 4-second barrier era of the 1980s and 1990s, and the modern three-second-plus era, with family dynasties of drivers and crew chiefs. The sport remains the NHRA's flagship, crowned each year at the U.S. Nationals and the season championship.
