Rugby Union

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Rugby union is a full-contact team sport played by two teams of 15 players — eight forwards and seven backs — on a rectangular field up to 100 m long and 70 m wide, with H-shaped goalposts on each goal line; it is governed worldwide by World Rugby, which publishes the Laws of the Game (World Rugby, n.d.). The quadrennial Men's Rugby World Cup is the pinnacle event: South Africa are the reigning champions, having beaten New Zealand 12–11 in the 2023 final at the Stade de France — the first team to win four Webb Ellis Cups — and the 2027 tournament in Australia will expand to 24 teams (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The Women's Rugby World Cup 2025 in England was won by the Red Roses, who beat Canada 33–13 at Twickenham in front of a record crowd on 27 September 2025, their third title (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). France won the 2026 Six Nations, their second consecutive championship, with Thomas Ramos the tournament's top scorer for the fourth straight year (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). The annual Six Nations (since 1883 as the Home Nations) and the Rugby Championship in the southern hemisphere are the leading international competitions (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Rugby union is the 15-a-side parent code of the rugby family on this site — Rugby League, Rugby Sevens, Rugby Tens, Rugby League Sevens, Rugby League Nines, Touch Rugby, Tag Rugby, Beach Rugby, Snow Rugby, Snow Rugby Sevens and Walking Rugby.

Among current leading players, the women's game is headed by England's Zoe Aldcroft and Ellie Kildunne, New Zealand's Portia Woodman-Wickliffe and Canada's Sophie de Goede; on the men's side, France's Antoine Dupont and Thomas Ramos, Ireland's Hugo Keenan and South Africa's Eben Etzebeth headline the world rankings (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; World Rugby, n.d.).

Rules

Rugby union is played under the World Rugby Laws of the Game (2026 edition): 15 players per side, matches of 80 minutes in two 40-minute halves, on a field up to 100 m by 70 m with 10–22 m in-goal areas and goalposts 3 m high with a 5.6 m crossbar (World Rugby, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Scoring: try 5, conversion 2, penalty 3, drop goal 3 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Play is continuous through tackles, with contested set pieces — the eight-man scrum and the line-out — restarting play, plus rucks and mauls; the ball may only be passed backwards and is advanced by running or kicking (World Rugby, n.d.). Foul play is penalized with free kicks, penalty tries, yellow and red cards; the video match official and television review are standard at elite level (World Rugby, n.d.). Men's and women's matches use identical laws, with separate World Cups, Six Nations/Women's Six Nations, and professional club competitions (World Rugby, n.d.); under-19, sevens, tens and wheelchair variants have published law variations (World Rugby, n.d.).

History

According to tradition, rugby was invented in 1823 when William Webb Ellis, a pupil at Rugby School in Warwickshire, picked up the ball during a football match and ran with it (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The Football Association codified the handling of the ball out of the game in 1863, and in 1871 the Rugby Football Union was founded; the first international was England–Scotland in 1871 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The sport split in 1895 when northern English clubs formed the Northern Union over broken-time payments — that code became rugby league (Wikipedia, n.d.-d). The Five Nations Championship began in 1910 (the Six Nations from 1883 origins), and the first Rugby World Cup was held in 1987 in Australia and New Zealand, won by the All Blacks (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Rugby was an Olympic sport from 1900 to 1924. Retired icons include New Zealand's Jonah Lomu, whose four tries against England in the 1995 World Cup semifinal electrified the tournament; Richie McCaw, captain of the 2011 and 2015 world champion All Blacks; Dan Carter, the record points scorer; South Africa's Francois Pienaar, who received the 1995 trophy from Nelson Mandela; and in the women's game England's 2014 world champions led by Sarah Hunter and Canada's Ghislaine Landry (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
