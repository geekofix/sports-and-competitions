Steeplechase

≈ 6 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

The 3000 metres steeplechase is the obstacle race of athletics: 3000 m on the 400 m track with 28 fixed barriers and seven water jumps — four barriers and one water jump per lap — combining middle-distance running with hurdling skill. It is governed worldwide by World Athletics and has been an Olympic event for men since 1900 (over varying distances until 1920) and for women since 2008 (Wikipedia, n.d.). The world records are 7:52.11 for men, set by Lamecha Girma of Ethiopia in Rabat in 2023, and 8:44.32 for women, set by Beatrice Chepkoech of Kenya in Monaco on 20 July 2018 (World Athletics, n.d.-e).

At the end of the 2026 season the women's event was headlined by 2024 Olympic champion Winfred Yavi of Bahrain, 2025 world champion Faith Cherotich of Kenya (pictured), who won at the 2026 Weltklasse Zürich, and Peruth Chemutai of Uganda; the men's event featured 2025 world champion Geordie Beamish of New Zealand, two-time Olympic champion Soufiane El Bakkali of Morocco, world record holder Lamecha Girma and 2026 Diamond League Final winner Gemechu Godana of Ethiopia, who clocked 8:02.74 in Silesia (World Athletics, n.d.-d).

Rules

The 3000 m steeplechase is run on the standard 400 m track with 28 barriers and seven water jumps: the barriers are 0.914 m high for men and 0.762 m for women and 3.66 m wide, and the water jump is 3.66 m by 3.66 m with water 0.70 m deep at the barrier; an athlete may step on top of a barrier but must cross the water jump within the marked area (World Athletics, n.d.-b). Lapped athletes continue in their lanes; the start is a standing start (races beyond 400 m), a single false start disqualifies since 2010, and the finish is judged by photo finish when the torso crosses the line (World Athletics, n.d.-b). Under the World Athletics Competition and Technical Rules (2026 edition), outdoor track races are run on a standard 400 m track with at least eight lanes at international competitions; every lane is 1.22 m ± 0.01 m wide and each athlete has a separate lane in all races up to and including 400 m, with staggered starts in the 200 m, 400 m, 800 m and the relays (World Athletics, n.d.-b). Races up to and including 400 m — including the first leg of the 4x200 m, the medley relay and the 4x400 m — use a compulsory crouch start in starting blocks, while longer races use a standing start (World Athletics, n.d.-b). Since 2010 a single false start disqualifies the athlete; the finish is judged when the torso crosses the vertical plane of the finish line, with a photo-finish camera reading to 1/1000 s and times officially recorded to 1/100 s at major championships (World Athletics, n.d.-b). A wind gauge is read in the running direction and a reading above +2.0 m/s invalidates a record in events up to 200 m; since January 2025 shoe soles are limited to 25 mm in track events and spike pins to 9 mm (World Athletics, n.d.-b). Men and women compete in separate races under identical rules; U18 and U20 age-group competitions use the same distances, and para athletics offers the same events in classification classes (World Athletics, n.d.-b).

Men and women compete in separate races with the barrier heights given above; U18 and U20 competitions use lower barriers and shorter distances, and para athletics offers the steeplechase in classification classes (World Athletics, n.d.-b).

History

The steeplechase grew out of cross-country running with walls, ditches and water at Oxford University in the mid-19th century and was added to the English Championships in 1879; it became a standard Olympic event for men in 1900 and made its women's Olympic debut at Beijing 2008 (World Athletics, n.d.-a). Moses Kiptanui of Kenya was the first man under eight minutes (7:59.18, Zürich 1995) and won three world titles between 1991 and 1995; Saif Saaeed Shaheen, pictured, set the world record of 7:53.63 in Brussels in 2004, which stood until Lamecha Girma's 7:52.11 in 2023 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Dorcus Inzikuru of Uganda won the first women's Olympic title in Athens 2004, Ruth Jebet set the first women's world record ratified in 2000-era (8:52.78, 2016), and Beatrice Chepkoech broke eight seconds off it with 8:44.32 in Monaco 2018 — still the world record in 2026 (World Athletics, n.d.-e).
