Steeplechase

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

The steeplechase is the athletics event in which runners cover a middle-distance race while hurdling fixed barriers and a water jump; its standard championship distance is 3000 metres (2000 and 1000-metre versions exist for youth and other classes). A test of speed, endurance and hurdling technique, it is run on the track at the Olympic Games and World Athletics Championships and is named for the cross-country horse races of the same tradition.

Rules

A 3000-metre steeplechase packs 28 fixed barriers and seven water jumps into its laps of the track. The barriers are heavy, immovable hurdles roughly 0.91 metres high (the women's standard is slightly lower), which runners may step on or clear in any style "+E+" the great steeplechasers clear them without breaking stride "+E+" while the water jump stretches almost 3.7 metres beyond its barrier, forcing athletes to land on the waterlogged slope and drive out cleanly. The event is run in lanes only for the first bend; the remainder is an all-out race in which pacing, barrier technique and the ability to hold form through the final water jumps determine medals, with shorter versions scaling the barrier count and height for youth classes.

History

The name comes from horse racing: early races called steeplechases were run between church steeples across open country, with the term appearing in the British press from the early nineteenth century, and the first footrace to use the name recorded at a pub gathering in Edinburgh in 1827. The athletics version grew out of English cross-country running, was standardized at Oxford University in the mid-nineteenth century, added to the English Championships in 1879, and evolved its modern barrier-and-water format by the early 1900s. The men's steeplechase has been contested at the Olympic Games in its current style since 1900, while the women's 3000-metre version joined the Olympic programme at Beijing 2008, and the event's modern era has been defined by the great distance-running schools of East Africa, above all Kenya, whose athletes have dominated world championship and Olympic medal tables for decades.
