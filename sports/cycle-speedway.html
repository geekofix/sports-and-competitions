Cycle Speedway

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Cycle speedway is a track cycling discipline in which up to four riders race laps of a short, banked oval circuit — typically 70–90 m long — on brakeless fixed-gear bicycles, and points are awarded for each placing (British Cycling, n.d.; Cycle Speedway [CSX], n.d.). A match between two teams consists of a series of 8 to 24 individual races, and the team with the higher aggregate points score wins (CSX, n.d.). The sport's international organization, the National Association of Cycle Speedway Associations (NACSA), was founded in 1950 and staged the first international match, England versus the Netherlands on 26 October 1950 at Earls Court in London in front of 10,000 spectators (CSX, n.d.). In Britain the sport was organized from 1971 by the British Cycle Speedway Council and today sits with British Cycling's Cycle Speedway Commission; around 40 clubs remain active in the UK, with affiliated federations in Poland, the Netherlands, Australia and the USA (British Cycling, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). World championships are held biennially, alternating with European championships (CSX, n.d.). The discipline welcomes men and women — women's racing is an established part of the programme, with stars such as Jess Varnish, a former world champion (Wikipedia, n.d.).

This is part of the artistic and specialist cycle disciplines — Artistic Cycling, Cyr Wheel, Cycleball — within the cycling family (parent Cycling); the full list is under Important Links.

Rules

Races are run anticlockwise over four laps, with up to four riders per race; placings earn points (typically 5-3-2-1), and the team points from each race in the match are summed (CSX, n.d.). The machine is a single-speed bicycle with a freewheel, no brakes and 26-inch wheels; helmets have been compulsory since 1999, and contact between riders is legal, though dangerous riding is penalized (British Cycling, n.d.). Tracks are 70–90 m in length, usually asphalt or concrete with banked turns (Wikipedia, n.d.). Classifications include open men's and women's events plus youth categories, and national leagues feed the biennial world and European championships (CSX, n.d.). Because the bikes have no brakes, riders control speed by the fixed gear and by leaning into the banks, and races are short, explosive sprints of under a minute (British Cycling, n.d.).

History

Cycle speedway grew out of informal track racing in the early twentieth century and was formalized after the Second World War; the NACSA was founded in 1950 to govern international matches, beginning with the famous England–Netherlands meeting at Earls Court on 26 October 1950 (CSX, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The British Cycle Speedway Council was formed in 1971 to run the domestic sport, which today operates under British Cycling (British Cycling, n.d.). Icons of the sport include Jim Varnish, the 1985 world champion, his son Brett Aitken, and Jess Varnish, who won the women's world title and became one of the best-known female riders in the discipline (Wikipedia, n.d.).
