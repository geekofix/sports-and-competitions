Shito-Ryu Karate

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shitō-ryū is one of the four principal styles of karate recognised by the World Karate Federation (WKF), founded by Kenwa Mabuni (1889–1952) as a synthesis of the Shuri-te he learned from Ankō Itosu and the Naha-te he learned from Kanryō Higaonna — the style's name takes the first character of each master's name (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; WKF, n.d.). Its syllabus of roughly 100 kata is the largest of any karate style, and it fields both kata and kumite competitors at the WKF World Championships, where at Cairo 2025 Mo Sheung Grace Lau of Hong Kong China won the women's kata title, defeating Japan's Maho Ono 46.10–43.30, while Japan's Kakeru Nishiyama won the men's kata (WKF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

Figure: Nakasone Genwa. (1938). Mabuni Kenwa [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. Public domain (PD-Japan-oldphoto). https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mabuni_Kenwa.jpg

Among today's leading exponents, the women's kata elite is headed by Hong Kong China's Grace Lau, the 2025 world champion, while the men's kata scene is led by Japan's Kakeru Nishiyama, her Cairo 2025 counterpart; in kumite, Shitō-ryū practitioners compete in the same WKF weight categories for women and men, from −55 kg to +84 kg (Wikipedia, n.d.-c; WKF, n.d.).

Rules

Shitō-ryū competition follows the WKF rules: kata are performed solo and scored by judges with flag/points systems, while kumite bouts run three minutes in weight classes (−55, −61, −68, −76, +84 kg for women; −60, −67, −75, −84, +84 kg for men) with yuko (1 point), waza-ari (2 points) and ippon (3 points) scoring, hantei decisions and electronic scoring at major events (WKF, n.d.). Technically the style organises blocks, strikes and kicks as jōdan, chūdan or gedan on the migi (right) or hidari (left) side, and trains bunkai — the practical application of kata movements — plus kobudō weapons alongside empty-hand techniques (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Its five rules of defence (uke no go gensoku) underpin all blocking, and the style's dojo kun is "kokoro inamovable, mind inamovable" (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women and men compete in identical kata and kumite categories, with separate women's and men's events at championships (WKF, n.d.). After Mabuni's death the style split into branches including Shitō-kai (World Shitō-ryū Karate-dō Federation), Seitō, Tani-ha Shūkōkai, Motobu-ha and Itosu-ryū, each with its own kata emphasis but shared WKF competition rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

History

Kenwa Mabuni was born on 14 November 1889 in Shuri, Okinawa, and died on 23 May 1952 in Tokyo; from the age of 13 he trained in Shuri-te under Ankō Itosu, and through his friend Miyagi Chōjun he was introduced to Kanryō Higaonna, from whom he learned Naha-te (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). In 1918 he co-founded the Okinawa Karate Kenkyū-kai research club and in 1924 a dōjō with Miyagi, Motobu Chōki and others, and after moving to Osaka in 1928–29 he established the Yoshukan dōjō, from which Shitō-ryū emerged; the style was recognised by the Dai Nihon Butokukai in the 1930s and officially registered in 1939 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Mabuni compiled 73 kata for the style and created eight himself, including Aoyagi and Meijō, which he designed specifically for women's self-defence at the government's request (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). After his death in 1952 the leadership split between his sons: Kenei Mabuni (1918–2015), 10th dan, who founded Shitō-kai and led the World Shitō-ryū Karate-dō Federation, and Kenzo Mabuni (1927–2005), who founded the Seitō branch; his student Chōjirō Tani founded Tani-ha Shūkōkai in 1948 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
