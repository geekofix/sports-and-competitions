Shito-Ryu Karate

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shito-ryu is one of the four major traditional styles of Japanese karate, founded by Kenwa Mabuni (1889\u20131952), who blended the Shuri-te lineage of Anko Itosu with the Naha-te lineage of Kanryo Higaonna — the style's very name combines the first characters of its two teachers (Shi from Itosu/Higaoshi, To/Higaonna). It is famed for the largest kata repertoire of any mainstream karate style, preserving dozens of forms from both Okinawan traditions, and today it is one of the styles recognised by the World Karate Federation, with large organisations in Japan and worldwide.

Rules

As a traditional karate style, shito-ryu is practised through kihon (fundamentals), kata (prescribed forms) and kumite (sparring), the three elements of all recognised karate competition. Kata competition pits performers executing the style's extensive repertoire — commonly reckoned at between forty and seventy forms, drawn from both the Shuri-te (Itosu) and Naha-te (Higaonna) lineages — against opponents performing forms of their choice, scored on technical quality; in WKF-format competition, kumite is point-based sparring performed with controlled contact and protective equipment, with scoring zones on the head, body and sides. Traditional shito-ryu dojos also place distinctive weight on the use of hips and low stances in the Itosu line and on breathing and strength conditioning in the Higaonna line, so the style's syllabus blends both. Belt ranks (kyu/dan) and gradings standardise progression through the style's many branch organisations.

History

Kenwa Mabuni was born in Shuri, Okinawa, in 1889 and studied under Anko Itosu, the great codifier of Shuri-te, and later under Kanryo Higaonna in Naha, and after military service and a career as a police officer he moved to Osaka and taught karate openly. He initially named his synthesis Hanko-ryu ("half-hard style"), but by the early 1930s had adopted the name Shito-ryu to honour both teachers — combining characters from Itosu's and Higaonna's names; he registered the style with the Butoku-kai in 1939 and presided over its growth until his death in 1952. On his death in 1952, his Kansai-based organisation was continued by his sons Kenei and Kenzo and by his students, and the style subsequently divided into major branches: the Shito-kai, formalised as the All Japan Karatedo Shito-Kai in 1964; the Shukokai founded by Chojiro Tani in the late 1940s; the Itosu-kai founded by Ryusho Sakagami in 1940; and the Seishin-kai lineage of Kosei Kuniba, now one of the largest shito-ryu organisations in the world. In 1964 shito-ryu was one of the four traditional styles (with shotokan, goju-ryu and wado-ryu) represented when Japan's major karate organisations united, and today it remains one of the most practised styles worldwide, with strong followings in Japan, the Americas, Europe and the Middle East.
