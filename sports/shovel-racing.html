Shovel Racing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shovel racing is an American winter sport in which riders race down a snow-covered slope seated on a snow shovel — usually a metal No. 12 grain shovel — reaching speeds of up to 70 miles per hour (about 115 km/h). Born in the 1970s at New Mexico ski resorts, where lift operators slid down the mountain on shovels after work, it is practised today chiefly at community festivals and at its historic home, the World Championship Shovel Races formerly held at Angel Fire Resort in Angel Fire, New Mexico.

Rules

The rider sits in the shovel facing the handle, leaning back with feet pointed forward, and simply slides down the fall line — there is no steering or braking, so the shovel goes where gravity takes it. Competitors wax the underside of the shovel to increase speed, and in the classic production-class rules no modifications to the grain shovel are allowed except for wax and paint; helmets are mandatory. Each competitor gets two runs, with the fastest time determining the winner, on courses of roughly 1,000 feet. Historically an open "super-modified" class allowed sleds fitted with skis, aerodynamic pods and roll cages — machines that looked more like bobsleds than shovels — but this class was banned after safety concerns.

History

Shovel racing began in the 1970s at New Mexico's ski resorts, where workers used shovels as sleds to move quickly from one spot to another; Damien Deleon is unofficially credited as the "king of shovel racing", and a lift-operator's boast at Angel Fire about beating any ski school member down the slope on his shovel reportedly sparked the first organised race. Angel Fire Resort hosted the annual competition for some thirty years, which grew into the World Shovel Racing Championships — the sport's zenith — and it was featured in the 1997 Winter X Games; the sport was dropped from future X Games after racer Justin Williams died in a crash at that event. Liability concerns led Angel Fire to drop the event in 2005, and it returned in 2010 in the ban-the-modified-sleds production format; a final revival ran through 2019\u20132020 before the resort ended the tradition. Today the sport survives mostly at community events, with racing families such as the Gonzales siblings of Angel Fire — multiple world champions — among its legends.
