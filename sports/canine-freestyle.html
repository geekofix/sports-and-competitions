Canine Freestyle

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Canine freestyle (musical canine freestyle, in the UK called heelwork to music) is a dog sport in which a handler and dog perform a choreographed routine to music, combining obedience, tricks and dance. In the UK the sport is officially recognized by The Kennel Club and runs at Crufts; internationally the FCI has held World Championships in Heelwork to Music and Freestyle since 2010. Governing bodies include The Kennel Club, Canine Freestyle GB and the World Canine Freestyle Organization.

Rules

Competition rules vary between organizations, but most score technical merit and artistic impression. In the two official Kennel Club categories, Heelwork to Music requires the dog to stay in one of the eight prescribed heelwork positions for at least two thirds of the routine, while Freestyle allows tricks for at least two thirds. Routines last up to about four minutes, are performed off-leash, and the music must reflect the routine's rhythm. Pinch, prong and electronic collars are banned at Kennel Club licensed events. Dogs may start competing from 12 months (heelwork) or 18 months (freestyle), and classes run from starters to advanced with points-based progression.

History

The sport began around 1989 in Canada, England, the United States and the Netherlands, when obedience handlers started demonstrating dogs heeling to music, inspired by the equestrian sport of musical kur. In England, handler Mary Ray first demonstrated heelwork to music at Crufts in the early 1990s, and the Rugby Dog Training Club held the first competitive show in 1996. The Kennel Club revised its regulations in 2007, and at the 2010 FCI World Dog Show in Herning, Denmark, the first FCI World Championships in Heelwork to Music and Freestyle were held, won individually by Kath Hardman (HTM) and Thierry Thomas (freestyle).
