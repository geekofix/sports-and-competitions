Gatka

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Gatka is a traditional Sikh martial art of Punjab in which two practitioners spar with wooden sticks (gatka or soti), wearing a leather shield (phari) on the sword arm; it is practiced both as a sport form (khel) with points for controlled contact and as a ritual demonstration form (rasmi) (World Gatka Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The equipment is standardized: the wooden stick is 91–107 cm long and about 12.7 mm thick with a leather-wrapped hilt of 15–18 cm, and the shield (phari) is a 23 × 23 cm pad of dry leather (World Gatka Federation, n.d.-a). Gatka traces its roots to the 15th-century Shastar Vidya of Punjab and was promoted as a martial discipline by the sixth Sikh Guru, Hargobind (1595–1644), who founded the Ranjit Akhara in Amritsar and the concept of miri-piri (spiritual and temporal authority), and later by the tenth Guru, Gobind Singh (1666–1708), whose Nihang warriors were its custodians (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Today the art is organized by the International Gatka Federation (founded 1982, formalized 1987), the World Gatka Federation and national bodies such as the National Gatka Association of India (NGAI), which held the 12th Punjab State Gatka Championship in Rupnagar on 18–19 April 2026, while the 12th UK Gatka Championship was held in Woolwich on 16 July 2026 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; NGAI, n.d.). Schools now exist in India, the United Kingdom, the United States, Uruguay and elsewhere (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

At the end of 2026 the leading competitors are the teams of the Amritsar akharas in India and the UK Gatka Association squads — the 2026 UK champion Zora Singh of Baba Fateh Singh Akhara, Woolwich — with the sport growing among women and juniors at the state and national championships (NGAI, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

In the competitive form (khel), two gatkabaaz spar for a set time wearing the wooden stick and the phari shield; points are awarded for clean, controlled strikes to valid target areas, and the competitor with more points — or the first to reach the target score — wins the bout; in the demonstration form (rasmi), pairs perform choreographed sequences of strikes and deflections with sticks and shields to music, judged on technique, timing and presentation (World Gatka Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Bouts are officiated by a referee with judges, and safety rules govern contact — full-force head strikes are prohibited in sport gatka, and protective gear is required at championships (World Gatka Federation, n.d.-a). Weight and age categories exist at national championships — for example the 12th Punjab State Championship in Rupnagar in April 2026 included junior, sub-junior and senior divisions for boys and girls (NGAI, n.d.). Men and women compete in separate divisions under identical rules; the stick and shield specifications are the same for all categories (World Gatka Federation, n.d.-a).

History

Gatka emerged in 15th-century Punjab as part of Shastar Vidya, the Sikh science of weapons, and was institutionalized in the 17th century when Guru Hargobind established the Ranjit Akhara in Amritsar — where warriors trained with swords, shields and sticks — and preached miri-piri; the Nihang orders under Guru Gobind Singh kept gatka as a battlefield skill and a spiritual discipline (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). After the decline of the Sikh Empire in 1849, gatka survived in akharas (wrestling schools) and at religious festivals such as Hola Mohalla in Anandpur Sahib, where stick duels are still demonstrated (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). In the 20th century it was revived as a sport: the International Gatka Federation was founded in 1982 and formalized in 1987, the World Gatka Federation published a standard rule book, and today national championships are held in India (NGAI), the UK, the US and Canada, with the art also practiced as a cultural performance at the Vaisakhi festival (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; NGAI, n.d.).
