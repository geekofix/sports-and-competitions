Backgammon

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Backgammon is one of the oldest known board games, played by two players who move 15 checkers each across a board of 24 points according to the roll of two dice, aiming to bear all checkers off first. The modern competitive game is governed by the World Backgammon Federation (WBGF), founded in 1991, which sanctions the World Backgammon Championship and maintains the official world rankings; the 57th World Championship was held in Monte Carlo from July 25 to August 2, 2026, where Petko Kostadinov of the USA (born in Bulgaria) won the main event, defeating Jason Pack in the final with Naoki Iketani third, and Martha Ghio of the USA won the Women's World Championship (World Backgammon Federation [WBGF], n.d.-a; World Backgammon Championship, n.d.). Alongside the main event, Monte Carlo hosts the Monte Carlo Open and the Ultimate Backgammon Challenge, and the 2026 women's champion Ghio also competed at the top tables (World Backgammon Championship, n.d.) (World Backgammon Federation, n.d.-a) (World Backgammon Federation, n.d.-b).

Backgammon is also a major money game: online platforms and live tournaments with buy-ins of thousands of dollars are common, and the doubling cube — introduced in the 1920s in New York — doubles the stakes and lets either player offer to double the wager, which the opponent may accept (take) or refuse (drop), conceding the current stake (WBGF, n.d.-b).

Rules

The board has 24 triangular points arranged in four quadrants of six points each; each player's 15 checkers start in a standard position, and the players move their checkers in opposite directions. On each turn a player rolls two dice and must play both numbers if possible — as two separate moves or one combined move — moving checkers toward their home board. A point occupied by two or more of one's own checkers is a "made point"; a single enemy checker on a point is a "blot" and is hit by landing on it, sending it to the bar. A player with a checker on the bar must enter it into the opponent's home board before making any other move; if both entry points are blocked, the turn is lost (WBGF, n.d.-b).

Checkers may not land on points occupied by five or more enemy checkers (or six in some rule variants). Once all 15 of a player's checkers are in their home board, they may begin bearing off — removing checkers with rolls equal to or higher than the point number, with the exact number required for the highest occupied point. The first player to bear off all 15 checkers wins the game; match play at the World Championship is to a set point target (e.g. 25 points in the 2026 final) with the doubling cube in use (WBGF, n.d.-a).

There are no gender- or age-specific rule differences — men and women play the identical game, and the WBGF runs a separate Women's World Championship as a dedicated title alongside the open event; junior and senior events exist at national level. Variants include longgammon (three dice), hypergammon (three checkers pre-moved), and nackgammon (a popular Middle Eastern variant with a different starting layout) (WBGF, n.d.-b).

History

Backgammon's ancestors — games such as senet in Egypt and the Roman ludus duodecim scriptorum — date back more than 5,000 years; the Royal Game of Ur (about 2600 BC) is the oldest known board game. The modern game's rules crystallized in the 17th–19th centuries in Europe, where it became a staple of coffeehouses and clubs; the doubling cube was introduced in the 1920s by New York gamblers (WBGF, n.d.-b).

Competitive backgammon organized internationally in the 20th century: the first World Championship was held in Las Vegas in 1967, won by Tim Holland of the USA, and from 1979 the event moved to Monte Carlo under Lewis Deyong, who merged the competing American and European circuits; Luigi Villa of Italy won the first Monte Carlo world title in 1979. A famous milestone came in the late 1970s, when the computer program BKG 9.8 — written by Hans Berliner at Carnegie Mellon University — defeated Villa in a televised match in 1979, the first time a computer beat a world champion at a board game (WBGF, n.d.-a).

On the women's side, the dedicated Women's World Championship has been held since the 1980s; early champions include Eva zack and later stars such as Kathy Kidd, with Martha Ghio of the USA taking the 2026 title in Monte Carlo (World Backgammon Championship, n.d.). Today the strongest players also compete online, and the WBGF maintains the official world ranking list.
