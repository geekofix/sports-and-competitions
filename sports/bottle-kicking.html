Bottle-kicking

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Bottle-kicking is a centuries-old Easter Monday custom of the Leicestershire villages of Hallaton and Medbourne, England: two teams try to carry or kick three "bottles" — two beer kegs and a solid wooden "dummy" painted red and white — from the Buttercross in Hallaton across a mile-long course to the opposing village's stream, best of three bottles wins. The event is accompanied by the hare pie scramble and the distribution of penny loaves, and the 2026 game was played on Easter Monday, April 6, 2026, with Hallaton scoring first and looking to take a second bottle (BBC, 2026; Harborough Museum, n.d.-a).

Both villages field teams of all ages, and women have always taken part in the scramble and procession; modern teams include mixed squads, though the competitive kicking sides remain traditionally village-based (Harborough Museum, n.d.-a).

Rules

The course runs about a mile from the Buttercross on Hallaton's village green to the Medbourne boundary stream (and vice versa for the return bottles). Three bottles are contested; the first team to win two bottles wins the match. A bottle is "captured" when it is carried or kicked over the opponent's stream line; teams may form huge scrums around the bottle, and there are no formal offside or foul rules — play is governed by long-standing custom, with referees (traditionally the vicar and local officials) starting each bottle with a kick-off and declaring captures (BBC, 2026). The event day begins with a parade through Medbourne, a tug-of-war, a church service, the hare pie blessing and scramble at about 2:50 p.m., distribution of penny loaves, and the first kick-off around 3:15 p.m. Men's and women's participation is organized by village, with identical rules for all (Harborough Museum, n.d.-a).

History

Records of bottle-kicking date to the late 18th century, though local lore traces the custom to pre-Christian times: legend says two Hallaton ladies were saved from a raging bull by a startled hare, and in gratitude gifted land to the church on condition that the vicar provide a hare pie, twelve penny loaves and two barrels of ale for the poor every Easter Monday; when Medbourne villagers joined the scramble and stole the beer, the village rivalry began. In 1790 a rector tried to ban the event, but relented after "No pie, no parson" was scrawled on the vicarage wall. The custom has been cancelled only twice — in 2001 (foot-and-mouth disease) and 2020 (COVID-19 pandemic). The neighboring village of Cranoe traditionally plays as a freelance force, joining whichever side is losing. The historic bottle used between 1850 and 1950 is preserved at Harborough Museum (BBC, 2026; Harborough Museum, n.d.-a).
