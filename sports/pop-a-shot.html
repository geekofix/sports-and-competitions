Pop-A-Shot

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Pop-A-Shot is the original arcade basketball game — an electronic shooting machine on which players score as many baskets as possible within a countdown of typically 30 to 60 seconds using small scaled basketballs on a sloped return ramp; invented in 1981, the brand became so dominant that its name is used generically for all arcade basketball games, and it is now also an organized competitive sport whose national championship airs on ESPN (Pop-A-Shot, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The 2026 Pop-A-Shot National Championship — season three of the revived series — brought the top 12 arcade basketball shooters to the ESPN Wide World of Sports Complex in Orlando, aired on ESPN2/ESPN8: The Ocho on 7 August 2026, and was won by Mitch Phillis, who had undergone major kidney-donation surgery for his father earlier that year; Scott Setzke was the 2025 champion (Pop-A-Shot, n.d.). The competition is open to women and men alike, with mixed fields at the qualifiers and the national final.

Rules

A standard arcade basketball unit has one or two hoops side by side, an electronic scoreboard with a countdown clock and infrared sensors that register each made basket; shots score points, and many machines award bonus points or extra time when a scoring threshold is reached before the clock expires (Wikipedia, n.d.). In tournament play all competitors use the same machine and settings, the highest score within the time limit wins, ties are broken by a shoot-off, and shots must be released before the buzzer. In the branded national championship format, qualifying rounds at bars, arcades and entertainment venues across the country feed a finals field of 12, where players shoot fixed timed rounds and the cumulative score decides the champion; the whole final must be completed in under 60 minutes for the broadcast slot (Pop-A-Shot, n.d.). No age or gender classes exist — one open field.

History

The original Pop-A-Shot was invented in 1981 by Ken Cochran, a former college basketball coach in Salina, Kansas, who built the game so children could practice shooting rhythm; the coin-operated machine became a fixture of American arcades and bars, and the company pioneered electronic scoring, infrared sensors and steel frames (Pop-A-Shot, n.d.). As the coin-op market saturated, Pop-A-Shot licensed its name for home games, and Peoria native Tony Stucker bought the brand and brought back the national championship in 2024 after a 25-year hiatus — about 250 players at a Chicago qualifier, finals on ESPN8: The Ocho — with a new coin-operated Pop-A-Shot Elite machine returning to arcades in 2025 (Gadget Review, 2025; Pop-A-Shot, n.d.).
