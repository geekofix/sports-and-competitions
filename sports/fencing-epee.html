Fencing Épée

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Épée is the heaviest of the three fencing weapons and the closest to a real duel: the entire body of the opponent is the valid target, there is no right of way, and the first fencer to land a touch scores — two touches landing within about 40 milliseconds count as a double touch for both fencers (FIE, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.). The weapon is governed worldwide by the Fédération Internationale d'Escrime (FIE), founded on 29 November 1913 in Paris and headquartered in Lausanne, with more than 150 member federations; the FIE World Championships 2026 in Hong Kong (22–30 July 2026) comprised twelve events, including men's and women's individual and team épée (FIE, n.d.-a). Épée has been on the Olympic program for men since Paris 1900 and for women since Atlanta 1996 (Olympics.com, n.d.). See Fencing on this site for the shared rules of all three weapons, and Foil and Sabre for the other weapons (Fédération Internationale d'Escrime, n.d.-a) (Fédération Internationale d'Escrime, n.d.-b).

At the end of 2026 the leading épée fencers of the 2026/27 season are, on the women's side, Italy's Roberta Marzani at the top of the FIE world rankings, ahead of her compatriots and of France's and China's leading épéeists; on the men's side Hungary's Gergely Siklósi and Máté Tamás Koch lead the rankings (FIE, n.d.-b).

Rules

The épée weighs at most 770 g with a maximum total length of 110 cm and a stiff V-shaped blade of at most 90 cm; the button at the tip must register a pressure of at least 750 g to score, and the guard is a large round bell guard (FIE, n.d.-b). The bout is fenced on the standard 14 m × 1.5–2 m conductive piste: individual direct-elimination bouts run in three periods of three minutes with one-minute breaks and are won at 15 touches, with a one-minute priority period if tied; pool bouts last three minutes or five touches; team events are nine-bout relays to a cumulative 45 touches (FIE, n.d.-b).

Because the whole body — including hands, feet and head — is valid, any touch scores for whoever hits first; there is no right of way in épée, and a double touch within 1/40 of a second (about 40 ms) is awarded to both fencers (FIE, n.d.-b). Corps-à-corps is penalized only when it prevents a touch, and covering a valid target area with the body or the weapon arm is a foul; leaving the piste with both feet awards a touch to the opponent, and the referee sanctions infringements with yellow, red and black cards (FIE, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.). Men and women fence under identical rules; the only equipment difference is the compulsory chest protector for women at all three weapons (FIE, n.d.-b). Age classifications are cadet (under 17), junior (under 20), senior and veteran (40 and over), each with its own World Championship and World Cup circuit (FIE, n.d.-a). Wheelchair para épée is governed by World Abilitysport in classes A, B and C (Wikipedia, n.d.).

History

The épée developed from the 19th-century dueling sword with a sharp point; it entered the Olympic program for men at Paris 1900 and for women at Atlanta 1996, and electronic scoring was introduced for épée at the Berlin 1936 Olympics, earlier than for the other weapons (Olympics.com, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The first Olympic épée champion was the Frenchman Émile Coste (1868–1950), who also won individual foil gold in 1900 (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Italy has dominated the men's épée historically: Edoardo Mangiarotti (1919–2012) won six Olympic gold medals in épée — individual titles at London 1948 and Melbourne 1952 plus four team golds — the most Olympic épée titles of any fencer, and his brother Dario Mangiarotti (1915–2014) added further Olympic and world medals (Wikipedia, n.d.). France's Lucien Gaudin (1947–2012) and Hungary's Pál Schmitt (b. 1941, épée gold at Munich 1968) were other icons of the mid-20th century, while the Italian Garozzo brothers and the Hungarian team defined the late 1900s (Wikipedia, n.d.). On the women's side, the event arrived with Atlanta 1996, where France's Laura Flessel (b. 1971, retired in 2012) won individual and team gold — she remains the standard-bearer of French épée with five world titles (Wikipedia, n.d.).
