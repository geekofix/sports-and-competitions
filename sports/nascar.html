NASCAR

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

NASCAR (National Association for Stock Car Auto Racing) is the American sanctioning body and operator of stock car racing, best known for its premier NASCAR Cup Series (NASCAR, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It was founded by Bill France Sr. in Daytona Beach, Florida, on 21 February 1948 and sanctions more than 1,500 races a year at over 100 tracks in the United States, Canada, Mexico, Brazil and Europe (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The calendar's centerpiece is the Daytona 500, "The Great American Race", first run in 1959, and the other national series are the Xfinity Series and the Craftsman Truck Series (NASCAR, n.d.). Women compete in the same national series — there is no separate women's championship — and drivers such as Hailie Deegan are among the current stars of the Xfinity and Truck ranks.

Rules

Cup Series races are run over 36 points-paying events plus exhibition races, mostly on ovals (superspeedways such as Daytona and Talladega, intermediate tracks and short tracks) but also on road courses and street circuits (NASCAR, n.d.). Races are divided into stages, and a playoff system (introduced in 2004 as the "Chase for the Cup") determines the champion: a group of drivers is seeded into the playoffs and competes in elimination rounds, with the last remaining drivers racing for the title at Phoenix Raceway (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Cars are strictly regulated "stock" machines built on the common Next Gen chassis with manufacturer bodies (Chevrolet, Ford, Toyota), and points are awarded for race and stage finishes according to a published scale (NASCAR, n.d.). Safety rules include mandatory head-and-neck restraints, SAFER energy-absorbing barriers, cockpit safety nets and a minimum driver age of 18 in the national series; men's and women's drivers compete under identical rules and classifications (NASCAR, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

History

Stock car racing traces its roots to Prohibition-era moonshine runners who modified their cars to outrun police and raced each other on dirt roads; after World War II, Bill France Sr. organized regional races into a national sanctioning body in 1948 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first Daytona 500 was held in 1959 and won by Lee Petty; the modern era was defined by Richard Petty, "The King", and Dale Earnhardt, each with a record seven Cup titles, and later by Jeff Gordon's four (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Danica Patrick became the most successful woman in Cup history with a win at Daytona in 2008 and a best championship finish of third in 2009, paving the way for today's generation of women drivers (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
