Working Cow Horse

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Working cow horse (reined cow horse) is the Western riding event that tests a horse's "cow sense" — its training to read, separate and control a single cow — across three scored phases: rein work (a judged pattern of circles, spins and sliding stops), herd work (cutting a cow from the herd and holding it), and fence work (boxing the cow at the end of the arena, running it down the fence and circling it both directions in the open) (National Reined Cow Horse Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). It descends from the practical work of California and Western ranch horses — the Spanish vaquero tradition — and women riders compete in every class alongside men (American Quarter Horse Association, n.d.).

The highest level is governed in the United States by the National Reined Cow Horse Association (NRCHA), based in Pilot Point, Texas, whose marquee event is the annual Snaffle Bit Futurity in Fort Worth with a payout above $1 million and a $125,000 Open champion's cheque (National Reined Cow Horse Association, n.d.). The breed associations — AQHA, APHA and the Appaloosa Horse Club — run a similar working cow horse class without the herd-work phase (American Quarter Horse Association, n.d.).

Rules

Each horse-and-rider pair works a single cow for up to 2½ minutes, and the run is judged phase by phase: rein work scores the pattern's circles, spins, lead changes and stopping; herd work scores how cleanly the rider cuts a cow from the herd and how well the horse holds it with minimal rein contact; fence work scores boxing the cow at the short end, turning it, running it down the long fence turning once each way, and circling it in the open both directions (National Reined Cow Horse Association, n.d.). Judges award points with deductions for losing the cow, visible rein resistance or a horse refusing to turn the animal; age classes govern equipment — snaffle bit with two hands for three-year-olds in futurities, hackamore (bosal and mecate) for four- and five-year-olds in derbies and classics, and a bridle for six-year-olds and over in bridle spectaculars (American Quarter Horse Association, n.d.). All breeds and all levels from non-professional to open are eligible, and women and men compete together in the same classes (National Reined Cow Horse Association, n.d.).

History

The reined cow horse traces to the California Spanish vaqueros, who for almost 150 years developed the equipment, riding styles and step-by-step training that produced the finished ranch horse working great herds of longhorns driven from Mexico to California (National Reined Cow Horse Association, n.d.). By the early twentieth century the working cow horse had become a luxury rather than a necessity, and little sustained the tradition through the Depression and the Second World War — until the California Reined Cow Horse Association was formed in 1949 to preserve and educate the public about this horsemanship, becoming the National Reined Cow Horse Association (National Reined Cow Horse Association, n.d.). The first Snaffle Bit Futurity was held in 1970 at the Cal Expo fairgrounds in Sacramento with 27 entries; it moved between Sacramento, Fresno, Santa Rosa, Las Vegas and Reno before settling in Fort Worth, Texas, and today it is called "the Greatest Show on Dirt" (Wikipedia, n.d.). Among the sport's icons, Sandy Collier of Buellton, California, remains the only woman to win the NRCHA Open Snaffle Bit Futurity, taking the 1993 title on Miss Rey Dry (Wikipedia, n.d.).
