Breaststroke Swimming

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Breaststroke swimming is the stroke with the frog kick: the body stays breast-down, both arms move simultaneously in the water and both feet turn outward for the whip kick, the head must break the surface once per cycle, and at every turn and at the finish both hands must touch the wall simultaneously on the same level; it is raced over 50 m, 100 m and 200 m and governed worldwide by World Aquatics (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; World Aquatics, n.d.-c). The long-course world records at the end of the 2026 season are 56.88 s over 100 m for Adam Peaty of Great Britain (2019) and 1:04.13 for Lilly King of the USA (2017) — a women's mark that had stood for over nine years — 2:05.48 over 200 m for Qin Haiyang of China (Fukuoka 2023), and 29.16 s over 50 m for Rūta Meilutytė of Lithuania (2023) on the women's side (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Among the 2026 leaders, the women come first: Lilly King of the USA (pictured), holder of the 100 m world record, Tang Qianting of China — 2024 world champion over 100 m and 200 m breaststroke in long and short course and Olympic silver medallist at Paris 2024, with an Asian record of 1:04.39 over 100 m — and Rūta Meilutytė, the 50 m world record holder (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). On the men's side Qin Haiyang is the benchmark: he won both the 100 m and 200 m breaststroke at the 2025 World Championships in Singapore — after becoming the first man to sweep all three breaststroke events (50/100/200 m) at one World Championships in Fukuoka 2023 — and holds the 200 m world record, while Adam Peaty's 100 m record of 56.88 s from 2019 still stands; at the 2026 European Championships Poland's Filip Nowacki won the men's 200 m in 2:07.70 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Breaststroke is swum under the same pool, lane, block and water-temperature rules as the other strokes (World Aquatics, n.d.-b; World Aquatics, n.d.-d). From the start and after each turn the swimmer may take one arm stroke and one leg kick underwater, but the head must break the surface before the 15 m mark; during the race the body must remain breast-down, the arms must move simultaneously in a horizontal plane, and the feet must be turned outward during the propulsive part of the kick, with the hands not passing below the hips except for the single permitted pull after the start or turn (World Aquatics, n.d.-d).

At each turn and at the finish the breaststroker must touch with both hands at the same time and on the same horizontal level; a false start disqualifies under the single-false-start rule, and pulling on the lane rope is prohibited (World Aquatics, n.d.-d). Events run as heats, semifinals and finals (50 m, 100 m) or heats and finals (200 m) with automatic timing to 1/100 s, and records require SW12 ratification (World Aquatics, n.d.-e). Men and women swim identical distances, and para swimming offers breaststroke in the SB classes (World Aquatics, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

History

Breaststroke was contested at the St. Louis 1904 Olympics over 440 yards and has been on the men's Olympic programme since 1908 and the women's since 1912; it is the stroke in which the most rule refinements have occurred, from the underwater pull to the head-break requirement (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Kosuke Kitajima of Japan, pictured, is the breaststroke icon of the modern era: he won gold over 100 m and 200 m at both Athens 2004 and Beijing 2008 — the first swimmer to repeat as Olympic breaststroke double champion — and set world records at both Games (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Other legends include Australia's Leisel Jones, Olympic 100 m breaststroke champion at Athens 2004 and world record holder in the 2000s, and the USA's Adam Peaty is covered among the current records above (World Aquatics, n.d.-e; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
