Cammag

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Cammag (also written camman) is a traditional stick-and-ball game of the Isle of Man, closely related to Scottish shinty and Irish hurling and camogie. Players use a curved wooden stick, the camman (Manx for "little bent thing"), to strike a small leather or cork ball, the crick, and drive it toward the opposing goal. There is no national governing body; the game survives as a local custom with informal, parish-specific rules, most famously in the annual open match played on the Tynwald field at St John's on St Stephen's Day (26 December), traditionally between teams representing the North and the South of the island, usually starting at 2 p.m. and played over three 20-minute periods (Culture Vannin, n.d.; BBC News, n.d.). Historic games could involve entire towns or villages, with anything from four to about 200 players; the modern St John's match regularly draws more than 50 players on the field, and the 26 December 2025 match attracted the largest crowd ever and had to be decided by a penalty shootout after finishing goalless at full time (Isle of Man Today, 2026). Both men and women now take part in the modern revival, although the historic winter game was played by men only (BBC News, n.d.).

Rules

Because there is no standardized rulebook — the game is famously said to have "no rules" — local custom and the agreement of the players govern each match (BBC News, n.d.). The playing area is a road, field or beach with a goal at each end marked by posts, trees or a gap; historically each town sometimes acted as its own goal. Teams hit the crick with the camman in any direction, and the side that drives the ball through the opponents' goal scores. Details such as team size, the number of goals needed to win and the allowed strokes vary from parish to parish; modern St John's matches use the North–South divide, whose historic boundary ran from the Grand Island Hotel to Niarbyl according to research by David Fisher in the Manx National Heritage archives, a 2 p.m. start and three 20-minute periods, with a penalty shootout available to decide a draw in the modern revival (Culture Vannin, n.d.; Isle of Man Today, 2026). Equipment is supposed to consist of a bent stick — traditionally a gorse-wood camman, a treasured possession if of suitable size and shape — and a crick of cork or wood, sometimes covered in cloth or leather, ranging from about two inches in circumference to the size of a fist. The game is a physically demanding contact sport and protective equipment is advised (Wikipedia, n.d.).

History

Cammag is the Manx descendant of the Gaelic stick-and-ball games; the Manx word cammag, like Scottish Gaelic camanachd and Irish camán, derives from the Gaelic root cam, "bent". The sport may have been introduced to the Isle of Man by Irish missionaries in the fifth century, and the earliest written record dates to 1760, when three men and a boy were brought before the church court for playing cammag on a Sunday. Press references begin in 1843, when the Manx Sun reported an attempt to ban the game within towns, with further complaints in 1846, 1851, 1869, 1871 and 1884; on 14 March 1885 the Isle of Man Times reported that the "ancient game of Cammag" had been revived at Mooragh, Ramsey, and the Ramsey Cammag Club was formed that December. Cammag was gradually supplanted by hockey in the early 20th century, but revival matches were played in 1937 for the Aeglagh Vannin Hollantide festivities, a camogie exhibition took place in Noble's Park, Douglas, in July 1938 during the Celtic Congress, and a composite shinty–hurling international was played at Port-e-Chee in 1979 (Wikipedia, n.d.; Manx Hockey Association, 2015). Today the annual St John's match keeps the custom alive as one of the island's two great ancient traditions alongside Hunt the Wren Day (BBC News, n.d.).
