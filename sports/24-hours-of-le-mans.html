24 Hours of Le Mans

Basic Information Rules History

Basic Information

The 24 Hours of Le Mans is the world's oldest active endurance race, held annually since 1923 on the Circuit de la Sarthe near Le Mans, France. It is organized by the Automobile Club de l'Ouest (ACO), forms the centerpiece of the FIA World Endurance Championship, and is one leg of motorsport's Triple Crown of Motorsport.

Rules

The race runs for exactly 24 hours; the winner is the car that covers the greatest distance. Entries compete in classes — currently the top Hypercar class (LMH/LMDh prototypes) and GT cars (LMGT3). Each car is crewed by several drivers (three in the top class) who share the driving in stints; regulations cap how long any single driver may drive (for example, no more than four hours within any six-hour window and no more than 14 hours in total) to keep fatigue and risk low. Refueling and repairs are only allowed in the pits. To be classified, a car must cross the finish line under its own power after the 24 hours. Variants: sprint races in the Le Mans Cup series and the historic Group C era used different technical regulations, and the 24 Hours of Le Mans motorcycle race follows its own rulebook.

History

The race was created by ACO secretary Georges Durand; the first edition ran on 23–24 June 1923 and was won by André Lagache and René Leonard in a Chenard-Walcker. Ford's 1-2-3 finish in 1966 with Bruce McLaren, Chris Amon and Ken Miles remains legendary. Mazda became the first Japanese manufacturer to win in 1991 with the rotary-engined 787B. Tom Kristensen holds the record with nine victories; Jacky Ickx has six and Derek Bell five.
