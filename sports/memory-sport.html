Memory Sport

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Memory sport (competitive memory, "mental athletics") is the mind sport in which competitors memorize and recall as much information as possible in fixed disciplines and time limits — the ten classic disciplines range from one-hour numbers and one-hour decks of cards to five-minute historic dates and speed cards, where a shuffled deck of 52 cards must be memorized as fast as possible (World Memory Sports Council, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The World Memory Championships have been held annually since 1991 (except 1992), founded in London by Tony Buzan and chess grandmaster Raymond Keene, and are organized by the World Memory Sports Council (WMSC) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Since a 2016 dispute, the International Association of Memory (IAM) runs a parallel world championship, so two world champions are crowned each year: the WMSC championship 2026 runs 11–13 December in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, and the IAM championship 2026 runs 17–19 December in Kuta, Bali, Indonesia (Art of Memory, n.d.). The reigning champions are Naranzul Otgon-Ulaan of Mongolia (WMSC) and Vishvaa Rajakumar of India (IAM), and women compete at the very top — Katie Kermode of Great Britain holds the 15-minute names-and-faces world record with 224 names (IAM World Championship 2018) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Art of Memory, n.d.).

Rules

The ten classical disciplines are: one-hour numbers; five-minute numbers; spoken numbers (read one per second); 30-minute binary digits; one-hour playing cards; 15-minute random words; 15-minute names and faces; five-minute historic/fictional dates; 15-minute abstract images (WMSC) or five-minute random images (IAM); and speed cards, always the final discipline, in which the competitor memorizes the order of one shuffled 52-card deck as fast as possible (World Memory Sports Council, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Scoring awards points per correctly recalled item (numbers, words, names, images) or, for cards, per deck in order, with penalties for errors; speed cards is scored purely on time (World Memory Sports Council, n.d.). A world champion must reach the published minimum total points across the ten disciplines — for 2026 the WMSC sets 2,099 points as the minimum in categories with fewer than three competitors (World Memory Sports Council, n.d.). Competitions run in senior, junior (under 18) and kids (under 12) categories, may be held in person or online, and results feed the official WMSC/IAM world rankings (Art of Memory, n.d.). There are no separate men's and women's disciplines — all categories are open, and women hold world records and world titles in the same events (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

History

Trained memory has been taught since ancient Greece — the "art of memory" with method of loci, linking and chunking — but competitive memory as a sport began only in the late 20th century (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Tony Buzan (1942–2019), the British author who popularized mind mapping and wrote Use Your Memory and Master Your Memory, and chess grandmaster Raymond Keene founded the World Memory Championships, first held in London in 1991 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). England's Dominic O'Brien won a record eight titles (1991, 1993, 1995–1997, 1999–2001) and wrote the classic How to Develop a Perfect Memory; compatriots Andi Bell, Ben Pridmore and Alex Mullen each added three titles (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Other icons include North Korea's Ryu Song I, the 2019 WMSC world champion who holds the all-time best hour-numbers (4,620 digits) and 30-minute binary (7,485 digits) marks, Mongolia's Munkhshur Narmandakh (WMSC champion 2017 and 2021), and China's Wang Feng (2010–2011) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). After a 2016 dispute, the IAM was launched and has run its own world championship since 2017, with Andrea Muzii of Italy the 2019 IAM champion (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
