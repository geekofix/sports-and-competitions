Chin Lone

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Chin Lone is the traditional bare-knuckle fighting style of Myanmar's villages — cloth- or gauze-wrapped hands, no gloves — the rural root of modern lethwei, still fought at festivals and in village rings: punches, kicks, knees, elbows and headbutts, decided by knockout or, traditionally, by the crowd's judgement. It differs from sport lethwei mainly in its informality (no weight classes, no timed rounds, often fought on earth). Men fight in chin-lone; women's participation is rare and culturally restricted in the traditional setting, though modern lethwei has women's divisions. Within the combat sports and martial arts on this site, its closest relatives are Lethwei, Bando, Muay Thai, Kun Khmer; the related pages are listed under Important Links (Black Belt Magazine, n.d.).

Rules

Traditional chin-lone bouts have no fixed rules: two fighters, hand-wrapped in cloth strips, fight until one is knocked out, cannot continue, or concedes; village elders/judges decide disputed outcomes; no rounds or time limit. Modern competitions that include chin-lone heritage (village tournaments, WNG-style events) add safety rules: medical checks, hand-wrap inspection, referee stoppage, sometimes 3×2-minute rounds. Headbutts, elbows, knees and kicks are all permitted — the "art of nine limbs" of lethwei (Black Belt Magazine, n.d.).

History

Bare-knuckle fighting is documented in Burmese records from the Pagan Kingdom era (9th–13th centuries); it served as military training, a rite of passage and popular entertainment, and became a spectator sport in the late 1800s — this village form is chin lone. Lethwei developed from it into the regulated ring sport (traditional lethwei, with hand-wraps but no gloves). Famous traditional fighters include Lone Chaw, who rose from village bouts in the Ayeyarwady Delta to national titles; the World Lethwei Federation sanctions the modern sport internationally (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
