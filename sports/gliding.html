Gliding

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Gliding (sailplane flying) is the air sport of flying unpowered aircraft (gliders/sailplanes) using rising air — thermals, ridge lift and wave — to stay aloft and cover long distances or race around courses. It is governed by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI), which runs the FAI World Gliding Championships (since 1950) in classes such as 18 m, Open, Club and Grand Prix, and sets gliding world records.

Rules

At FAI World Gliding Championships and Grand Prix events, pilots fly assigned tasks: either racing tasks (speed around turnpoints, scored by handicapped speed) or distance/area tasks (greatest distance or speed over a declared course). Competitors start together or at intervals, and flights are recorded by GNSS loggers, which are scored automatically; the fastest handicapped time (or greatest distance) wins. Classes include Open and 18 m (the premier ones), plus Club class and a Women's World Championship. FAI records cover straight distance, goal-and-return, altitude gain and speed over 100/300/500 km courses. Pilots need a glider pilot license, and competitions follow the FAI Sporting Code (Section 3 — Gliding).

History

Gliding began with Otto Lilienthal's controlled flights in Germany in the 1890s; the first gliding competitions were held in the Wasserkuppe Rhön contests of the 1920s. The FAI, founded 1905, has recognized gliding records since 1930 and held the first World Gliding Championships in 1950. Gliding clubs spread worldwide, and motor gliders and paragliding grew out of it. Record-setters include Hans-Werner Grosse (Germany), who set over 50 world records, and Klaus Ohlmann (Germany, multiple distance records over 3,000 km). Today the FAI runs World Championships biennially and the Sailplane Grand Prix series.
