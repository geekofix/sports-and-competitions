Cirit

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Cirit (jereed, “equestrian javelin”) is the traditional Turkish equestrian team sport in which two teams of horsemen score points by throwing blunt wooden javelins at each other — a hit, an outride or a mid-air catch all score. It is governed today by the Traditional Sports Federation of Turkey, with international championships organized since 2011 and major festivals in Erzurum and other eastern provinces; cirit was a medal sport at the 2016 World Nomad Games (cancelled in 2018) and is on the path to UNESCO intangible-heritage listing. The Turkish defense company ROKETSAN named its 70 mm laser-guided missile after the game. Men's and, increasingly, women's clubs play cirit today. Within the Central Asian nomad games on this site, its closest relatives are Khuviin Tamir, Mongolian Archery, Mounted Archery; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

Two teams of horsemen line up facing each other on a large grass field, each player about 10 m from the next; with the right hand a player holds the first cirit — a dried oak or poplar stick 70–100 cm long and 2–3 cm in diameter with a rubber-tipped blunt end — while waiting players hold theirs in the left hand. Riders gallop toward the opposing line and throw at opponents; a direct hit, catching an incoming javelin in mid-air, or outriding the opponent all score points, and defenders use acrobatic maneuvers (leaning to the side, under the horse's neck) to avoid being hit. “Cirit boys” run out to retrieve errant javelins and deliver them to the end zones. Referees — experienced former players — count the hits and announce the winner; feuds between players can be settled by putting them on the same team. Horses are usually not large and at least four years old; traditional regional costumes of the Ottoman sipahis are often worn.

History

Jereed was the essential sporting and ceremonial game of the Turkic peoples of the Central Asian steppes, where life revolved around the horse, and it was carried to Anatolia during the westward migrations of the 11th century. It was played at weddings, religious holidays and fairs, and was banned by Sultan Mahmud II in 1826 for its danger, only to regain popularity; the first modern cirit club was founded in 1957, and the sport is now strongest in Erzurum, Bayburt, Kars, Erzincan, Artvin, Uşak, Balıkesir, Diyarbakır, Siirt and Konya. It was a demonstration sport at the 2014 World Nomad Games and a medal sport at the 2016 Games. A painting from around 1809 shows two men playing cirid — the game's long visual history.
