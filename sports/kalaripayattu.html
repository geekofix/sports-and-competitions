Kalaripayattu

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Kalaripayattu is a martial art that originated in Kerala, in southwestern India, during the 11th–12th centuries CE, when indigenous Dravidian combat traditions of the Sangam period merged with Dhanur Vedic traditions and later Chinese influences (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The name combines the Malayalam kalari (training ground) and payattu (practice), so the art is literally "practice in the arts of the battlefield" (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Training takes place in a kalari and covers empty-hand striking and grappling, weapons such as the sword and shield, spear, dagger and the flexible urumi sword, plus the study of marma — the 107 vital points of the body — and Ayurvedic healing (kalari chikitsa) for injuries sustained in training (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Because legend traces the art to the sage Parasurama and, through Bodhidharma, to the origins of Chinese kung fu, it is often called the "mother of all martial arts" (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The art was suppressed under British rule after 1793 and remained restricted until the early 20th century, when it was revived; the Indian Kalaripayattu Federation, established in 1995, now organizes national championships in India (Indian Kalaripayattu Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Among today's leading practitioners is Meenakshi Amma Gurukkal (born 1942) of Vadakara, Kerala, who has run the Kadathanad Kalari Sangham since 1949, trains 150–160 students a year including many women, and received the Padma Shri in 2017 — the only female practitioner ever so honored (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

Rules

Kalaripayattu has no single world governing body; in India the competitive sport is organized by the Kalaripayattu Federation of India (KFI) and the Indian Kalaripayattu Federation (IKF), which run state-level and national championships with age and weight categories for men and women (Indian Kalaripayattu Federation, n.d.; Kalaripayattu Federation of India, n.d.). The traditional curriculum is taught by a gurukkal (master) in a kalari and progresses from body conditioning and footwork (chuvadu) through empty-hand forms, paired drills and weapons practice with wooden practice weapons before live blades are used (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Weapon sets include the sword and shield (val and kedaham), the spear (vel), the dagger and the long flexible steel whip-sword (urumi), the staff (muchan) and the battle axe (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In competitive bouts, points are awarded for controlled, correctly executed techniques on agreed target areas, and direct strikes to marma points or any technique intended to cause serious injury are prohibited; the art also preserves the therapeutic system kalari chikitsa, in which masters treat dislocations and injuries with massage and herbal medicine (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Historically the art was practiced almost exclusively by men of the warrior class, but today women train and compete at national level in India, and schools such as the Kadathanad Kalari Sangham under Meenakshi Amma Gurukkal have made women's training a particular focus (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). Rule source: the national federations KFI and IKF, which codify the sport format of traditional Kalaripayattu (Indian Kalaripayattu Federation, n.d.; Kalaripayattu Federation of India, n.d.).

History

The historian Philip Zarrilli traces the precursors of Kalaripayattu to the Dravidian combat techniques of the Sangam period (600 BCE–300 CE) and to Sanskritic Dhanur Vedic traditions, in which warriors trained in target practice, horse and elephant riding and the weapons of the period (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). According to legend, Parasurama, the sixth avatar of Vishnu, learned the art from Shiva and established the first 108 kalaris in Kerala along with the first 21 gurus (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). After the British conquest of Malabar, martial training was banned from 1793 and again restricted from 1805, and the kalaris went underground until the late 19th century (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The great revivalist was Kottackkal Kanaran Gurukkal (1850–1941), who sold about 260 acres of land to preserve the tradition and is called the "Dronacharya of Kalaripayattu"; an estimated 60 percent of today's kalaris trace their lineage to him (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Other pivotal masters include C.V. Narayanan Nair of the CVN Kalari and Sankaranarayana Menon (1929–2023), known as Unni Gurukkal, who received the Padma Shri in 2022 and spread Kalaripayattu to more than 50 countries through branches in the United States, the United Kingdom, the UAE, France and Belgium (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). On the women's side, Meenakshi Amma Gurukkal (born 1942) became a defining icon, receiving the Padma Shri in 2017 for keeping the tradition alive and opening it to women practitioners (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).
