Kalaripayattu

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Kalaripayattu (kalari = training ground, payattu = combat practice) is the ancient martial art of Kerala in South India, often called the "mother of all martial arts". It combines strikes, kicks, grappling, weapon forms (sword, dagger, spear, staff, shield, bow and arrow), vital-point techniques (marma vidya) and a traditional healing massage system (kalari chikitsa). Training takes place in a sunken earth-floored pit called a kalari, overseen by a master (gurukkal). The Kalaripayattu Federation of India (KFI), based in Kozhikode and recognized by the Indian Olympic Association, governs the competitive sport.

Rules

Training follows a strict ritual: the gurukkal lights a lamp on the seven-tiered sacred platform (poothara), the student salutes the deity of the kalari, and weapons training traditionally comes before unarmed techniques. A distinctive feature is marma vidya: knowledge of the roughly 107 vital points of the body, which can be used to incapacitate an opponent. In modern competition, kalaripayattu is contested under federation rules with protective equipment in sparring bouts, while traditional forms (kata-like sequences with weapons) are judged by masters. Competitions are held at district, state and national level in Kerala and across India.

History

Kalaripayattu developed in Kerala from the 11th-12th centuries, with roots claimed in the Sangam-era warrior training of South India and the Dhanur Veda; legend credits the sage Parashurama with establishing the first 108 kalaris. The martial ballads Vadakkan Pattukal celebrate its warriors, and the late medieval period is considered its golden age. The British suppressed the art after their conquest of Malabar, banning kalari training, and it was revived after Indian independence in 1947. Two major styles survive: the Vadakkan (Northern) style of Malabar, emphasizing fluid movement and weapons, and the Thekkan (Southern) style of Travancore, emphasizing powerful unarmed techniques. A popular legend holds that the monk Bodhidharma carried the art to China, where it influenced kung fu.
