Royal Shrovetide Football

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Royal Shrovetide Football is a mob football game played annually on Shrove Tuesday and Ash Wednesday in the Derbyshire market town of Ashbourne, England. Two teams — the Up'ards, traditionally those born north of Henmore Brook, and the Down'ards, born south of it — contest a hand-painted, cork-filled leather ball through the town's streets, fields and river, trying to "goal" it at their own end of town (Ashbourne Town Council, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It is one of the oldest surviving mass football games in Britain and the only one to carry the "Royal" prefix. The game is open to women and men alike: thousands of players of both sexes take part each year, and a recent public campaign has pressed for women to be allowed to perform the ceremonial "turn-up" of the ball (DerbyshireLive, 2026). In the 2026 edition, played 17–18 February, the Up'ards won 3–0 — the first 3–0 victory since 1967 — with Craig Brown goaling on day one and Josh Redfern and Ben Sellers on day two, reversing the Down'ards' 1–0 win of 2025 (DerbyshireLive, 2026; BBC, 2026).

Rules

The match is played over two days, each from 2 p.m. until 10 p.m., beginning with the ceremonial "turn-up" of the ball to the crowd at the Shrovetide plinth in the Shaw Croft car park by a local dignitary or guest (Ashbourne Town Council, n.d.). Sides are set by family tradition and place of birth relative to Henmore Brook, and each team tries to carry, kick or throw the ball to its own goal three miles (4.8 km) away — the Up'ards to the Sturston Mill goal in the east, the Down'ards to the Clifton Mill goal in the west. A goal is scored by tapping the ball three times against the goal plinth; since the original mills were demolished (Clifton Mill 1967, Sturston Mill 1981) and purpose-built stone goals were erected on the banks of the Henmore in 1996, goaling requires standing in the river (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). If a goal is scored before roughly 5 or 6 p.m., a fresh ball is turned up and play resumes; after that time a goal ends the day, and if no goal is scored by 10 p.m. play simply ends. The scorer keeps the ball as a trophy and is carried shoulder-high to the Green Man Royal Hotel, where the goal is validated; beyond that the game has almost no laws — the traditional prohibitions are murder, unnecessary violence, playing on private property, cemeteries or churchyards, hiding the ball in bags, and transporting it by motor vehicle (Ashbourne Town Council, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

History

Shrovetide ball games have been played in England since at least the 12th century, and the Ashbourne game — also called "hugball" — is documented from at least c. 1667, though a fire at the committee office in the 1890s destroyed the earliest records (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). An account from 1797 by the schoolboy John Arkwright describes a Shrovetide match between Ashbourne Grammar School boys and the town; a poem by Charles Cotton in his "Burlesque upon the Great Frost" (1683) already mentions football at Ashbourne. Originally the game pitted the separate townships of Ashbourne and Compton against each other; when Compton was amalgamated into Ashbourne in the 1860s, the modern Up'ards–Down'ards divide was fixed. The Shrovetide Anthem was written in 1891 to raise funds for street-playing fines (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The "Royal" title dates from 1922, when the town presented Princess Mary with a Shrovetide ball on her Shrove Tuesday wedding; the ball has been turned up by princes of Wales in 1928 (the future Edward VIII) and 2003 (Prince Charles), and by footballer Sir Stanley Matthews in 1966 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). A ball goaled by H. Hind on Ash Wednesday 1887 survives and predates the record-destroying fire. Ashbourne-born soldiers even played the game in France during the First World War (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
