Royal Shrovetide Football

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Royal Shrovetide Football is a mob football game played annually on Shrove Tuesday and Ash Wednesday in the Derbyshire market town of Ashbourne, England. Two teams — the Up'ards, traditionally those born north of Henmore Brook, and the Down'ards, born south of it — contest a hand-painted leather ball through the town's streets, fields and river, attempting to "goal" it at their own mill goal three miles away. It is one of the oldest and largest surviving mass football games in Britain and the only one to carry the "Royal" prefix.

Rules

The match is played over two days, each from 2 p.m. until 10 p.m., beginning with the ceremonial "turn-up" of the ball to the crowd in the Shaw Croft car park by a local dignitary or guest. Sides are set by family tradition and place of birth relative to Henmore Brook, and each team tries to carry, kick or throw the ball to its own goal — the Up'ards to the old Sturston Mill site, the Down'ards to the old Clifton Mill site — where goals are scored by tapping the ball three times against the millstone plinth; since the original mills' demolition (Clifton Mill in 1967, Sturston Mill in 1981), purpose-built stone goals on the banks of the Henmore have marked the sites, three miles (4.8 km) apart, and goaling famously requires standing in the river. If a goal is scored before 5 or 6 p.m., a fresh ball is turned up and play resumes; after that, a goal ends the day. The scorer keeps the ball as a trophy and is carried shoulder-high back to the Green Man Royal Hotel, where officials validate the goal, and the game has almost no other laws: murder and the transport of the ball by motor vehicle are the traditional prohibitions, and the events are stewarded by the Shrovetide committee rather than refereed.

History

The game is of at least medieval origin: an account from 1797 by the schoolboy John Arkwright writes home about a Shrovetide football match between Grammar School boys and the town, and similar mob football was played across England before the 1863 codification of association football. Originally the game pitted the separate townships of Ashbourne and Compton against each other, a structure that changed when Compton was amalgamated into Ashbourne in the 1860s, fixing the modern Up'ards–Down'ards divide; the Shrovetide Anthem was written in 1891 for a concert raising funds to pay street-playing fines. The "Royal" title dates from 1922, when the town presented Princess Mary with a Shrovetide ball on her Shrove Tuesday wedding, and the game was turned up by a Prince of Wales twice, in 1928 (the future Edward VIII) and in 2003 (Prince Charles). The purpose-built stone goals were erected in 1996 on the Henmore's banks to make goaling more challenging, Clifton's obelisk having served since 1968 and Sturston's salvaged mill-post since 1981; the game survived two world wars — Ashbourne-born soldiers even played it in France during the First — and continues today as a two-day festival that draws thousands of players and spectators, with goals often taking many hours and some days ending goalless.
