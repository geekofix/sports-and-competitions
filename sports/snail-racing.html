Snail Racing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Snail racing is the humorous novelty competition in which garden snails — usually the common garden snail Cornu aspersum — race along a damp track laid on a table, in small-groups at village f\u00eates and in named "world championship" events. Its flagship is the World Snail Racing Championships, held since the 1960s at Congham in Norfolk, England, each July.

Rules

Races are usually run on a circular track formed by a damp cloth on top of a table, with a radius traditionally of 13 or 14 inches (33\u201336 cm): snails are placed in a starting circle in the middle and race outward to the perimeter, the first shell to cross the edge winning. Each snail must be the same common species (typically the garden snail), and the moisture of the track is essential — the damp cloth both encourages the snails to move and keeps the race fair; racing numbers are painted on the shells or stickers/tags placed on them to distinguish competitors. Heats are short (the benchmark for "world class" is a time of two minutes over the 13-inch course, set in 1995 by a snail named Archie), and inattentive or reclusive snails that settle into their shells get encouragement from their owners — but must race unaided. Disqualifications are rare; a waterlogged course (as in 2007) simply cancels the event, because snails refuse to race in the rain.

History

The sport's English home came about in the 1960s, when Tom Elwes of Congham saw a snail race while on holiday in France and brought the idea home to Norfolk; the World Snail Racing Championships were founded there and the first records date from around 1970, with the event staged annually ever since in the village — today at Grimston Cricket Field near Congham Hall, on a Saturday in July, often drawing two hundred or more entrants and raising money for local causes (about \u00a3500 for the village church in recent years). Famous champions include the 2008 winner Heikki Kovalainen — a snail named after the Formula One driver, which took the crown in 3 minutes 2 seconds — while Archie's 1995 two-minute benchmark remains the measure of greatness; race-goers enjoy the "form study is difficult because they hide inside their shells" problem, as the racing pundit John McCririck once observed. Snail racing is also staged in other counties and countries (usually as a f\u00eate or fundraising event), but Congham remains the sport's spiritual home, its slow-and-steady world title the most famous in gastropod sport.
