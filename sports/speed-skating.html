Speed Skating

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Speed skating — long-track speed skating — is the Olympic ice-racing discipline in which skaters race in pairs against the clock over set distances on a 400-metre double-lane oval, from the 500-metre sprint to the 10,000-metre endurance races. It was the first winter sport to acquire an international federation (the International Skating Union, founded 1892) and has been on every Winter Olympic programme since the first Games of 1924, with its World Cup circuit, world championships and mass Dutch fan culture making it one of winter sport's historic cornerstones.

Rules

The standard long-track rink is a 400 m oval (smaller tracks of 200\u2013333 m exist) with two lanes: skaters race in pairs, counterclockwise, one starting in the inner and one in the outer lane, changing lanes each lap so both cover the same distance; infringements on the pairmate are heavily penalised, with skaters permitted to leave the inner lane only if not interfering. The classical individual distances of the World Cup and Olympics are 500 m, 1000 m, 1500 m, 3000/5000 m and 5000/10,000 m for men and women respectively — each a single timed race decided on hundredths of a second — plus the Team Pursuit (added to the Olympics in 2006) and the Mass Start, a pack race over 16 laps with intermediate sprints (an Olympic event since 2018), and a Team Sprint event. Results at championships are single-distance titles (since 1996 a World Single Distance Championships has complemented the historic Allround format of combined distances); klapskates, skin suits and aerodynamics are strictly regulated, and the sport's world records on the big ovals of Salt Lake City, Calgary and Heerenveen's Thialf are its most prized statistics.

History

Speed skating was born in the canal-and-lake countries of northern Europe, with organised racing on frozen Dutch waterways from the seventeenth century and the first formal races in Britain in the eighteenth; the ISU was founded in 1892 — the first international federation of any winter sport — and staged world championships over four distances (500 m, 1500 m, 5000 m and 10,000 m) with an all-round combined title from the early years. The sport entered the Olympics at Chamonix 1924 (with an extra allround title awarded that year), when Clas Thunberg of Finland became its first superstar; women raced as a demonstration in 1932 before their official Olympic programme began in 1960. The professional International Speedskating League (1972\uu201374, built around Ard Schenk and Jonny Nilsson) briefly broke the amateur era's hold, before the modern age: the klapskate revolution of the 1990s (Dutch invention) slashed times, indoor ovals spread worldwide, and the World Cup circuit drove the sport global — with the Netherlands its superpower (Thialf, Sven Kramer, Ireen W\u00fcst, Jorien ter Mors-era champions deeply embedded in Dutch culture), Norway, South Korea strong in sprint distances, and stars such as Eric Heiden, who won five golds at Lake Placid 1980, and today's mass-start and pursuit specialists completing the sport's long Olympic roll of honour.
