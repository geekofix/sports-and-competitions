Ladies Gaelic Football

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Ladies' Gaelic football is the women's version of Gaelic football, one of the four Gaelic games governed on the island of Ireland by the GAA family of organizations. It is played by two teams of 15 on the same full-size pitch as the men's game (from under-14 up), with the same H-shaped goals and the same scoring — a goal under the crossbar is worth 3 points, over the bar 1 point — but with a slightly smaller ball (size 4) and modified rules for contact. The Ladies' Gaelic Football Association (LGFA), founded in 1974, governs the women's game, and its pinnacle event is the All-Ireland Senior Ladies' Football Championship, whose final is held at Croke Park, Dublin.

Rules

Under the LGFA Official Guide and Playing Rules, a senior championship match consists of two halves of 30 minutes, and each team fields 15 players with rolling substitutions. The rules of play closely follow men's Gaelic football: the ball may be carried for four steps, then must be bounced or soloed, and may be kicked or hand-passed; only the goalkeeper may handle the ball inside the goal area. Ladies' rules make some contact illegal where the men's game permits it, and the smaller size-4 ball is used. Fouls are penalized with free kicks, and cynical play with yellow/red cards and a sin-bin. The All-Ireland final at Croke Park draws tens of thousands of spectators.

History

Women have played Gaelic football in Ireland since the early 20th century, and the Ladies' Gaelic Football Association was founded in 1974 to organize the game properly; it now has thousands of clubs across all 32 counties. The first All-Ireland Senior Ladies' Championship was played in 1974, won by Tipperary, and the final moved to Croke Park in the early 2000s, underlining the game's growth. Ladies' Gaelic football is also played by Irish communities in Britain, the US, Canada, Australia and beyond. Stars such as Cora Staunton of Mayo, one of the most decorated players in the history of the game, have raised its profile, and the LGFA works toward full integration with the GAA and the Camogie Association.
