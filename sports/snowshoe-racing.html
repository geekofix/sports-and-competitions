Snowshoe Racing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Snowshoe racing (snowshoe running) is winter cross-country running on snowshoes — a sport with organised roots in nineteenth-century Canada and a modern world championship circuit under the World Snowshoe Federation (WSSF). Races are run on groomed or packed snow courses from 5 km sprints to half marathons, at club, national and world level, and the discipline is part of the Special Olympics and Arctic Winter Games programmes.

Rules

Racing snowshoes are small, light running models — much smaller than recreational snowshoes, fixed with secure bindings to ordinary running shoes — with metal crampons for grip on packed snow. Races are mass-start cross-country events over snow, typically 5 km and 10 km at championship level, with junior distances shorter and some events running to half marathon; a competitor must have both snowshoes on when crossing the finish line (a Special Olympics rule standard at club level too), and courses follow groomed trails with lane discipline at the start. National championships (e.g. the U.S. national championships staged each March by the United States Snowshoe Association, which organises a season of qualifying races) and the WSSF's world championships crown the sport's champions; relay and sprint formats appear on many programmes, including the Arctic Winter Games and Special Olympics Snowshoeing events (50 m to 800 m distances and relays for its athletes).

History

The snowshoe itself is ancient — developed in Central Asia around 4000 BC and refined independently in North America and Europe, with the webbed racing snowshoe of Algonquin-style design perfected by American Indians — and snowshoe racing became an organised club sport in Victorian Canada: the Montreal Snow Shoe Club (formally reorganised in 1850) staged "tramps" and races through the mid-nineteenth century, and the Canadian snowshoe associations of the 1880s codified championships, so the sport's competitive pedigree predates organised skiing. After a long dormancy the sport revived as a running discipline late in the twentieth century: the United States Snowshoe Association was formed to govern the revived racing circuit, national championships were staged annually in venues such as Cable, Wisconsin, and the La Ciaspolada in Italy's Dolomites — a 10 km race drawing 5,000-plus entrants — became the world's biggest snowshoe event. The International Snowshoe Federation (renamed the World Snowshoe Federation in 2015, founded 2010) launched world championships in 2010 in Vancouver (a "World Snowshoe Invitational" 10 km staged during the 2010 Winter Olympics) and has since staged them at venues including Quebec City, Vezza d'Oglio in Italy and Saranac Lake, New York; today the sport thrives wherever runners meet snow, with elite mountain runners crossing over each winter.
