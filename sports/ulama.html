Ulama

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Ulama is the living descendant of the ancient Mesoamerican ballgame (ullamaliztli in Nahuatl): a team contest played on a long, narrow court in which players strike a heavy solid rubber ball with the hip and keep it in play, above all in the Mexican state of Sinaloa (Wikipedia, n.d.). Regarded as one of the oldest continuously played ball sports in the world and the oldest known game played with a rubber ball, ulama was shown as an exhibition at the Mexico City 1968 Olympic Games, and FIFA president Gianni Infantino has called it the oldest precursor of football (Wikipedia, n.d.; Mexicolore, n.d.). Three forms survive: ulama de cadera (hip ulama) with teams of 5–12 players, a 3–4 kg rubber ball, leather hip pads and loincloths; ulama de antebrazo (forearm ulama) with 1–3 players on a smaller court with a lighter ball — a form traditionally played by women; and ulama de mazo (paddle ulama) with a 6–7 kg wooden paddle and a ball of about 500 g (Wikipedia, n.d.). Modern organized play includes AJUPEME USA — seven players a side, two 20-minute halves, an approximately 8 lb (3.6 kg), 8-inch ball made of Castilla-tree rubber and sulfur, hip contact only — and the Pok-Ta-Pok World Cup series held since 2015 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Women play their own forearm form and compete in modern women's teams under the same court rules as men (Mexicolore, n.d.).

Rules

The traditional game is played on a long, narrow court called a tastei, divided by a centre line (analco) and bounded at each end by an end line (chichi or chivo); in hip ulama, teams of five to twelve strike a roughly 3–4 kg solid rubber ball using only the padded hip or upper thigh, wearing leather hip pads and loincloths (Wikipedia, n.d.). A team scores a point (raya) when the opponents fail to return the ball, let it stop before it crosses the centre line, touch it with a wrong body part or commit an error such as touching a teammate; the first side to the agreed total wins, and under traditional scoring the score can reset, making matches long (Wikipedia, n.d.). The forearm form (ulama de antebrazo) uses a smaller, lighter ball on a shorter court with one to three players per side and is the form most often played by women, while the paddle form (ulama de mazo) uses a heavy wooden paddle and a much lighter ball for three to four players (Wikipedia, n.d.; Mexicolore, n.d.). In the standardized modern format played by AJUPEME USA and allied associations, seven players a side contest two 20-minute halves on a marked court, using an 8-inch, roughly 8 lb ball of Castilla rubber and sulfur with hip-only contact (Wikipedia, n.d.). There is no single world federation; the game is governed locally by community authorities and by associations such as AJUPEME and the Belize Hipball Association (Wikipedia, n.d.).

History

The Mesoamerican ballgame was played from at least the second millennium BC — rubber balls date to about 1600 BC — by the Olmec, Maya and Aztec civilizations, and roughly 1,500 ancient ball courts survive archaeologically, tied to ritual and cosmology (Wikipedia, n.d.; Mexicolore, n.d.). The Spanish suppressed the game after the conquest, but it survived in the rural communities of Sinaloa, where it has been passed down for centuries as ulama (Wikipedia, n.d.). Modern revival began in the twentieth century, including an exhibition at the Mexico City 1968 Olympic Games, and organized associations followed: AJUPEME was founded by Armando Uscanga and Reyna Puc, the Federación Mexicana de Ulama (FEMUC) was founded in 2018 in Quintana Roo, and the Belize Hipball Association governs the game in Belize (Wikipedia, n.d.). Since 2015 the Pok-Ta-Pok World Cup has been held at ancient and modern sites — 2015 Chichén Itzá, 2017 Guatemala City, 2018, 2021 and 2023 Orange Walk (Belize), 2019 San Salvador and 2022 Xcaret — organized by the ACCDAPM association (Wikipedia, n.d.).
