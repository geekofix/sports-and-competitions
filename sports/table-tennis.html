Table Tennis

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Table tennis (ping pong) is the racket sport of hitting a lightweight ball back and forth across a 2.74 m table divided by a net, with rallies faster than the eye can easily track; it has been an Olympic sport since Seoul 1988 and has the largest playing population of any racket sport, dominated for decades by China (International Table Tennis Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The world governing body is the International Table Tennis Federation (ITTF), and the professional tour is run by World Table Tennis (WTT) with Grand Smash, Champions and Contender events plus world rankings (WTT, n.d.). At the Paris 2024 Olympics, held at Paris Expo Porte de Versailles from 27 July to 10 August 2024 with five events, China swept the podium in several disciplines: Chen Meng won the women's singles gold ahead of teammate Sun Yingsha, Fan Zhendong won the men's singles over Sweden's Truls Möregårdh, and Wang Chuqin and Sun Yingsha took the mixed doubles and men's team titles with Fan Zhendong and Ma Long (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The 2025 World Table Tennis Championships in Doha crowned Wang Chuqin (over Brazil's Hugo Calderano) and Sun Yingsha (over Wang Manyu) as singles champions, and at the centenary 2026 World Team Championships in London (28 April–10 May 2026) China won both team titles (Wikipedia, n.d.-c; Wikipedia, n.d.-d). At the 2026 WTT Singapore Smash, world No. 1 Sun Yingsha beat Wang Manyu 4–2 for her third Smash title while Wang Chuqin beat Lin Yun-ju 4–0 (WTT, n.d.).

Among the current top women — named first here — are world No. 1 and 2025 world champion Sun Yingsha, Paris 2024 Olympic champion Chen Meng, Wang Manyu, and Europe's leading players Bernadette Szőcs of Romania (2022 European champion), Annett Kaufmann and Sabine Winter of Germany, who all feature at the top of the WTT world rankings (WTT, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). On the men's side the leading names are world No. 1 and 2025 world champion Wang Chuqin, Paris 2024 Olympic champion Fan Zhendong, Olympic bronze medalist Félix Lebrun of France, silver medalist Truls Möregårdh and Brazil's Hugo Calderano (WTT, n.d.; Olympics, n.d.).

Rules

The table is 2.74 m long, 1.525 m wide and 76 cm high, with a 15.25 cm net across the middle; the ball is 40 mm (40+ mm plastic since 2014) and weighs 2.7 g, and rackets may have red and black rubber on the two sides (International Table Tennis Federation, n.d.). A serve must be struck from an open palm behind the end line, tossed at least 16 cm, and land first on the server's side and then the receiver's; service alternates every two points, and at deuce (10–10) every point (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). A game is won at 11 points with a two-point margin, and matches are best of five or seven games; in doubles, partners alternate hits and the serve rotates in fixed order (International Table Tennis Federation, n.d.). A let is called when the serve touches the net and lands correctly or play is interrupted; a player loses the point for touching the table, moving it, or hitting the ball twice (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Olympic events are men's and women's singles, men's and women's team (three players, five matches each) and mixed doubles; the ITTF Laws of Table Tennis also govern equipment inspections, racket surface rules (one side red, one black) and umpire procedures (Olympics, n.d.). Men and women play under identical rules, tables and scoring; only the draw seeding differs (International Table Tennis Federation, n.d.).

History

Table tennis grew out of after-dinner parlour games in Victorian England in the 1880s — books as nets, cork or champagne-cork balls — and was commercialized under the trademarked name "ping pong" around 1890 before the generic "table tennis" prevailed (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The International Table Tennis Federation was founded in Berlin in 1926 by Ivor Montagu and others, and the first World Championships were held in London in December 1926, won by Hungary's Roland Jacobi and Mária Mednyánszky (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). Sponge rubber, introduced by Japanese players in the 1950s, revolutionized the game, and China's emergence from the 1960s made it the sport's superpower; the 40 mm ball replaced the 38 mm ball in 2000, plastic (poly) balls in 2014, and the speed-glue era ended with the 2008 ban (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Olympic men's singles champions tell the story: Yoo Nam-kyu (1988), Jan-Ove Waldner (1992), Liu Guoliang (1996), Kong Linghui (2000), Ryu Seung-min (2004), Ma Lin (2008), Zhang Jike (2012), Ma Long (2016, 2020) and Fan Zhendong (2024); on the women's side Deng Yaping (1992, 1996), Wang Nan (2000), Zhang Yining (2004, 2008), Li Xiaoxia (2012) and Chen Meng (2024) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
