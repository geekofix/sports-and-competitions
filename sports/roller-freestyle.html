Roller Freestyle

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Roller freestyle, also known as freestyle slalom skating, is a roller sport in which skaters perform tricks through lines of cones — spinning, jumping and sliding on inline or quad skates — judged for technical difficulty, style and flow. It developed from street slalom skating in the 1990s and is governed internationally by World Skate, whose Inline Freestyle Technical Commission publishes the official rulebook and stages world championships as part of the World Skate Games; the sport's events are often grouped with the jumps and slides of freestyle skating more broadly.

Rules

The competitive core of the discipline is freestyle slalom, contested in three main events: Classic, in which each skater performs a prepared two-minute programme with music through a line of 20 cones spaced at 50, 80 and 120 cm; Battle, a knockout format in which groups of skaters take short turns and are ranked by judges; and Pair slalom, in which two skaters perform together in a 160–180 second routine. Speed slalom is the sport's race event: a skater sprints on one foot through a line of 20 cones spaced 80 cm apart, with the first cone placed 12 metres from the start line, and times to the hundredth of a second decide the result. Judging criteria under the World Skate rulebook include technical difficulty, style and variety of tricks, with penalty deductions for cones hit or missed and for falls, and skaters are ranked by direct comparison of marks. Equipment is regulated for safety — helmets are mandatory for juniors and recommended at all levels — and competition surfaces must be flat, hard and smooth, with cones of standardised size and spacing.

History

Freestyle slalom skating grew out of the street skating scenes of France, Russia and China in the 1990s, where slalomers wove through urban obstacles and then standardised on plastic cones; dedicated competitions emerged in the early 2000s and an international structure followed, first through the World Slalom Skaters Association (WSSA), whose rulebooks from the 2000s shaped the Classic, Battle and Speed formats still used today. The sport's strongest national schools developed in China, Russia, France and Italy, and international federations ran world cup series across Europe and Asia. In 2017 World Skate — after absorbing the former FIRS structures — reorganised inline freestyle under its own technical commission and added it to the programme of the biennial World Skate Games, alongside European championships run by World Skate Europe Inline Freestyle, and the discipline today combines slalom, speed slalom and freestyle slides under one global rulebook, with the Roller Freestyle World Championships staged within the World Skate Games format.
