Bökh

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Bokh (Mongolian wrestling) is the traditional folk wrestling style of Mongolia and one of the "three games of men" at the Naadam festival, alongside horse racing and archery. Two wrestlers grapple standing, gripping the jacket (zodog) and shorts (shuudag), and the bout is lost the moment any part of the body other than the hands or feet touches the ground; there is no time limit and no weight class. The Mongolian Wrestling Federation (founded 1961, a member of United World Wrestling) governs the sport nationally, Naadam was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010, and bokh is also contested at the World Nomad Games (United World Wrestling [UWW], n.d.) (UNESCO, 2010) (World Nomad Games, n.d.). The 2026 Naadam festival ran July 11–13, 2026, in Ulaanbaatar with an expanded field of 1,024 wrestlers in a single-elimination tournament of ten rounds; Orkhonbayar Bayarsaikhan (born June 30, 1998), a State Lion title holder and captain of Mongolia's team on the 2025 Netflix series "Physical: Asia", won all ten rounds to become the 26th State Wide Champion (avarga) and was promoted directly to the highest rank, Dayan Avarga, under the new law (UWW, n.d.). The all-time record remains the 2011 Naadam, which drew 6,002 wrestlers — a Guinness World Record — with Sanjaadamba winning all thirteen rounds (Guinness World Records [GWR], n.d.).

On the women's side, wrestling at the Naadam festival is traditionally men-only, but women compete in the other two games of men — archery and horse racing — at Naadam, and women's Mongolian wrestling has grown in domestic competitions outside the festival (UNESCO, 2010) (World Nomad Games, n.d.).

Rules

Under the rules of the Mongolian Wrestling Federation, a bokh bout has no time limit and no weight classes: the two wrestlers start standing, after performing the eagle dance (devekh) with outstretched arms, and grip the opponent's zodog jacket and shuudag belt; the bout ends when any part of the body other than hands or feet touches the ground, or when a wrestler is thrown, pinned or forced out of the ring. The winner then performs the victory dance while the loser's zasuul (coach) sings praise (UWW, n.d.). At the National Naadam in Ulaanbaatar, 512 or 1,024 wrestlers compete in a single-elimination tournament of nine or ten rounds; the highest-ranked wrestler may choose his opponent each round (UNESCO, 2010). Lifetime titles are awarded by rounds won at Naadam: nachin (falcon, 5 rounds), khartsaga (hawk, 6), zaan (elephant, 7), garuda (8), arslan (lion, 9) and avarga (titan/champion); a 2003 law added the iarudi rank, and under the newest law a champion may be promoted directly to Dayan Avarga (UWW, n.d.). Fouls include grabbing below the belt without a proper grip, twisting limbs, head-butting and kicking; the referee may restart the bout or award the win. Men's and women's competitions use the same rules; women's bokh is contested at the World Nomad Games and in domestic Mongolian tournaments, and youth categories exist at national events (World Nomad Games, n.d.).

History

Wrestling has been part of Mongol military training and festivals since at least the 13th century — the Secret History of the Mongols (1240) records a match between Buri the Wrestler and Belgutei around 1200 — and Genghis Khan promoted wrestling to select warriors and rank candidates (UNESCO, 2010). The structured Naadam festival dates from 1639 and became an annual state festival in 1912; it was inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2010, together with its three games of men (UNESCO, 2010). The Mongolian Wrestling Federation was founded in 1961 and is a member of United World Wrestling; bokh is also a core sport of the World Nomad Games, held in Kyrgyzstan (World Nomad Games, n.d.). Icons include Tömöriin Artag, the 1968 Olympic silver medalist in freestyle wrestling who began in bokh, and modern champions such as Hakuho, who left bokh to become yokozuna in Japanese sumo (UWW, n.d.).
