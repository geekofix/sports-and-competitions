Crokinole

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Crokinole is a Canadian disk-flicking dexterity board game, similar to pitchnut, carrom and pichenotte with elements of shuffleboard and curling reduced to table-top size: players take turns shooting wooden discs across a circular board, aiming for the recessed 20-point centre hole while trying to knock opposing discs off the board into the ditch (Wikipedia, n.d.). A tournament board has a playing surface about 26 inches (660 mm) in diameter with three concentric rings worth 5, 10 and 15 points moving inward, a 20-point hole in the centre guarded by eight pegs, and a gutter around the edge (Wikipedia, n.d.). Each player or team generally has 12 discs of one color, and a round ends when all discs are shot; only the score difference is counted (Wikipedia, n.d.). The World Crokinole Championship has been held every June since 1999 in Tavistock, Ontario — on the first Saturday of June, with around 400 players in 2024 — and includes competitive, recreational, junior and a cue-stick category (CBC, 2024; World Crokinole Championship, n.d.). The 2024 champion was Connor Reinman, his second consecutive title, while Justin Slater holds five world titles (CBC, 2024).

The name comes from the French croquignole — originally a hard biscuit, but in Canadian French also the flick itself (pichenette) (Wikipedia, n.d.). The oldest known board was made in 1876 by Eckhardt Wettlaufer in Perth County, Ontario, and is kept at the Joseph Schneider Haus museum in Kitchener; the game appears in Ontario farmhouses as far back as 1867 and spread through southern Ontario farming and Mennonite communities, with factories selling boards by mail order across Canada and the northern United States by the early 20th century (Wikipedia, n.d.; CBC, 2024). Its popularity was revived by Wayne Kelly’s definitive The Crokinole Book, after which the Tavistock world championship was established (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men, women and juniors all compete, usually two players or teams of two (World Crokinole Championship, n.d.).

Rules

Official tournament rules are published for the World Crokinole Championship: players shoot from the outer edge of their own quadrant toward the centre; if any opposing disc is on the board, the shot must make contact with an opposing disc (directly or indirectly), otherwise the shot is a foul and the disc — plus any of the shooter’s discs it moved — is removed (World Crokinole Championship, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). A disc that drops completely into the 20-point hole is removed immediately and set aside; it scores 20 at the end of the round if the shot was valid (Wikipedia, n.d.). Discs landing in the ditch are removed and score nothing; a disc that touches the outer line but returns under its own momentum stays in play ("spinning disc rule") (World Crokinole Championship, n.d.). At the end of the round, only the difference between the two scores is counted, and the game continues over several rounds to an agreed total, commonly 100 points (Wikipedia, n.d.). Two players or two teams of two; no gender divisions (World Crokinole Championship, n.d.).

History

Crokinole’s tradition began in Canada in the 1860s — historian Wayne Kelly documents a game of the same name in Ontario farmhouses as far back as 1867 — and the oldest surviving board was built in 1876 by Eckhardt Wettlaufer of Perth County, Ontario, as a gift for his son’s fifth birthday (CBC, 2024; Wikipedia, n.d.). A related board variant was patented in New York in 1880 by M.B. Ross (Wikipedia, n.d.). The game spread through rural Ontario and Quebec households, especially in Mennonite communities — the oldest continuously running crokinole club, the Jacobs Crokinole Club at St. Jacobs, still plays weekly (CBC, 2024). Mail-order boards sold across Canada and the northern U.S. in the early 1900s; the modern era began with Wayne Kelly’s The Crokinole Book and the founding of the World Crokinole Championship in Tavistock in 1999, Wettlaufer’s hometown (Wikipedia, n.d.).
