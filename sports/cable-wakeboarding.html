Cable Wakeboarding

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Cable wakeboarding is wakeboarding behind an overhead cable system instead of a boat: the rider is towed by a cable loop around a cable park, using the tow to hit rails, kickers and other obstacles, and is judged on the tricks performed during a run. It is governed by the International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation (IWWF), which runs the IWWF World Cable Wakeboard & Wakeskate Championships, while the Cable Wakeboard World Council publishes the technical rule book (International Waterski & Wakeboard Federation [IWWF], n.d.-a) (Cable Wakeboard World Council, n.d.). Cable wakeboarding was included in the programme of The World Games 2025 in Chengdu (IWWF, n.d.-b). The 2026 IWWF World Cable Wakeboard & Wakeskate Championships were held August 8–15, 2026, at the Shunyi Olympic Cable Park in Beijing, China — a venue built for the 2008 Olympics — with 173 athletes from 30 countries competing in wakeboard, wakeskate and seated (para) disciplines; Almaz Smailkulov of Kyrgyzstan won the men's wakeboard title (76.33 points) ahead of Italians Fabrizio Cicerale and Giorgio Pieri, while Monique Douma won the women's 30+ division (IWWF, n.d.-a). The 2026 IWWF World Cableski Championships followed in Weert, Netherlands, September 10–13, 2026 (IWWF, n.d.-b).

On the women's side, women compete in all age divisions at the world championships — including Monique Douma, the 2026 women's 30+ world champion in Beijing — and the seated wakeboard (para) discipline is a full medal event, making cable wakeboarding one of the more inclusive towed water sports (IWWF, n.d.-a).

Rules

Under the Cable Wakeboard World Council rule book (edition V12, 2026), a cable wakeboard run lasts a set time (typically two passes around the cable circuit) and is judged on the tricks performed on obstacles — rails, boxes, kickers, ramps and sliders — and on the water between them. Judges score each trick on difficulty, execution, variety and composition of the whole run; falls are penalized, and the highest-scoring runs advance in knockout heats (Cable Wakeboard World Council, n.d.). The cable park consists of a loop of cable carried by towers, with an electric winch that tows the rider at about 30–36 km/h; riders wear a wakeboard (or wakeskate — a board without bindings), a helmet and an impact vest, and start from a dock or platform (IWWF, n.d.-a). Fouls include cutting the course, interfering with another rider and unsportsmanlike conduct; riders may be disqualified for dangerous riding (Cable Wakeboard World Council, n.d.). Divisions: men's and women's, junior, open and 30+/40+ masters classes, plus the seated (para) division for riders with disabilities, which uses adapted equipment and is judged on the same criteria (IWWF, n.d.-a). Wakeskate — a twin-tip board without bindings — has its own world title at the same championships (IWWF, n.d.-b).

History

Cable wakeboarding began in the late 1990s when wakeboarders started being towed by ski lifts and cable systems at water parks instead of boats; the first cable parks opened in Germany and the United States around 2000, and the Cable Wakeboard World Council grew out of the IWWF's cable wakeboard commission. The IWWF has organized world cable wakeboard championships since 2010, and the discipline was added to The World Games programme in 2025 in Chengdu (IWWF, n.d.-b). Today there are several hundred cable parks worldwide, and the sport bridges wakeboarding, wakeskating and snowboard-style rail riding (IWWF, n.d.-a).
