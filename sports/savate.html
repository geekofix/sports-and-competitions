Savate

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Savate — French boxing, boxe française — is a French combat sport that combines the four punches of English boxing with a codified arsenal of kicks delivered in special shoes (chaussons); it is governed worldwide by FISav, the Fédération Internationale de Savate, which counts more than 60 member countries and organises World Championships in three disciplines: assaut (controlled contact), combat (full contact) and canne de combat (stick fighting) (FISav, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The 2025 World Combat Championships ran their qualifying heats and junior finals in Plovdiv, Bulgaria, 2–6 July 2025 with more than 250 athletes from 22 countries, and concluded with senior finals in several host cities ending on 29 November 2025 in Yaoundé, Cameroon, where France's Emma Mortier took the women's 48 kg world title; the World Assaut Championships were held in Boulogne-sur-Mer, France, 9–13 September 2026, where Chloé Nandi added another world title to her collection of six and Sara Djondo won the women's under-75 kg final (FF SAVATE, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Among the current leading tireuses, Chloé Nandi (six world titles across disciplines), Sara Djondo (2026 world assaut champion, under-75 kg) and Emma Mortier (2025 world combat champion, 48 kg) head the women's elite, while the men's side is led by French world champions such as Jonathan Garel, Iden Garcia, Stéphane Fofou, Christopher Brugiroux and Douglas Crouard Goubin from the 2025 combat finals (FF SAVATE, n.d.).

Rules

Savate is fought with boxing gloves, the savate shoe and the tenue intégrale (full suit); only the four boxing punches — direct bras avant, direct bras arrière, crochet and uppercut — and kicks with the foot are allowed: fouetté (roundhouse), chassé frontal, chassé latéral, revers and coup de pied bas, with no knee or elbow strikes, no kick catching with the arms and no clinch work; target areas exclude the back of the head and neck, the throat and the groin, and in women's bouts the chest may not be kicked or struck (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Competition has three levels: assaut, where contact is controlled and referees penalise excessive force — bouts of three rounds of one minute thirty under current French federation rules — précombat as an intermediate full-contact stage with helmet and shin guards, and combat, the full-contact form with minimal protection (gloves, mouthguard, groin protector) in which knockouts are possible (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; FF SAVATE, n.d.). World championships are staged in eight weight categories per gender, from 48 kg to over 75 kg for women and 56 kg to over 85 kg for men; men and women compete under identical rules in parallel categories, and canne de combat bouts are fought with a wooden cane in the same ring format (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; FISav, n.d.).

History

Savate grew out of two 19th-century street traditions: the chausson (jeu marseillais) of Marseille sailors and the savate of Paris; Michel Casseux (1794–1869) opened the first regulated salle in 1825 and barred headbutts, and Charles Lecour (1808–1894) merged the kicking game with English boxing around 1832 after losing a bare-knuckle bout to an English boxer (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Joseph Charlemont published the Traité de la boxe française in 1877 and founded the Société des Boxeurs Français in 1899 together with his son Charles, codifying the academic style, and savate was a demonstration sport at the 1924 Paris Olympics (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). After a decline during the two world wars, the rival French federations merged in March 1985 into the Fédération Internationale de Boxe Française Savate, renamed FISav in October 1999; the first women's European and world championships followed in 1995–96 together with the creation of HandiSavate, the first world canne de combat championships were held in 2004 on Réunion — codifying Maurice Sarry's work of the late 1970s — and FISU recognised savate on 1 March 2008, with the first university world championships in Nantes in June 2010; Julie Gabriel of England has been FISav president since 2010 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The icons of the codification era are Casseux, Lecour and Joseph Charlemont (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
