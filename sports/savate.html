Savate

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Savate, also known as boxe française or French boxing, is a French hybrid martial art and full-contact combat sport that combines the punches of Western boxing with a wide repertoire of kicks executed exclusively with the shod foot. Its name comes from the French for "old shoe" — the heavy boots of the Parisian street fighters and sailors among whom the art arose — and a practitioner is called a tireur (or tireuse). The sport is governed internationally by the World Savate Federation (WSAF) and national federations worldwide, with world championships in assaut and combat.

Rules

Savate is fought in two principal formats: assaut, a light-contact or touch contest in which technical quality, control and elegance are judged and force is excluded or penalised, and combat, a full-contact format in which knockouts decide bouts as in other full-contact sports. Its defining technical rule is that only the shoe may kick: all kicks must strike with the foot, never the shin or knee, which are banned along with elbows; punches follow the English boxing repertoire of jab, cross, hook and uppercut, and any part of the body above the belt is a legal target. Kicks are classified by their mechanical families — the fouetté (whip kick, striking with the toe), chassé (a pushing or driving kick with the heel or sole), revers (a hooking kick with the shoe's sole) and coup de pied bas (a low sweeping kick to the shin) — all delivered from a long, upright, fencing-like stance that reflects the sport's duelling heritage. Fighters wear purpose-made savate shoes and boxing gloves sized by weight category, bouts are contested in timed rounds set by competition class, and scoring rewards clean touches to valid areas, including the face, torso and legs, evaluated by judges along with each technique's execution.

History

Savate's ancestry lies in the rough street fighting of 19th-century France — the low-kicking savate of Paris and the high-kicking, slipper-footed chausson of the Marseille sailors who may have carried kicking styles home from voyages to the Indian Ocean and the Americas — and by one theory even earlier, in the punishments of Napoleon-era soldiers. The first structured teaching came from Michel Casseux, who opened a Parisian salle in 1825 to teach the street art, banned gouging and grappling, and attracted aristocrats and even royalty to his system; his follower Charles Lecour, after being beaten in a cross-style contest, added English boxing's punches around 1838 to create the modern marriage of hands and feet. The art was refined in print by Joseph Charlemont — whose Traité de la boxe française (1899) became the sport's technical syllabus — and his son Charles, a noted champion; the Société des Boxeurs Français was founded to govern the growing sport, and after the First World War the Fédération Française de Boxe standardised competition. Savate saw a post-World War II decline and revival from the 1970s, and today the sport is contested in the two disciplines of assaut and combat by federations spanning over fifty nations, with world championships and a strong youth and university structure; related arts include canne de combat (stick fighting) and savate défense, while savate's shod kicks have influenced modern kickboxing and MMA striking curricula.
