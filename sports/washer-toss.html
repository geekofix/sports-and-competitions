Washer Toss

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Washer toss — also called washer pitching or simply washers — is the American lawn game in which players toss metal washers at a target, usually a cup, can or box sunk into the ground or a vertical stake, scoring for washers landing in or around it; it belongs to the same throwing-game family as horseshoes and cornhole and needs only inexpensive equipment (American Washers Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Played casually at barbecues, campsites and tailgates as well as in organized leagues and tournaments, the game is open to women and men alike in singles, doubles and mixed pairs (Wikipedia, n.d.). The American Washers Association preserves the game's history and standardizes tournament play (American Washers Association, n.d.).

Rules

Two teams — singles or doubles — stand beside one target and throw toward the other, set typically 20–25 feet (about 6–8 m) away, alternating throws of a set number of washers per round (Wikipedia, n.d.). Scoring follows the local rule sheet: a washer landing inside the cup or can scores the highest value (commonly five points for a "ringer" around the stake or in the cup), with secondary points for the nearest washer and leaners against the target in some variants; when both teams score in the same round the points cancel, and the first team to an agreed total — commonly 11 or 21 points — wins the game (Wikipedia, n.d.; American Washers Association, n.d.). Tournament rule sheets fix the distance, washer size and weight, target type and the exact scoring table, since regional variants (such as Texas washers) differ (American Washers Association, n.d.).

History

Washer pitching grew out of informal rural American recreation; oral tradition traces it to players who lacked horseshoes or horseshoe stakes and instead threw hardware washers at cups or cans sunk into the ground (Wikipedia, n.d.). Through the twentieth century it spread across the American South and Midwest, developing regional variants, and in recent decades it has gained standardized kits, league play and championship tournaments run by organized bodies such as the American Washers Association, which documents the game's origins and promotes competitive play (American Washers Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).
