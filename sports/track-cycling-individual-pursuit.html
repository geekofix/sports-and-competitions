Track Cycling Individual Pursuit

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

The individual pursuit is the classic track cycling duel in which two riders start on opposite sides of the velodrome and race 4,000 m, each trying to catch the other; the race ends when one rider catches the other or when both have finished, and the times are compared (UCI, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It has been an Olympic event for men since Tokyo 1964 and for women since Barcelona 1992 — at the same 4 km distance since Tokyo 2020 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b).

At the 2026 UCI Track Cycling World Championships in Shanghai, 14–18 October 2026, Britain's Anna Morris, pictured in an individual pursuit final, won the team pursuit, the omnium and the madison — the first woman to win all three Olympic endurance events at one World Championships since the women's madison was introduced in 2017 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

This is part of the track sprint and time-trial disciplines — Individual Sprint, 500m Time Trial, 1km Time Trial — within the cycling family (parent Cycling); the full list is under Important Links.

Rules

Under UCI Regulations Part 3, the individual pursuit is 4 km for elite men and women (3 km for juniors), started from opposite gates; if a rider catches the opponent the race is over and the times decide, and electronic timing reads to 1/1000 s (UCI, n.d.-b). The team pursuit pits teams of four against the clock over 4 km with exchanges every half lap, the riders dropping off after each pull (UCI, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Equipment follows the track rules — fixed-gear bike, no brakes, minimum 6.8 kg, aerodynamic bars and disc wheels permitted in the pursuit, mandatory helmet; men and women compete separately at the same distance, and the pursuit is also part of the omnium (UCI, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

History

The pursuit has been at the heart of track cycling since the 19th century; Britain's Sir Bradley Wiggins, pictured with his Olympic time-trial bike, won Olympic gold in the individual pursuit at Beijing 2008 and in the team pursuit at Beijing 2008, London 2012 and Rio 2016, and set the hour record in 2015 (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

On the women's side, Britain's Laura Kenny (née Trott) won five Olympic golds across London 2012 and Rio 2016 in the individual and team pursuit, and the endurance era was also shaped by Australia's Sarah Ulmer, Olympic champion in 2004 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b).
