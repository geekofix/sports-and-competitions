Kenjutsu

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Kenjutsu ("sword techniques", 剣術) is the umbrella term for the classical schools (koryu) of Japanese swordsmanship founded before the Meiji Restoration in 1868. Unlike the modern sport of kendo, which uses shinai and armor, kenjutsu schools preserve battlefield sword techniques, practiced mainly through kata with wooden swords (bokken), blunt steel (iaito) and, rarely, sharp swords (shinken) for test cutting (tameshigiri). The classical schools are coordinated in Japan by the Nihon Kobudo Kyokai and preserved worldwide by their individual ryuha organizations.

Rules

There is no single rulebook: each koryu has its own curriculum of kata and, in some schools, free sparring (gekken) with protective equipment. Training centers on kata — solo and paired forms that encode cuts, blocks, throws and disarms — plus suburi (solo cutting practice) with the bokken to develop correct technique and distance (ma-ai). Advanced students may practice with fukuroshinai (leather-covered bamboo) or, in a few schools, shinken for tameshigiri on targets such as rolled tatami. Etiquette, rei (bowing) and the transmission of the school's scrolls (menkyo kaiden) are essential parts of practice; rank is granted by the school's headmaster (soke), not by a central federation.

History

Kenjutsu originated with the samurai class in the Kamakura period (1185-1333) and flourished during the Sengoku wars (1467-1615), when hundreds of schools (ryuha) were founded. With the peace of the Edo period (1603-1868), combat techniques were formalized into kata and safe training weapons — bokken and later shinai — were developed. In 1912 the Dai Nippon Butoku Kai issued an edict standardizing ten core kata across schools, which evolved into modern kendo, while the older schools continued as koryu. Among the most famous are Katori Shinto-ryu (founded 1447 by Iizasa Choisai Ienao, one of Japan's oldest schools), Itto-ryu, Kashima Shinto-ryu and Miyamoto Musashi's Niten Ichi-ryu. After the Meiji Restoration many schools declined, but since the 20th century the Nihon Kobudo Kyokai and international organizations have kept dozens of koryu alive worldwide.
