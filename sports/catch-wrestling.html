Catch Wrestling

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Catch wrestling — "catch-as-catch-can" — is the submission-grappling style of wrestling in which almost anything goes: competitors wear no jacket, may grip anywhere on the body, and may attack the legs with entanglements ("hooks") as well as apply joint locks and chokes ("stretches"); a bout is won by pinning the opponent's shoulders to the mat or by submission (United World Wrestling [UWW], n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Catch wrestling was an Olympic event at St. Louis 1904 — with only seven American entrants — and at Antwerp 1920, where submissions were banned, and it was then replaced at the Games by freestyle wrestling, a style derived directly from catch; today the Olympic styles are governed by UWW, while competitive catch survives in a revival scene that organizes its own world championships, above all the Snake Pit Catch Wrestling World Championships in Birkenhead, England, and the events of the American Catch Wrestling Alliance (ACWA) in the United States (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Because catch has no single world federation of its own, UWW — the world governing body of wrestling, founded as the International Amateur Wrestling Federation in 1921 — is the relevant international federation for the Olympic context, and the Snake Pit and ACWA rule the revival scene (UWW, n.d.). The women's side of the revival is small but growing: women's catch bouts are now staged at Snake Pit events alongside the men's, while the historic professional era was almost exclusively male (Snake Pit Catch Wrestling, n.d.). At the end of this article's scope, the leading names of the revival scene are the Snake Pit world champions crowned annually in Birkenhead.

Rules

Under catch-as-catch-can rules there is no prescribed uniform and no required grip: the wrestlers may seize arms, legs, neck or trunk in any combination, and leg attacks — the classic "heel hook" and other entanglements — are fully legal, as are joint locks on wrists, elbows, shoulders, knees and ankles and neck chokes, except where a specific event's rulebook (for example the Olympic rules of 1904–1920) banned submissions (UWW, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Victory comes by pinfall — holding both of the opponent's shoulder blades on the mat for the referee's count — or by submission, when the opponent signals defeat by tapping; a bout may also end by referee stoppage for injury or by points in timed formats. Modern revival events (Snake Pit, ACWA) contest weight classes and age groups — men's and women's divisions, juniors and seniors — usually as single-fall or best-of-three-falls matches, sometimes inside a time limit, and are refereed under catch rulebooks that explicitly permit hooks and stretches; the Olympic freestyle and Greco-Roman styles governed by UWW, by contrast, forbid most leg attacks and all submissions in the standing phase of Greco-Roman (UWW, n.d.). The world-level rule source for wrestling in general is UWW (uww.org); for competitive catch specifically the Snake Pit and ACWA rulebooks apply.

History

Catch wrestling crystallized in 19th-century Britain from Lancashire catch-as-catch-can and Cumberland-and-Westmorland wrestling, and it became the dominant professional wrestling style of the early 20th century. Its defining icons are Georg Hackenschmidt (1877–1968), the Estonian "Russian Lion" born in Tartu who invented the bear hug, won the world catch championship in 1908, lost it to Frank Gotch in 1908 and regained it on 4 September 1911 in Chicago; Frank Gotch (1877–1917), the Iowa farmer nicknamed "the Toe-Holder"; Lou Thesz (1901–2002), who held the world title for 25 years; and Karl Gotch (1924–2007), the Olympic-style wrestler who later trained Japanese stars such as Antonio Inoki (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Hackenschmidt is pictured below in a Bain News Service photograph held by the Library of Congress. Women's catch wrestling has a shorter documented history — Edith Garrud, the British suffragette jiu-jitsu instructor, famously defended herself against police using grappling in the 1910s — and women's catch competition is now part of the modern revival scene (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
