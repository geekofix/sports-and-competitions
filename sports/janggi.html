Janggi

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Janggi is Korean chess, a board game played on a 9 × 10 grid of intersections with a 3 × 3 palace marked with diagonals at each end, derived from the Chinese game xiangqi but without the river and with its own piece movements (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Each side commands 16 pieces: one general, two guards, two elephants, two horses, two chariots, two cannons and five soldiers (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Blue moves first, and to balance the first-move advantage the starting score is handicapped — Blue starts at 72 points and Red at 73.5 (the "deom") (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The game is governed in Korea by the Korea Janggi Association, and the first world janggi tournament was held in Harbin, China, in 2009 (Korea Janggi Association, n.d.). In 2026 the leading players are the holders of the national dan titles in Seoul, with women's janggi — played under identical rules in its own championships — led by the reigning women's national champion (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

The board has 9 vertical and 10 horizontal lines; the palace is the 3 × 3 area at each end with both diagonals drawn (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The general moves one step orthogonally or diagonally inside the palace and may not face the enemy general across an empty file (the "flying general" rule) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Guards move like the general but may also move one step diagonally anywhere in the palace; chariots move any number of steps along lines and may also use the palace diagonals; horses move one step orthogonally then one step diagonally outward (like a knight, but blocked by a piece adjacent to the orthogonal step); elephants move one step orthogonally and then two steps diagonally (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The cannon is unique: it moves like a chariot but cannot jump over any piece, and to capture it must jump over exactly one piece — its own or the opponent's — the "screen" (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Soldiers move one step forward or sideways and one step diagonally forward inside the enemy palace, and may capture backward; they can never move backward outside the palace (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Before play, each player secretly chooses the starting positions of their horse and elephant (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Passing a turn is allowed, and "bikjang" — both generals entering the opponent's palace simultaneously — is legal rather than an immediate loss (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Janggi has a no-draw rule: if a position repeats three times the referee intervenes, and if the game cannot be decided, the winner is determined by material points — chariot 13, cannon 7, horse 5, elephant 3, guard 3, soldier 2 — with Red receiving the 1.5-point handicap (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The objective is checkmate of the enemy general; men's and women's competitions use identical rules (Korea Janggi Association, n.d.).

History

Janggi developed in Korea from the Chinese xiangqi during the Joseon era, with the first Korean rule codifications appearing in the 18th and 19th centuries; the modern 9 × 10 board, palace and piece set were standardized in the 20th century (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The Korea Janggi Association was founded to organize national dan-ranking tournaments and the professional league, and the first world janggi tournament was held in Harbin, China, in 2009, with players from China, Korea, Japan, North America and Europe (Korea Janggi Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Women's janggi grew strongly from the 2000s with dedicated women's championships (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Because janggi is a mind sport without a long professional tour, its best-known figures are the legendary masters of the Joseon-era manuals and the modern national champions whose games are published in the association's records (Korea Janggi Association, n.d.).
