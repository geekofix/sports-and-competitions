Skeleton

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Skeleton is the sliding sport in which an athlete sprints to push a small steel sled, dives on head-first in a prone position and steers with subtle shoulder and knee movements down an ice track at speeds of up to 140 km/h, only centimetres above the ice; it is governed by the International Bobsleigh & Skeleton Federation (IBSF) and has been an Olympic discipline since St. Moritz 1928, returning to the programme at Salt Lake City 2002 with a women's event added (Olympics, n.d.; IBSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). At Milano Cortina 2026, held at the Cortina Sliding Centre from 12 to 15 February, Britain's Matt Weston won the men's gold with a track record in all four runs — the first British male Olympic skeleton champion — ahead of Germany's Axel Jungk and the defending champion Christopher Grotheer, while Austria's Janine Flock, at 36 the oldest Olympic champion in women's skeleton history, won the women's gold ahead of the Germans Susanne Kreher and Jacqueline Pfeifer; 2026 also saw the debut of a mixed team event (Wikipedia, n.d.-c; IBSF, n.d.).

Among today's leading athletes, the women's field is headed by Austria's Janine Flock — Olympic champion at Milano Cortina 2026 at age 36 — and the Netherlands' Kimberley Bos, the 2025 world champion who took bronze at Beijing 2022, the first Olympic medal in a non-skating ice sport for the Netherlands, while the men's side is led by Britain's Matt Weston, Olympic champion 2026 and 2025 world champion, with Germany's Axel Jungk and Christopher Grotheer completing the podium in 2026 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-c). Men and women race on the same track in separate events under the same rules, with only the sled and combined weight limits differing by gender; since Milano Cortina 2026 nations also field a mixed team of one woman and one man (IBSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Rules

Skeleton is governed by the IBSF (https://www.ibsf.org/). Athletes race alone on a shared bobsleigh-and-luge track, typically 1,200–1,650 m long with 15–20 curves; at the Olympics each competitor takes four runs over two days and the lowest combined time wins (IBSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The start is a standing sprint of about 30–40 m pushing the sled, followed by a dive onto the sled in a prone, head-first position; the athlete steers by pressing shoulders and knees against the sled, which has no steering or braking mechanism (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The sled is a steel frame 80–120 cm long and 8–20 cm high with runners 34–38 cm apart, weighing at most 43 kg for men and 35 kg for women, and the combined weight of athlete and sled may not exceed 115 kg for men and 92 kg for women; ballast may be added to the sled only, never to the rider (IBSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Equipment comprises a helmet with chin guard, a skin-tight uncoated racing suit, goggles and spiked shoes, and the runner temperature is checked against a reference runner to prevent illegal heating (IBSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men and women compete in separate events with an identical race format — the only differences are the lower weight limits for women — and since Milano Cortina 2026 nations also field a mixed team event with one woman and one man per nation (IBSF, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

History

Skeleton's origins lie in St. Moritz, Switzerland: British soldiers built a toboggan track between Davos and Klosters around 1882, and in 1884 the Outdoor Amusement Committee of the Kulm Hotel built the Cresta Run, a 1.2 km natural ice track still used today; the St. Moritz Tobogganing Club followed in 1887, and head-first riding became the standard after the 1887 Grand National (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In 1892 the Englishman L. P. Child introduced the "America", a bare metal sled whose bone-like frame gave the sport its name (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The Fédération Internationale de Bobsleigh et de Tobogganing (FIBT, founded 1923) made skeleton an Olympic sport for the first St. Moritz Games in 1928 — 13 men from five nations started on the Cresta Run — and again in 1948, where Italy's Nino Bibbia won gold, Italy's first-ever Winter Olympic medal, while America's John Heaton took silver in both 1928 and 1948, still the only athlete with two Olympic skeleton medals (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). After 1948 skeleton was dropped from the Olympic programme in favor of luge at the 1954 IOC session, but the first artificially refrigerated track at Königssee in 1969 and a new bobsleigh-compatible sled in 1970 revived it internationally: the first European Championship was held in 1982 at Königssee and the first World Championships the same year at St. Moritz, with the women's world title added in 2000 at Igls (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; IBSF, n.d.). Skeleton returned to the Olympics at Salt Lake City 2002 for men and women, and at Beijing 2022 Germany swept the golds — Christopher Grotheer in the men's and Hannah Neise in the women's at Yanqing — while Kimberley Bos gave the Netherlands its first sliding-sport medal and Yan Wengang China's first (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Notably, the Cresta Run forbade women riders from 1929 until December 2018, when the ban was lifted and Britain's Carina Evans became the first woman to descend from the top in 2019 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
