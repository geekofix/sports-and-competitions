Duck Herding

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Duck herding is a dog sport in which a handler directs a trained herding dog — usually a Border Collie — to gather and move a small group of ducks, typically Indian Runner ducks, around a fenced course of pens, gates and obstacles, either in judged herding trials or in timed competitions (American Kennel Club [AKC], n.d.; American Herding Breed Association [AHBA], n.d.). There is no world federation; the sport is organized by national kennel and herding bodies — the AKC and the AHBA in the United States, the Border Collie Society of America (BCSA), and country-pursuits providers in Britain — which run trials and competitions year-round, with the BCSA National Specialty Herding Trial at Purina Farms in Gray Summit, Missouri, among the premier US events on ducks and sheep (AKC, n.d.; AHBA, n.d.). Handlers of all genders compete on equal terms; the dog-handler team is what is scored, and the ducks are treated as working livestock under strict animal-welfare guidelines (AKC, n.d.). At the end of 2026, the sport's current scene is centered on national specialty trials and league events in the United States and Britain rather than a single world championship (AHBA, n.d.).

Women and men compete alike as handlers in duck herding.

Rules

In the United States, duck herding at trials is governed by the AKC Herding Regulations and by the rules of the AHBA and BCSA (AKC, n.d.; AHBA, n.d.). Trials are held in a fenced arena — fencing must be at least 36 inches high when ducks are worked — and the stock is typically five ducks per run, with three runs per day (AKC, n.d.). Dogs compete in three test levels — started, intermediate and advanced — on different course types (A, B, C and D), where Course A is the fenced arena course used for ducks, sheep and cattle, testing the dog's ability to gather, drive, cross-drive and pen the stock on the handler's commands, given by voice or whistle (AKC, n.d.). Herding tests are judged pass/fail, while herding trials are scored, and titles such as HIAd (Herding Intermediate, Course A, Ducks) are earned by accumulating qualifying scores (AKC, n.d.). The course is laid out with pens, gates, panels and obstacles such as a foot bath or wagon; the dog must move the ducks calmly through the course and into the pen without splitting the group, and faults are called for a dog that grips, worries or loses control of the stock (AHBA, n.d.). In British country-pursuits duck herding, teams are timed as they take turns directing the dog with a shepherd's crook to guide the ducks around an obstacle course and back into the pen, with medals for the winning teams (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Eligibility rules (registration, minimum age of the dog, health) apply in all venues, and the welfare of the ducks is regulated by animal-welfare law and RSPCA-type guidelines (AKC, n.d.). Men's and women's handlers compete in the same classes with identical rules (AHBA, n.d.).

History

Moving ducks with dogs has been everyday farm work for centuries wherever poultry was kept, and competitive duck herding grew out of the sheepdog trials and agricultural shows of twentieth-century Britain and North America, where farmers first demonstrated their dogs' skill on ducks and geese (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Indian Runner ducks became the standard stock because their upright stance and strong flocking instinct make them calm, intelligent and easy to guide as a single group (AHBA, n.d.). In Britain, duck herding became a popular country-pursuits team activity in which a professional shepherd supplies the dogs and the ducks and the participants take turns handling the dog with a crook (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In the United States, the AKC's herding program — tests and trials with ducks, sheep and cattle — formalized the sport from the 1990s onward, and breed organizations such as the AHBA and the BCSA added national duck trials, with the BCSA National Specialty Herding Trial at Purina Farms becoming a showcase event (AKC, n.d.). Handlers and judges of both sexes have shaped the sport since its early trials, and women are prominent among today's top handlers and judges (AHBA, n.d.).
