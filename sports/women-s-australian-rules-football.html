Women's Australian Rules Football

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Women's Australian rules football is the female branch of Australian football, played under the same rules as the men's game — 18 players a side on an oval ground, playing the ball by kick or handball and scoring goals and behinds between tall posts — governed nationally by the AFL with its women's competition as the sport's elite level. The AFL Women's (AFLW) league, launched in 2017, sits at the top of a pathway that runs through state leagues to community clubs in every Australian state.

The women's game has grown explosively since the 2010s, drawing record crowds and television audiences and making stars of players who became the face of the league.

Rules

The playing rules follow the Australian game mark, kick and handball framework adapted only slightly for the women's competition: quarters of shorter elite match length, a smaller elite grade ball, and the same tackle framework governed by AFL laws — tackles between knee and shoulder, holding the ball provisions, marks and the run of play — with umpires enforcing bounce-downs and out-of-bounds throw-ins. Scoring gives six points for a kicked goal between the middle posts and one point for a behind; league-specific provisions touch on roster size, list rules and salary restrictions rather than the playing laws, which the AFL keeps continuous across the men's and women's games.

History

Women have played Australian football since the earliest years of the code — matches were recorded in Western Australia as early as 1915 — and exhibition games continued through the twentieth century while formal leagues formed at state level, like the Victorian Women's Football League in 1981. The national push arrived with exhibition games from 2013 and the AFL's national league plan, and AFLW launched in 2017 with eight teams playing a seven-round season that broke attendance records and received national television coverage. The league has since expanded nationally, seasons lengthened, and the player cohort has become fully professional, cementing the women's game within the Australian football landscape.
