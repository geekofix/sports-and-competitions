Skijoring

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Skijoring (from the Norwegian skikj\u00f8ring, "ski driving") is the winter sport in which a person on skis is pulled along the snow by a horse, one or more dogs, or a motor vehicle. In its equestrian form — the most spectacular — a team of a horse, rider and skier races over a snow course with jumps, gates and rings at speeds over 40 mph; in its dog form, a cross-country skier is harnessed to a sled dog or two and glides along a prepared trail, part of the family of dryland-and-snow dog sports. Its competitive heartlands are Scandinavia, the American West and the Alps.

Rules

Equestrian skijoring courses are typically a straight track of roughly 900 feet (about 275 m) over which the horse accelerates hard from a running start, towing the skier on a 30-foot rope — often combined with gates, small jumps, and ring spearing — with the fastest adjusted time (time penalties for missed gates or rings) winning; teams consist of a horse, rider and skier (or snowboarder). In dog skijoring, the dog (any breed over about 15 kg for most club rules) wears a sledding harness attached by a bungee line to a belt worn by the skier, who must supply some power with poles, so it is a true two-athlete team; races are usually sprint events on groomed trails of a few kilometres, with classes for skier plus one or two dogs, and commands ("hike", "gee", "haw", "on by") controlling the dog. Pulk-style competitions sometimes have the skier pull a load instead; behind snowmobiles and ATVs — including adapted downtown events — the motorised version uses the tow format at higher speeds.

History

The practice is ancient — Lapland reindeer herders and Scandinavian travellers have harnessed animals to pull skiers for centuries, and skijoring appeared as a military dispatch competition at the Nordic Games of 1901, 1905 and 1909, where it caught the eye of modern Olympics founder Pierre de Coubertin. Equine skijoring reached North America in the early 1900s (documented recreationally at Lake Placid in 1915 and the Dartmouth Winter Carnival), and the sport's great American institution began in 1949, when Leadville, Colorado staged its first equestrian race — still running today with its gates, jumps and rings; the 1928 St. Moritz Olympics included skijoring behind riderless horses on the frozen lake as a demonstration sport, won by three Swiss athletes, and Denver listed it as an exhibition sport in its 1976 Olympic bid. Modern organised competition ranges from the North American racing tradition and the White Turf meeting at St. Moritz (where skiers drive the horses from behind around an oval) to national dog-skijoring federations under, for example, the International Federation of Sled Dog Sports umbrella for dryland sports. New professional series such as Pro Skijor (growing out of Skijoring Utah, founded 2017) are expanding the sport's professional circuit across the American West.
