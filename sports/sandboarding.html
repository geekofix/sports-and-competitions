Sandboarding

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Sandboarding is a boardsport similar to snowboarding in which riders slide down sand dunes standing on a board with both feet strapped in; sand sledding variants are ridden sitting or lying down. Practised wherever big dunes exist — above all in desert regions of the western United States, Peru and Chile, Brazil, North and South Africa and the Arabian peninsula — it is a recreational extreme sport with an international competition structure, including the Sandboard World Cup organised in Huacachina, Peru by InterSands, the International Sandboarding and Sandski Association.

Rules

There are few universal playing rules; competition formats are set by organisers such as InterSands and typically borrow from snowboarding: riders race down a dune course against the clock, or perform judged freestyle runs of tricks. The defining equipment rules are material ones: because sand is abrasive and dry, boards are built with a much harder base than snowboards — typically Formica or Laminex composite — and the underside is waxed, usually with paraffin-based sandboard wax, before each run, since untreated boards quickly grind to a halt and the wax both protects the base and enables gliding on both wet and dry sand. Riders wear strap-in bindings and protective gear, and at facilities such as Monte Kaolino in Germany the venue adds further structure: its competition hill is roughly 120 metres high with a 220–260 m run at about 35 degrees, and riders descend into a water landing site — an arrangement that makes it the world's only sand sport facility with a lift.

History

Sliding on sand has pre-modern roots: from ancient Egypt to ritual sliding down Chinese dunes around 800 AD, people have ridden planks of wood, clay or leather down dunes for transport and fun. The modern sport emerged in the mid-20th century, credited in part to Brazilian surfers who rode their surfboards on dunes when the sea was flat, and the 1960s–1970s saw the first purpose-built sandboards and dedicated riders in the United States, developing in parallel with snowboarding's rise. Institutionalisation came in Germany, of all places: the 35-million-tonne quartz-sand heap of Monte Kaolino at Hirschau in Bavaria — piled up from 1935 by kaolin mining — was discovered for sand skiing in the 1950s, hosted the annual Sandboarding World Championship from 1990 until 2007, and still stages the Sand Spirit festival every July, while in 2009 it added a funicular, making it the world's only sandsports hill with a lift. The Sport's world governance now rests with InterSands, founded in Switzerland in the early 2000s to standardise competition, whose Sandboard World Cup is staged yearly at Huacachina, Peru's desert oasis, and national federations from Peru to Brazil, the United States and South Africa run circuits from there.
