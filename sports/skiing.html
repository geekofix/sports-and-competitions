Skiing

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Skiing is the umbrella term for travelling over snow on skis, and as a competitive sport it is governed worldwide by the International Ski Federation (FIS), which covers the alpine disciplines (downhill, super-G, giant slalom, slalom and combined), the nordic disciplines (cross-country, ski jumping and nordic combined), freestyle skiing, snowboarding, ski cross and ski flying (FIS, n.d.-b). The FIS Alpine World Cup, launched for the 1966–67 season by Serge Lang, Honoré Bonnet and Bob Beattie, reached its 60th edition in 2025–26, opening in Sölden in late October 2025, with the Olympic break in February 2026 — the Milano Cortina Games staged alpine skiing at Bormio for the men and Cortina d'Ampezzo for the women from 7–18 February — and the 1,000th World Cup downhill raced on 18 December 2025 in Val Gardena, won by Marco Odermatt (FIS, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Among the current leading women are Mikaela Shiffrin of the United States, who reached 110 World Cup victories on 25 March 2026, won her sixth overall title and ninth slalom globe in 2025–26, and took Olympic slalom gold at Milano Cortina 2026, and Switzerland's Camille Rast, the 2026 overall runner-up at age 22; among the men, Marco Odermatt won his fifth consecutive overall title in 2025–26 plus the downhill, super-G and giant slalom globes (FIS, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

All FIS disciplines are run under the FIS International Competition Rules (ICR), which specify equipment (ski length, width, binding release settings), course setting and safety, timing, and the competition formats (FIS, n.d.-b). In the speed disciplines — downhill and super-G — athletes race a single run on a long, fast course with widely spaced gates; in the technical disciplines — slalom and giant slalom — two runs on a shorter, tighter course, the second with the course reset, and the combined awards one run of each (FIS, n.d.-b). Cross-country races are mass-start, interval-start or sprint formats in classic or free technique over set distances, with ski-jumping-style scoring not used — placings are by finish order — while ski jumping and nordic combined follow their own FIS regulations (FIS, n.d.-a). Freestyle skiing comprises moguls, aerials, ski cross, halfpipe, slopestyle and big air, judged or raced as the discipline prescribes, and snowboarding mirrors the alpine formats with parallel and cross disciplines (FIS, n.d.-a). Men and women compete in separate events in every discipline on the same courses and under the same technical regulations, with junior (under-18) and under-21 age classes at championships; the Olympic Winter Games, the biennial FIS World Championships and the annual World Cup form the international calendar (FIS, n.d.-a).

History

Skiing is one of the oldest means of transport, with rock carvings in Rødsand, Norway, depicting skiers from around 5000 BC, and Sondre Norheim of Telemark pioneered the modern Telemark technique and the first ski jumping in the 1860s (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first ski club was founded in 1849 in Trysil, Norway, and the Holmenkollen festival began in 1892; skiing debuted at the first Winter Olympic Games in Chamonix 1924, the same year the FIS was founded on 18 February 1924 by 14 nations (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The FIS Alpine World Championships began in 1931 in Mürren, organised by Sir Arnold Lunn, who had invented the slalom in 1922, and the World Cup followed in 1966 (FIS, n.d.-a). Icons of the sport include Norway's Sondre Norheim, France's Jean-Claude Killy, three-time Olympic champion at Grenoble 1968; Austria's Gustav Thöni; the Swede Ingemar Stenmark, with 86 World Cup victories; Italy's Alberto Tomba, three Olympic golds in 1988–1994; Austria's Hermann Maier and the American Lindsey Vonn, with 82 World Cup wins — all retired from the elite circuit (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
