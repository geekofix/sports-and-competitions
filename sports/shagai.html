Shagai

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shagai are the cleaned ankle bones (astragalus) of sheep, goats, horses or camels used across Central Asia for games, counting and divination; the games are called shagai in Mongolian, chückö in Kyrgyz, asyk/ashyk/aşık in Kazakh and Turkish, oshuk in Tajik and gachuha in Manchu. Each bone can land on four stable sides traditionally named horse, camel, sheep and goat (a fifth, cow, on uneven ground). The best-known competitive form is ankle-bone shooting, played at the Mongolian Naadam (formally added in 1998 and covered by the UNESCO inscription of Naadam in 2010) and as Ordo at the World Nomad Games since 2014 (106 athletes in 2026). Players of all ages and both sexes take part; shagai are still exchanged as tokens of friendship. Within the Central Asian nomad games on this site, its closest relatives are Naadam, Toguz Korgool, World Nomad Games; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

In ankle-bone shooting, each team sets a target bone (traditionally a horse shagai) and players take turns flicking their own shagai with the middle finger along a wooden rail (khashlaga) held in the other hand, trying to knock the target over or to land closest to it at a distance of about 10 m; knocked-down targets are captured and the turn continues, and teams play to an agreed score. In dice-style games four bones are tossed: two points if all show the same side, one point for a pair, and the celebrated “all four sides” (horse, camel, sheep, goat) is the luckiest throw. Other folk games include horse race (flicking a “horse” bone along a line of stationary bones) and birthing camels. At the World Nomad Games the ankle-bone shooting is played as Ordo, the ancient Kyrgyz name, under standardized rules.

History

Ankle-bone games are among the oldest games of the steppe — the bones are considered the first dice — and they are used for fortune-telling across Central Asia (horse and camel sides are lucky). Shagai games are especially popular at Naadam; Mongolia registered the ankle-bone shooting with UNESCO as part of Naadam's 2010 inscription. In Kyrgyzstan the shooting game is known as Ordo and was a competitive sport at the first World Nomad Games in 2014. The Tuvan player and modernizer Kuzhuget Ali created painted sets, and monuments to the game stand in Osh and Naryn in Kyrgyzstan. Mongolians still keep shagai in little pouches and give them as friendship tokens; wolf shagai (the calcaneus) are collected as good-luck charms.
