Fire Hockey

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Fire hockey is the popular name for the traditional Purépecha fire-ball game of Michoacán, Mexico — Uárhukua Ch'anakua (hockey) and its flaming variant pasáratakua ("the ball that is passed burning"), a stick-and-ball game played for more than a thousand years by the Purépecha people, in which teams hit a ball with curved wooden sticks toward the opponent's goal; in the fire variant the wooden ball is soaked in pine resin and petrol and set alight, and the game is played at night (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The game belongs to the Mesoamerican ballgame family that includes Basque Pelota and Pelota Purépecha on this site, and it gained international fame through the television program Trans World Sport, which featured it as "fireball hockey" (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

The game is still played in Purépecha communities such as Tzintzuntzan and Nahuatzen in Michoacán, mainly by men's teams, with women's teams increasingly participating in the non-fire variant; modern exhibition matches are played with protective equipment because the burning ball reaches high temperatures and can cause severe burns (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Rules

Uárhukua Ch'anakua is played on a rectangular earthen or grass pitch (the cancha) between two teams of five to seven players, each armed with a curved wooden stick — the uárhukua — about 1 m long with a flattened, spoon-like head used to scoop, carry and strike the ball (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The original ball was a small bundle of monarch-butterfly cocoons; today a hard ball of wrapped natural fabric or wood is used, and goals are scored by hitting the ball past the opponent's goal line between two posts or markers (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In the pasáratakua fire variant, a wooden ball is coated with pine resin and petrol, set alight and struck while burning, typically at night; because of the obvious danger, modern play of the fire variant is limited to short exhibition matches with fire-safety measures, water buckets and protective clothing, and it is not played competitively in the official community tournaments (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Players may run with the ball in the stick, pass and shoot, but body contact is limited and the goalkeeper may use hands; matches are informal, with agreed numbers of goals or time (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men and women play under the same rules in the non-fire variant, with mixed community teams common in festivals; the fire variant is traditionally a men's spectacle (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

History

Ball games with sticks or rubber balls were played across Mesoamerica from antiquity — the Aztec ōllamaliztli and the Maya ballgame are the best known — and the Purépecha of Michoacán developed their own hockey-like variant, documented since the pre-Columbian era and described by Spanish chroniclers of the 16th century (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The game remained a living tradition in the Purépecha heartland around Lake Pátzcuaro, played on village pitches during fiestas, and it was revived as a cultural spectacle in the 20th century; the fire-ball version pasáratakua was filmed for international television by Trans World Sport in the 1980s, which brought the game to a worldwide audience as "crazy fireball hockey" (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Today the comunidad purépecha organizes tournaments of the non-fire game to preserve the language and traditions, and the fire variant survives mainly as a demonstration at cultural events (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
