Cross-Country Equestrian

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Cross-country is the timed phase of eventing (three-day eventing) in which horse and rider gallop over a long course of solid natural obstacles, and it is also a standalone FEI discipline with its own championships. Governed by the Fédération Equestre Internationale (FEI), cross-country is part of the Olympic eventing program and the FEI Eventing World Championships. Courses run 3-6 km with 20-40 jumps depending on level, including ditches, water complexes, banks and logs.

Rules

Under the FEI Eventing Rules, riders complete the cross-country course within an optimum time; exceeding it adds time penalties (a fraction of a point per second over optimum, or a set penalty system at lower levels). A fall of horse or rider is a major penalty (elimination at most levels); refusals at an obstacle add penalty points (first refusal 20, second 40, third elimination), and dangerous riding can bring elimination. Riders wear a protective vest and helmet, and horses wear boots. The cross-country phase usually decides eventing, where dressage and show jumping penalties are added.

History

Cross-country riding developed from cavalry tests and fox hunting in 19th-century Ireland and Britain, and it joined the Olympics with eventing at Stockholm 1912. The FEI standardized eventing with the dressage, cross-country and jumping phases. Legendary cross-country riders include New Zealander Mark Todd, a three-time Olympic champion in eventing, and Britain's William Fox-Pitt. The FEI Eventing World Championships are held biennially, alternating with Olympic years.
