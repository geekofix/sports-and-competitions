Castells

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Castells — singular castell — are human towers built at festivals in Catalonia, Spain, by amateur teams called colles castelleres. A colla erects a tower in stages: first the pinya, a dense base of hundreds of people bracing one another; then the trunk (tronc), the stacked human levels; often a pom de dalt (a small top group of three or four) is added; and finally the enxaneta, a small child who climbs to the very top and raises one hand with four fingers extended — the aleta — to signal that the tower is complete before it is dismantled in a controlled way. The Coordinadora de Colles Castelleres de Catalunya (CCCC) is the umbrella federation uniting more than 100 colles, and on 16 November 2010 in Nairobi UNESCO inscribed castells on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (CCCC, n.d.; UNESCO, n.d.). The most prestigious competition is the biennial Concurs de Castells de Tarragona; the 30th edition took place on 4 October 2026 at the Tarraco Arena in Tarragona with twelve colles competing over five rounds, and the Castellers de Vilafranca won their 14th title with the first-ever descarregat (dismantled) 9 de 9 amb folre — a nine-level tower, nine people per level, with a secondary base (folre) — while the Colla Vella dels Castellers de Valls finished second and the Joves de Tarragona third (Concurs de Castells de Tarragona, 2026; Wikipedia, n.d.). Castells are a genuinely mixed-gender sport: women and men build the towers together in every position, no separate men's and women's categories exist, and the enxaneta at the top is very often a young girl — the 2026 winning tower is pictured below.

Rules

Competitions (concursos) are governed by the CCCC's Reglament del Concurs de Castells: at the Tarragona contest each colla attempts one castell per round, there are five rounds, and the best three attempts count toward the final ranking. A tower is carregat (loaded) the moment the enxaneta completes the aleta with four fingers raised, and descarregat (dismantled) only when it is taken down in an orderly, controlled fashion; a fall ends the attempt and the tower scores only partial difficulty points. Juries score structures by difficulty — height (number of levels), width (people per level), and added elements such as the folre, manilles and puntals increase the value, which is why the 9 de 9 amb folre cleared in 2026 is counted among the greatest towers ever completed (CCCC, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Safety is regulated in detail by the CCCC safety protocol: the pinya is mandatory for taller structures, protective helmets (casc) have been compulsory for all children in the canalla (the tower's upper levels) since 2006, and a medical team must be present at every event. There are no gender or age categories in the sport itself — teams are mixed, and children from roughly school age upward may climb as enxaneta and canalla, which is exactly why the helmet rule and the pinya exist; the rulebook is published and maintained by the CCCC, the world-level body for castells (CCCC, n.d.).

History

The documented history of castells begins in 1770 in Valls, in the Camp de Tarragona, where the first recorded human tower was built; the tradition grew out of the ball de castells, a folk dance in which participants formed human pyramids, and the motto of the movement — "Força, equilibri, valor i seny" (strength, balance, courage and common sense) — reflects those roots (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Colla Vella dels Castellers de Valls, founded in 1881, is the oldest still-active colla and shaped the modern style of the pinya in the late 19th century. The 20th century brought rapid technical progress: towers grew from eight to ten levels, the first attempts at ten-level structures date to 1998, and in November 2015 the Castellers de Vilafranca became the first colla to complete a 4 de 10 (four people per level, ten levels high); the first-ever completed 9 de 9 amb folre followed only in October 2026 (Concurs de Castells de Tarragona, 2026). Women have been part of the pinya and the upper levels throughout the documented history, and in recent decades female castellers have increasingly taken leading roles as cap de colla (head of colla) and enxaneta. On 16 November 2010, UNESCO inscribed castells on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in Nairobi, recognizing the tradition's community, intergenerational character (UNESCO, n.d.).
