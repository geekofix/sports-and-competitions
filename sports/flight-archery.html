Flight Archery

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Flight archery is the oldest competitive form of archery: the sole objective is to shoot an arrow as far as possible, with no target to hit — only the landing point is measured (Wikipedia, n.d.). It belongs to the archery family of target and field disciplines governed internationally by World Archery, but it is a non-Olympic, record-driven discipline in which specialized flight bows and ultra-light arrows are used (World Archery, n.d.). Modern flight records are ratified in bow classes such as Conventional Flight Unlimited; in the United States, USA Archery keeps official flight records, while Guinness recognizes the longest accurate target shot — 283.4 m by American Para archer Matt Stutzman, who shoots without arms, in 2015 (USA Archery, n.d.; Guinness World Records, n.d.).

The current elite level of the sport is documented in class record tables: in 1987 Don Brown shot a Conventional Flight Unlimited arrow 1,222 m (1,336 yd), a mark that long stood as the benchmark of the discipline (Wikipedia, n.d.). At multi-sport events, traditional flight shooting is still contested with historical bows — at the 2021 Islamic Solidarity Games in Konya, Türkiye, men and women competed in the "limitless flight" (flight) event with Ottoman-style equipment, and medals were decided at the award ceremony shown in the photo above (Wikipedia, n.d.). Women compete on equal terms with men in flight archery; there are separate men’s and women’s record categories.

Rules

The rules are minimal by design: the archer shoots from a marked line, the arrow is measured from the shooting line to the first point of impact or rest, and each competitor typically gets a set number of arrows (often six) per round; wind, terrain and arrow recovery are the archer’s problem (USA Archery, n.d.). Bows must meet the class specification — for "unlimited" classes essentially any draw weight and mechanical aid such as the Ottoman siper (a draw-length extension that lets the arrow pass the bow) is allowed, while "conventional" classes restrict the bow to a handheld shape (Wikipedia, n.d.). There are no age or gender restrictions beyond the separate record categories, and para archers compete in the same measured-distance format. The rulebook and record tables are maintained by the national member federations under World Archery (World Archery, n.d.).

History

Flight archery almost certainly began on the open steppes of Central Asia, but it became an obsession of the Ottoman Empire in the 15th–17th centuries: Turkish bowyers built specialized flight bows (the yay) and tiny arrows (tir), and records were inscribed on stone pillars at the Ok Meydanı range in Istanbul, where competitions drew huge crowds (Wikipedia, n.d.). The famous distance of over 800 m was first exceeded by the Ottoman archer Mustafa Tozkoparan in 1514, whose record — 884 m — stood for centuries (Wikipedia, n.d.). In Europe the sport was revived in the 19th century by societies such as the Royal Toxophilites (founded 1781), and national flight records have been kept in Britain, the United States and Scandinavia ever since (Archery GB, n.d.). Today the discipline lives on at dedicated flight shoots in the United States, Germany, the Netherlands and Türkiye, and at traditional-flight events at the Islamic Solidarity Games and the World Nomad Games.
