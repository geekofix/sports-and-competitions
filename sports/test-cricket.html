Test Cricket

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Test cricket is the longest and most traditional format of cricket: two teams of eleven play up to four innings each — two per side — over as many as five days for men and four days for women, with roughly 90 overs per day and a red ball (International Cricket Council, n.d.). The International Cricket Council (ICC) governs the game together with the Marylebone Cricket Club (MCC), which owns the Laws of Cricket, and runs the World Test Championship (International Cricket Council, n.d.). Among the current leading women's players — named first — are India's 2025 Women's Cricket World Cup champions, pictured below with the trophy, including captain Harmanpreet Kaur and top scorer Smriti Mandhana (ICC Women's Cricketer of the Year 2018 and 2021), plus Australia's Sophie Molineux, captain of the 2026 Women's T20 World Cup-winning side; by March 2026, 154 women's Tests had been played by the ten full member nations (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The men's game is anchored by the World Test Championship and the rivalry series such as the Ashes (International Cricket Council, n.d.).

Rules

A Test match is played on a pitch 22 yards (20.12 m) long and 10 ft (3.05 m) wide, with two teams of eleven; each side bats twice (four innings in all), and play is divided into sessions over up to five days for men and four days for women, at about 90 overs per day (International Cricket Council, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Captains may declare an innings closed, the follow-on can be enforced after a large first-innings lead, and matches end in a win, loss, draw or the rare tie (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The red ball is used in men's and women's Tests alike, and the Decision Review System (DRS) with ball-tracking and the third umpire is standard at Test level (International Cricket Council, n.d.). In the World Test Championship a win is worth 12 points, a tie 6 and a draw 4 (International Cricket Council, n.d.). Women's Tests are capped at four days — the only structural difference from the men's five-day format — and both genders play under the same Laws of Cricket, with U19 age-group competition below (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The official rule sources are the MCC Laws of Cricket and the ICC playing regulations (International Cricket Council, n.d.).

History

The first Test match was played at the Melbourne Cricket Ground from 15 to 19 March 1877, with Australia beating England by 45 runs; the first women's Test followed on 28–31 December 1934 in Brisbane, won by England by nine wickets (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The Women's Cricket Association was founded in 1926 and the International Women's Cricket Council in 1958; the two merged into the ICC in 2005, and by March 2026 ten full member nations had contested 154 women's Tests; the multi-format Women's Ashes has used points since 2013, and the first day-night women's Test was played in the 2017–18 Ashes (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Icons of the men's game include Sir Don Bradman of Australia (retired 1948 with the unbroken 99.94 Test average), Garfield Sobers of the West Indies (retired 1974) and Sachin Tendulkar of India (retired 2013), and of the women's game Australia's Karen Rolton (retired 2008) and England's Charlotte Edwards (retired 2016) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
