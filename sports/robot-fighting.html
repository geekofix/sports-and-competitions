Robot Fighting

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Robot fighting, also called robot combat, is a sport in which teams design, build and drive remote-controlled armored machines armed with weapons such as spinning discs, hammers, flippers, lifters and flamethrowers, and battle them inside a bullet-resistant polycarbonate arena until one machine is disabled or the judges decide (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The best-known platform is BattleBots, founded by Greg Munson and Trey Roski in 1997 after Marc Thorpe's untelevised Robot Wars events of 1994–1997, with the first official BattleBots competition at the Long Beach Pyramid in August 1999 and television runs on Comedy Central (2000–2002), ABC (2015–2016) and Discovery (2018–2023) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Other major organizers are the National Havoc Robot League (NHRL), founded in 2018 by Austin McChord and now the world's largest robot fighting tournament, and RoboGames (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). In 2022, Tantrum won the Giant Nut at World Championship VII, while 2026 brought two new formats: the BattleBots Pro League on YouTube from July 2026, won by The Twins over Witch Doctor for the Giant Wrench, and the NHRL Pro Tour, the first professional robot combat league, broadcast on DAZN from 16 May 2026 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The sport is open to women and men alike — builders and drivers of every gender compete, and mixed teams are the norm (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Under the official BattleBots rules, the premier class is the 250 lb (113 kg) heavyweight, a limit in place since the 2015 revival, with a weight bonus up to 500 lb for walking robots; fights take place in the 48 × 48 ft BattleBox arena with hazards such as screws and hammers, last three minutes, start on a light signal, and end in a knockout when a machine is counted out for ten seconds or in a judges' decision (BattleBots, n.d.). Judging uses an 11-point system — 5 points for damage, 3 for aggression and 3 for control — awarded by the judges to decide close fights (BattleBots, n.d.). Safety rules require a pre-fight technical inspection, a failsafe that stops the weapon when the signal is lost, a weapon lock for transport, and remote start; prohibited are electrical weapons, liquids, entanglement devices, EMP effects, untethered projectiles and excessive fire (BattleBots, n.d.). NHRL events run additional weight classes (3 lb, 12 lb, 30 lb and 250 lb) and feature house robots that competitors must survive, with a $1,000 bounty for switching off a house bot's power switch — the old automatic-win rule was removed in 2021 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). There are no gender classes; women and men compete together in the same brackets (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

History

The modern sport traces to Marc Thorpe, who staged the first Robot Wars competitions in the UK from 1994 to 1997 before the BBC televised the show from 1998, and to the parallel US Robot Wars events (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Greg Munson and Trey Roski then founded BattleBots in 1997, held its first event at the Long Beach Pyramid in August 1999, and sold TV rights to Comedy Central for 2000–2002; after a hiatus the show returned on ABC in 2015–2016 and moved to Discovery in 2018, running through season 12 in 2023 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Legendary machines that shaped the sport include Tombstone with its spinning bar, Bite Force, which won the Giant Nut three times, End Game, the 2019 champion, Witch Doctor and the 2022 world champion Tantrum (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). On the independent side, Austin McChord founded the National Havoc Robot League in 2018, with its inaugural event in December 2018, growing it into the world's largest robot fighting tournament (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
