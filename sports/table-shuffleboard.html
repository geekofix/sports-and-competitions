Table Shuffleboard

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Table shuffleboard (also called American shuffleboard, indoor shuffleboard, slingers, shufflepuck, quoits or — in the Netherlands — sjoelen) is a bar and parlour game in which players push weighted metal-and-plastic pucks (weights) by hand down a long, smooth, waxed wooden table into a triangular scoring area at the far end. Unlike its deck cousin, it is played indoors on 9–22 ft tables and is common in bars, clubs and league play; women's leagues and mixed doubles exist alongside men's competition under identical rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Scoring zones are numbered 1-2-3, with a puck hanging off the far end of the table (a "hanger") worth 4 (or 5 on 1-2-3-4 tables), and a puck must lie fully inside a zone to count (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Each player or team slides four pucks per frame — eight in total — down the table, alternating shots, and only the pucks of the player or team in the lead score at the end of the frame; a puck touching a line scores nothing, and a puck that slides off the side gutter is out of play for the rest of the frame (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The table surface is sprinkled with silicone beads (or historically sawdust) to reduce friction, and the far end carries the numbered scoring triangle (1-2-3, with the hanger worth 4 or 5) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Games are played to 15 points in singles and 21 in doubles (house rules often 11 or 21), with the frame completed at the winning score; a "knock-off" variant lets players dislodge opponents' pucks, and team play rotates two players per side (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Men's and women's leagues play the same table, pucks and scoring (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

History

Table shuffleboard descends from the Tudor English games shovillabourde and shove ha'penny — Henry VIII was a keen player — which Dutch settlers brought to New York; deck shuffleboard was laid out ashore at the Lyndhurst Hotel in Daytona Beach in 1913 and the St. Petersburg Shuffleboard Club (1924) boasted 116 courts by 1939 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The indoor table version developed for bars and homes from the 19th century onward and is still sold as sjoelen in the Netherlands, where the Dutch sjoelen bond governs league play (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
