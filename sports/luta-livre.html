Luta Livre

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Luta livre ("free fight") is the Brazilian catch-wrestling and submission-grappling tradition developed without the gi (kimono), and it is the direct ancestor of vale tudo, Brazilian jiu-jitsu and modern MMA (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The competitive form, Luta Livre Esportiva, was created in Rio de Janeiro in the mid-20th century by Euclydes "Tatu" Hatem, who famously defeated George Gracie in a vale tudo bout in the 1940s, establishing ground fighting and submissions as decisive (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Today lucha livre is practiced worldwide as no-gi submission grappling, with international competition under the International Brazilian Luta Livre Federation (IBLLF) and a ruleset shared with catch wrestling and submission grappling: a bout is won by submission, pinfall, knockout or referee stoppage, with points for takedowns and dominant positions in point-scoring formats (International Brazilian Luta Livre Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Women compete in lucha livre and no-gi grappling at every level, from the ADCC world championships to MMA, where Brazilian women have been pioneers (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

Rules

In lucha livre esportiva and no-gi submission grappling, the bout starts standing and may go to the ground; a wrestler wins by forcing the opponent to submit (tap), by a pinfall that holds the opponent's shoulders or back to the mat for the required time, by knockout or technical knockout, or when the referee stops the contest (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Point-scoring formats award points for takedowns, guard passes, mounts and back control — typically 2 points for a takedown, 3 for a guard pass, 4 for mount or back mount — and the athlete with the most points when time expires wins (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Bouts are usually one round of 5–10 minutes in grappling, or multiple 5-minute rounds in the MMA-derived format (International Brazilian Luta Livre Federation, n.d.). Illegal techniques include eye gouging, biting, hair pulling, small-joint manipulation and slamming in some rulesets; the gi is prohibited, so grips are taken on the body, wrists and ankles (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Men's and women's divisions use identical rules, with weight classes from featherweight to heavyweight and an open/absolute category at major events (International Brazilian Luta Livre Federation, n.d.).

History

Catch wrestling reached Brazil in the late 19th and early 20th centuries through European immigrants and circus wrestlers, and the Gracie family — George, Carlos, Osvaldo, Gastão and Hélio — developed Brazilian jiu-jitsu from it in the 1920s–1940s (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In parallel, Euclydes "Tatu" Hatem founded Luta Livre Esportiva in Rio de Janeiro in the mid-20th century with an emphasis on ground fighting and submissions, and his victories over the Gracies in vale tudo matches proved the style's effectiveness (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Vale tudo — "anything goes" fighting with minimal rules — became the testing ground from which modern MMA emerged in the 1990s, and today's no-gi submission grappling competitions (ADCC and lucha livre events) descend directly from this line (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). On the women's side, Brazilian circus and festival wrestling produced famous female fighters such as Marina Oliveira, "Mulher Pantera" (born 1947 in Lins), who wrestled professionally across South America from the 1970s, and today women compete at the highest level of no-gi grappling and MMA (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).
