Shuffleboard

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shuffleboard (deck shuffleboard) is a target game in which players use long cues to push weighted discs down a narrow, waxed concrete court so that they come to rest within a marked scoring diagram at the opposite end. Played in singles or doubles, it is a sport of low physical demand but high strategic depth, popular in the United States \u2014 especially Florida's retirement communities \u2014 and on cruise ships; table shuffleboard is a related, separate game played by hand on a wooden board. It is organised internationally by the International Shuffleboard Association (ISA), founded in 1979.

Rules

Under National Shuffleboard Association rules, the regulation court is a 52 by 6 foot concrete (or terrazzo) slab, with baselines 39 feet apart and a scoring triangle \u2014 six feet wide and nine deep \u2014 at each end: zones marked 10, 8, 8, 7 and 10-off from front to back, the "10-off" area at the apex deducting ten points from any disc that stops there; discs that stop out of bounds or on lines score nothing. Each player has four discs \u2014 one side red, the other black (in major play; colours under ISA vary) \u2014 and shots alternate between the two ends, using a cue up to 6 feet 3 inches long; disc dimensions are approximately 1 by 6 inches. A match is played until one side reaches a set score, commonly 75 or 100, once eight shots are made \u2014 in singles, players then walk to the opposite end. Players may knock opponents' discs out of scoring position or block their own, making placement as important as pushing power.

History

Shuffleboard's ancestry lies in Tudor England, where "shovillaborde" was played by Henry VIII for stakes and reference is made to shovelboard in Shakespeare; its ancestor shove-groat \u2014 a game of sliding coins \u2014 was widespread enough to be banned by the Unlawful Games Act 1541, and its pub-game descendant shove ha'penny is still played today. The deck version grew out of improvised shipboard games of the 1830s, played by ocean-liner passengers passing time at sea, and was brought ashore in 1913 when Robert Ball laid out a land court at his Lyndhurst Hotel in Daytona Beach, Florida; the St. Petersburg Shuffleboard Club (founded 1924) grew to 116 courts by 1939, the Florida Shuffleboard Association organised in 1928, and the National Shuffleboard Association was formed in 1931, holding the first national tournament that year. The International Shuffleboard Association followed in 1979, and today, after a post-1960s decline, the game enjoys renewed popularity among both older players and a younger shuffleboard-scene generation, especially in the United States and Canada.
