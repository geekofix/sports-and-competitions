Trampoline

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

The trampoline is a rebound apparatus — a strong woven bed held under tension by more than a hundred coiled springs inside a rectangular steel frame — and trampolining is the sport and recreation performed on it: athletes bounce on the bed and launch up to roughly 10 m (33 ft) into the air to perform somersaults, twists and acrobatic figures (Wikipedia, n.d.). The name itself comes from the Spanish trampolín, "diving board" (Wikipedia, n.d.). Competitive trampolining is an Olympic gymnastics discipline contested since Sydney 2000, while the same apparatus powers backyard recreation, commercial trampoline parks and the team sports slamball and bossaball (Olympics, n.d.). Women have been full Olympic competitors since the discipline's debut in 2000 — the first women's gold went to Russia's Irina Karavaeva — and today's leading female trampolinists include China's Hu Yicheng, the 2025 world champion, and Britain's Bryony Page, Olympic champion at Paris 2024, while Ivan Litvinovich became the first man to win two Olympic trampoline titles (Tokyo 2020 and Paris 2024) and Wang Zisai took the 2025 men's world title (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).

Rules

A competition trampoline has a bed of about 4.28 m × 2.14 m suspended roughly 1.15 m above the floor by springs inside a steel frame of about 5.05 m × 3.05 m, padded end decks and a spotting rig (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). In competition — detailed under trampoline gymnastics on this site — routines of 10 consecutive elements are scored on difficulty, execution and, at the Olympics, measured time of flight, and landings are permitted on the feet, seat, front or back, while touching the frame, safety platform or a spotter interrupts the routine (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.). For recreational use the rules are safety standards rather than scoring: in Europe domestic trampolines must meet the EN 71-14 safety standard, in-ground or netted/enclosed installations are required for shared or public use, and multi-person trampolines carry additional load and spacing rules (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men and women compete in identical individual events at the Olympic Games and FIG World Championships, and both genders train on the same apparatus dimensions (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.).

History

The modern trampoline was developed at the University of Iowa: in 1934 the gymnast George Nissen (1914–2010) and his coach Larry Griswold built the first prototype from angle iron, canvas and rubber inner-tube springs, refining it into the first modern trampoline by 1936 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Nissen heard the Spanish word trampolín on a demonstration tour in Mexico in the late 1930s and anglicized it as the trademark; he received US patent 2,370,990 for his "Tumbling Device" on 6 March 1945, and the Griswold-Nissen Trampoline & Tumbling Company began commercial production in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, in 1941/1942 (Wikipedia, n.d.). During World War II the US Navy used trampolines to train pilots in spatial orientation, and afterwards NASA used them for astronaut training; Nissen also invented the trampoline game Spaceball together with astronaut Scott Carpenter (Wikipedia, n.d.). The English pioneer Ted Blake set up Nissen's first European factory in Brentwood, Essex, in 1956, and in 1971 Nissen and Griswold founded the United States Tumbling & Trampoline Association (Wikipedia, n.d.). International competition began with the first Trampoline World Championships at the Royal Albert Hall in London on 21 March 1964, organized by the newly founded International Trampoline Federation (FIT): the first world champions were America's Dan Millman (men) and Judy Wills Cline (women), and Wills went on to defend her title seven more times, making her the most decorated trampoline champion of all time with eight titles (Wikipedia, n.d.). The FIT became a FIG member on 1 January 1999, and trampolining made its Olympic debut at Sydney 2000, which the 86-year-old Nissen attended (Wikipedia, n.d.). Retired icons of the sport include Canada's Rosie MacLennan (Olympic gold 2012 and 2016, world titles 2013 and 2015) and China's Dong Dong (Olympic gold 2008 and 2012) (Wikipedia, n.d.).
