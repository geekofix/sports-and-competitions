Lacrosse

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Lacrosse is a team stick-and-ball sport that originated among the Indigenous peoples of North America — the Haudenosaunee played a ceremonial game (baggataway) from at least the 12th century — and is today governed by World Lacrosse, founded on 30 September 2008 from the merger of the men's and women's international federations, with 92 member nations and full IOC recognition since 2021 (World Lacrosse, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). At the top of the women's game stands Charlotte North of the United States, the leading scorer of the 2026 World Lacrosse Women's Field Championship in Tokyo (24 July – 2 August 2026), where the USA won its fifth consecutive world title ahead of Canada (Wikipedia, n.d.-b); on the men's side the 2027 World Championship will also be held in Tokyo (World Lacrosse, n.d.-b). Lacrosse was an Olympic sport in 1904 and 1908 and returns at Los Angeles 2028 in the sixes format — 6 v 6 — which the IOC approved on 16 October 2023 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Three main variants are played: field lacrosse (10 v 10), box lacrosse (6 v 6 indoors) and sixes (World Lacrosse, n.d.-b).

Within the lacrosse and stick-games family on this site, its closest relatives are Field Lacrosse, Box Lacrosse, Sixes Lacrosse, Hurling, Camogie; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

Field lacrosse is played 10 v 10 (three attackers, three midfielders, three defenders and one goalkeeper) on a field about 100–110 yards long and 53–60 yards wide, with a goal crease of 8.5 ft radius in the women's game and 9 ft in the men's; play starts with a draw (face-off), goals count one point, and the women's and men's games are four 15-minute quarters (World Lacrosse, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women's field lacrosse uses the same stick and ball rules but with a shallower crease, no checking to the head, and mandatory protective equipment (goggles, mouthguard; gloves optional) — men additionally wear full pads and helmets (World Lacrosse, n.d.-b). Box lacrosse is 6 v 6 in an ice-hockey rink with full contact and boards; sixes — the LA 2028 Olympic format — is 6 v 6 (five runners plus a goalkeeper) on a reduced field of about 70 × 40 m with a 3 m radius crease, 8-minute quarters, a 30-second shot clock and a center draw after each goal (World Lacrosse, n.d.-b). Age classes run U20 and U19 at world level, and men's and women's tournaments are separate at every championship (World Lacrosse, n.d.-a). Rule source: World Lacrosse rule books, https://worldlacrosse.sport/ (World Lacrosse, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). See field lacrosse, box lacrosse and sixes lacrosse on this site.

History

Indigenous peoples of northeastern North America played stick-and-ball games for centuries; French Jesuit missionaries in the 17th century named the Haudenosaunee game "la crosse" after the stick (crosse) resembling a bishop's crozier (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Modern lacrosse was codified in Canada: George Beers and the Montreal Lacrosse Club published the first written rules in 1867, the sport was an Olympic medal event at St. Louis 1904 and London 1908, and the International Lacrosse Federation (men) was founded in 1986 with the women's federation (IFWLA) dating to 1972 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The two federations merged on 30 September 2008 into World Lacrosse, headquartered in Colorado Springs, which gained IOC recognition in 2021; the Premier Lacrosse League (PLL) launched in 2019 as the top professional outdoor league, and the IOC approved sixes lacrosse for Los Angeles 2028 on 16 October 2023 (World Lacrosse, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
