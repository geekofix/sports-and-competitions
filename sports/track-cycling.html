Track Cycling

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Track cycling is bicycle racing on a steeply banked oval velodrome — the standard track is 250 m long with banking up to about 45° — and it is governed worldwide by the Union Cycliste Internationale (UCI), which has run the UCI Track Cycling World Championships since 1893 and oversees the Olympic track programme (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.). The disciplines split into sprint events (individual sprint, team sprint, keirin, time trial) and endurance events (individual and team pursuit, scratch race, points race, madison, elimination race and the multi-event omnium) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women have competed at the World Championships for over a century and at the Olympic Games since Seoul 1988; among the current leading riders — women first — are Ellesse Andrews of New Zealand, the Paris 2024 Olympic champion in the sprint and keirin and a multiple world champion, pictured below, and Ireland's Lara Gillespie, the 2025 world elimination race champion, alongside the men's stars Harrie Lavreysen of the Netherlands (20 world titles, Olympic sprint, team sprint and keirin champion) and Jeffrey Hoogland (Olympics, n.d.; Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

UCI regulations define the velodrome — classically 250 m (tracks from 133 m to 500 m are recognized), at least about 7 m wide, with banking up to roughly 45° — and the bike: a fixed-gear machine with no brakes, drop handlebars and a minimum weight of 6.8 kg, plus a mandatory helmet (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In the sprint, riders first qualify with a 200 m flying time trial, then race head-to-head over 2–3 laps; the team sprint pits national teams of 2–3 riders against the clock (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.). The keirin originated in Japan: riders follow a pacer derny motorcycle that peels off with about 750 m to go, leaving a flat-out sprint (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Time trials are 1 km for men and 500 m for women; the individual pursuit is a duel over 4 km from opposite sides of the track, and the team pursuit is 4 km for teams of four (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.). Endurance bunch races: the scratch race (15 km elite men, 10 km elite women) is first across the line wins; the points race awards 5-3-2-1 points at intermediate sprints (every 10 laps on tracks under 333.33 m, every 5 laps on longer ones) plus 20 points for lapping the field; the madison is a relay race for teams of two; and the omnium — held in one day since Tokyo 2020 — combines a scratch race, tempo race, elimination race and points race (10 km men / 7.5 km women for scratch and tempo, 25 km men / 20 km women for the points race), with placings worth 40, 38, 36 points and so on (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.). Men and women contest the same events, with some shorter women's distances, and para-cycling events run under UCI regulations as well (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.). The rulebook is Part 3 (Track Races) of the UCI Cycling Regulations (Union Cycliste Internationale, n.d.).

History

The first track cycling world championships were held in Chicago in 1893, and the Union Cycliste Internationale was founded on 14 April 1900 in Paris by the national federations of Belgium, France, Italy, Switzerland and the United States (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The sport boomed in the 1920s and 1930s with famous indoor six-day races at Madison Square Garden in New York and the Vélodrome d'Hiver in Paris, and track cycling has been on the Olympic programme at every Games except Stockholm 1912, with women's track events added at Seoul 1988 (Olympics, n.d.). Icons include Belgium's Eddy Merckx (b. 1945), who set the hour record twice (1972 and 1984) and dominated six-day racing; France's Jeannie Longo (b. 1958), a 13-time world champion and hour-record holder who retired in 2011; Britain's Sir Chris Hoy (b. 1976), winner of six Olympic gold medals, retired in 2013; Germany's Kristina Vogel (b. 1990), eleven-time world champion, retired after a 2018 training crash; and Australia's Anna Meares (b. 1983), 2004 Olympic sprint champion, retired in 2016 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
