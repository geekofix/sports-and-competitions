Cross-Country Skiing Skiathlon

≈ 6 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

The skiathlon is cross-country skiing's combined-technique race: athletes start together in a mass start, ski the first half of the course in classic technique, change skis and poles in a designated exchange zone, and finish the second half in freestyle (skating) — the discipline that decides who is the most complete cross-country skier (FIS, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). It is governed by the FIS under the International Competition Rules and has been an Olympic event since Salt Lake City 2002 (FIS, n.d.-a). A historic milestone was reached at the Milano Cortina 2026 Winter Olympics: for the first time the women's skiathlon was raced over the same 20 km distance as the men's (10 km classic + 10 km freestyle) instead of the previous 15 km (FIS, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The women's 20 km skiathlon on 7 February 2026 at Lago di Tesero was won by Frida Karlsson of Sweden, with Ebba Andersson (Sweden) second and Heidi Weng (Norway) third; the men's 20 km skiathlon went to Johannes Høsflot Klæbo of Norway, as part of his six-gold sweep in Tesero (FIS, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In the World Cup, skiathlons are held at 10+10 km, 15+15 km and 20+20 km distances over the season, and the Tour de Ski traditionally includes a skiathlon stage (FIS, n.d.-b). The skiathlon is open to women and men over identical distances at the Olympics since 2026 and at World Championships; junior, U23 and para (sit-ski) categories race their own skiathlons; at the end of 2026 the leading current athletes are the 2026 Olympic medalists — Frida Karlsson, Ebba Andersson and Heidi Weng among the women, Johannes Høsflot Klæbo among the men (FIS, n.d.-b). See also Cross-Country Skiing and Cross-Country Skiing Sprint on this site (FIS — International Ski and Snowboard Federation, n.d.-a) (FIS — International Ski and Snowboard Federation, n.d.-b).

Rules

Under the FIS International Competition Rules, the skiathlon is always a mass-start race: all competitors start together, seeded by World Cup ranking, and the first to the finish wins (FIS, n.d.-a). The course is divided into two halves of equal distance — at the Olympics 10 km classic followed by 10 km freestyle (20 km total), at World Cup events typically 5+5, 7.5+7.5, 10+10 or 15+15 km per technique (FIS, n.d.-a). Between the halves, athletes must pass through a marked exchange zone where they change skis and poles for the freestyle half; the clock keeps running throughout, so the exchange is a decisive tactical moment (FIS, n.d.-a). In the classic half, skis must move forward in prepared tracks and diagonal-stride or double-poling technique is required; any skating motion is a fault; in the freestyle half, any technique is allowed (FIS, n.d.-a). Equipment rules: classic skis and poles for the first half, skate skis and poles for the second, both FIS-certified, with ski length limited relative to the athlete's height; waxing is free (FIS, n.d.-a). There are no intermediate sprints or time bonuses in the skiathlon — the result is pure finishing time; lapping rules and safety rules (e.g., on narrow courses) follow the ICR (FIS, n.d.-a). The rules are identical for women and men — since Milano Cortina 2026 the Olympic skiathlon is 20 km for both genders, the first equal-distance Games in history — and junior, U23 and para cross-country skiing (sit-ski classes) contest their own skiathlons at championships (FIS, n.d.-a; FIS, n.d.-b).

History

The skiathlon was invented to combine the two techniques in one race and entered the Olympic program at Salt Lake City 2002, where the inaugural men's 20 km (10+10 km) was won by Norway's Thomas Alsgaard and the women's 10 km (5+5 km) by Canada's Beckie Scott (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The distance grew over the editions — 15+15 km for men and 7.5+7.5 km for women from 2006 onward — until Milano Cortina 2026 equalized the Olympic skiathlon at 20 km for both genders for the first time (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The discipline's greatest specialists include Norway's Petter Northug (b. 1986), Olympic champion 2010 in the 50 km and double world skiathlon champion, whose sprint finishes made him a crowd favorite; Sweden's Marcus Hellner (b. 1985), Olympic gold in the team sprint and relay; and on the women's side, the dominant Norwegian trio of Marit Bjørgen (b. 1980), Therese Johaug (b. 1988) and Astrid Uhrenholdt Jacobsen (b. 1987), all multiple Olympic and world skiathlon medalists (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). At the 2026 Games, Johannes Høsflot Klæbo of Norway won the men's 20 km skiathlon as part of his unprecedented six gold medals in Tesero, while Frida Karlsson's victory in the women's 20 km — the first equal-distance Olympic skiathlon — underlined the depth of Swedish women's skiing alongside Ebba Andersson and Heidi Weng (FIS, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
