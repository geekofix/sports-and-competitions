Outhouse Racing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Outhouse racing is a novelty team race in which decorated outhouses (privies) mounted on wheels are pushed or pulled through the streets of a town while one team member sits inside on the "throne" at all times. The best-known event is the Great Klondike International Outhouse Race in Dawson City, Yukon, Canada, held every Labour Day weekend since 1977; similar races are held in Cochrane and Almonte in Canada, Bristol, Connecticut, and many other towns in North America. The races combine speed, engineering and humor, and are usually part of local festivals.

Rules

A team typically consists of three to five members: one rider who must remain seated inside the outhouse for the whole race and two to four runners who push or pull it along a set course through town, usually about 2 km long in Dawson City. The outhouse rides on four caster wheels or, in winter variants, on skis across a frozen lake. The team with the fastest time wins; many events run head-to-head heats leading to a final. Decoration and costume categories are judged separately, and the race committee can disqualify any outhouse deemed unsafe or unsound. Obstacles and scavenger stops may be part of the course.

History

The Great Klondike International Outhouse Race in Dawson City began in 1977 as an idea of Karl Crosby of the local tourism office for Canada's centennial year, inspired by a miner's team that raced an actual outhouse whose wheels collapsed along the course; it started as a serious 5 km footrace between distance runners and evolved into the costumed spectacle held today. The tradition spread across North America: a Lions Club in Sullivan County, Pennsylvania, introduced outhouse races at its Founder's Day celebrations after hearing of a small Minnesota town that raced outhouses on skis across a frozen lake, and similar events followed in Mackinaw City, Michigan, billed as "the best case of the runs you'll ever have", in Bristol, Connecticut, and in Cochrane, Alberta, where the race grew out of the rodeo grounds. Today dozens of communities run their own outhouse races each year.
