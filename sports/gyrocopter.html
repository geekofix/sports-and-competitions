Gyrocopter

≈ 2 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

A gyrocopter (autogyro) is a rotorcraft in which an unpowered rotor autorotates in flight while a separate engine-driven propeller provides thrust; it cannot hover but can fly very slowly and land in short distances (Wikipedia, n.d.). Gyrocopters are an FAI air sport under the Rotorcraft Commission (CIG) and sit in the air-sports family.

Figure: Felix König. (2010-08-22). Gyrocopter in flight, 2010 [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. CC BY 3.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Gyrocopter_2010.JPG

FAI rotorcraft competition includes precision flying, navigation and slalom events at the World Gyrocopter Championships, and FAI ratifies gyrocopter speed, altitude and distance records (FAI, n.d.). The class covers gyroplanes from single-seat open frames to enclosed two-seat touring machines (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Rules

Gyrocopters are certified as normal-category or microlight aircraft depending on weight; pilots need the appropriate licence and a gyroplane rating (FAI, n.d.). The free-turning rotor makes the gyrocopter inherently stable and resistant to stalls, but pilots must still respect Vne and never push the stick forward abruptly (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men and women compete together in the same events.

History

The autogyro was invented by Spanish engineer Juan de la Cierva, whose C.4 made the first successful autogyro flight on 17 January 1923 near Madrid after earlier designs C.1–C.3 had failed (Wikipedia, n.d.). Cierva solved the problem of asymmetric lift with flapping hinges on the rotor blades; by the 1930s autogyros were flying across Europe and America, and the Pitcairn and Kellett companies built licenced versions in the US (Wikipedia, n.d.). After helicopters made the autogyro obsolete for military use, it survived as a sport aircraft, and today the FAI world championships keep competition gyrocoptering alive.
