Bodysurfing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Bodysurfing is wave riding using only the body — no board — aided by swim fins; it predates boardsurfing and is governed internationally by the International Surfing Association (International Surfing Association [ISA], n.d.-a), which includes bodysurfing in its definition of surfing. The two biggest annual competitions are the Pipeline Bodysurfing Classic on Oahu, Hawaii (held since February 1971) and the Oceanside World Bodysurfing Championship in California (since 1977); the 2026 International Bodysurfing Association world tour finals featured 48 men and 24 women (ISA, n.d.-b) (International Surfing Association, n.d.-a) (International Surfing Association, n.d.-b).

The women's side is well represented: the Pipeline Bodysurfing Classic and the Oceanside world championship have run women's divisions for decades, with Hawaiian-Australian riders among the most successful; legendary figures include Hawaii's Mark Cunningham (six Pipeline titles) and Buffalo Keaulana (multiple world titles) (ISA, n.d.-b).

Rules

Bodysurfing contests are judged on the best waves of a heat (typically 20–30 minutes, best 3–5 waves scored on a 0–10 scale per wave for speed, power, control, and maneuvers such as rolls, spins and aerials); riders must wear fins, and handplanes may be used in some divisions. The take-off zone at Pipeline is strictly defined, and priority rules govern who has right of way on a wave (ISA, n.d.-a). Equipment rules limit fin size and prohibit boards in pure bodysurfing divisions; wetsuits are standard in cold water. Men's and women's events use identical judging criteria, heat lengths and wave limits (ISA, n.d.-b).

History

Bodysurfing predates boardsurfing by millennia — Polynesian watermen rode waves with their bodies long before boards existed. In modern times, Fred Williams was taught bodysurfing at Sydney's Coogee Beach in 1899 by Tommy Tanna, and Wally O'Conner popularized it in the United States in the 1920s; Duke Kahanamoku demonstrated it at the 1912 Olympics. The Churchill swim fin, invented in 1940, transformed performance, and the Pipeline Bodysurfing Classic began in February 1971 — the first surfing event at Pipeline — with Mike Stewart setting a record of 13–15 wins; the Oceanside World Bodysurfing Championship followed in 1977 (ISA, n.d.-b). Hazards include spinal injuries in heavy shorebreak, which is why contests enforce strict safety and medical plans.
