Disc Dog

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Disc dog is a competitive dog sport in which handler-and-dog teams throw flying discs in events judged on distance, accuracy and choreography; it is a recognized, regulated animal sport in the same sense as equestrianism (Ashley Whippet Invitational [AWI], n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The sport took off on 5 August 1974, when the dog Ashley Whippet and her handler Alex Stein performed at a Los Angeles Dodgers game, leaping 9 ft to catch a frisbee thrown at 35 mph (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Frisbee Dog World Championship was created with Irv Lander and Eldon McIntire, and today the largest competition series is Skyhoundz (founded 2000 with Hyperflite discs), alongside the UFO World Cup, the Ashley Whippet Invitational (AWI) and the United States Disc Dog Nationals (USDDN) (Skyhoundz, n.d.; AWI, n.d.; United States Disc Dog Nationals, n.d.). The main event formats are toss-and-fetch (timed rounds scored by distance zones, with bonus points for airborne catches), freestyle (choreographed routines of 90–120 seconds judged for difficulty, execution and teamwork) and long distance (measured catches) (Skyhoundz, n.d.). Divisions include Open, Sport, Pairs Freestyle, MicroDog (small dogs) and Youth (AWI, n.d.). Men and women handlers compete in the same divisions on equal terms, with long distance sometimes split by gender (USDDN, n.d.). Among the sport's icons is the Romanian team Adrian Stoica and his dog Rory, world champions at UFO 2014 and European champions at AWI, UFO and Skyhoundz 2014, pictured below (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Rules

Under the Skyhoundz/AWI/USDDN rule sets, competitions are organized in divisions — Open, Sport, Pairs Freestyle, MicroDog and Youth — at local, national and international levels (Skyhoundz, n.d.; AWI, n.d.). In toss-and-fetch, the clock runs 60 seconds and catches are scored by the zone in which the dog catches the disc (typically 10/20/30/40-point zones at increasing distances), with a bonus (commonly +0.5 point per disc) for catches made while the dog is airborne; dropped discs score zero (Skyhoundz, n.d.). Freestyle routines last 90–120 seconds and are judged on difficulty, execution, choreography and the dog-handler teamwork, with deductions for dropped discs (AWI, n.d.). Long-distance events measure the distance of caught throws, with bonus zones for catches beyond a set line (USDDN, n.d.). Dogs must be vaccinated and under handler control; welfare rules require water, shade and rest (USDDN, n.d.). Women and men compete in identical divisions and scoring, with only the long-distance event sometimes split into men's and women's fields (Skyhoundz, n.d.).

History

Alex Stein and Ashley Whippet's 5 August 1974 performance at Dodger Stadium — a 35 mph throw and a 9 ft leap — is regarded as the moment disc dog became a spectator sport (Wikipedia, n.d.). Irv Lander and Eldon McIntire formalized the Frisbee Dog World Championship in the late 1970s, and the sport grew through the 1980s–90s into organized circuits (USDDN, n.d.). Skyhoundz was founded in 2000 together with Hyperflite's durable K10 disc, becoming the world's largest disc dog series with hundreds of events per year (Skyhoundz, n.d.). The UFO World Cup and the UpDog Challenge added further international championship formats, and today USDDN crowns national champions (UFO World Cup, n.d.; AWI, n.d.). Iconic teams include Adrian Stoica and Rory (UFO world champions 2014) and the American Ashley Whippet legacy, after which the AWI is named (Wikipedia, n.d.).
