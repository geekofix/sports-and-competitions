Judo

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Judo is the Japanese martial art and Olympic combat sport founded by Jigoro Kano (born 28 October 1860 in Kobe, died 4 May 1938) at the Kodokan in Tokyo in 1882, built on throws, takedowns, pins and submissions rather than strikes (Kodokan, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It is governed worldwide by the International Judo Federation (IJF), founded in 1951 by 13 European national federations, which runs the World Championships (since 1956, plus a mixed team event) and the World Judo Tour of Grand Slam, Grand Prix and Masters events (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a). Judo has been an Olympic sport for men since the Tokyo 1964 Games and for women since Barcelona 1992 (demonstration in 1988) (International Olympic Committee, n.d.). A contest lasts four minutes of real time for both sexes on a tatami contest area of 8 × 8 m to 10 × 10 m inside a minimum 14 × 14 m safety zone, with white vs blue judogi (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a). In the 2026–28 Olympic cycle the scoring is ippon, waza-ari (two waza-ari combine to ippon) and yuko, which was reinstated in 2025; a hold-down of 20 seconds scores ippon and 10–19 seconds scores waza-ari; three shido penalties mean an indirect hansoku-make loss, and dangerous acts earn a direct hansoku-make; if no score is made, golden score continues until the first score (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a). Seven weight classes are contested for each sex — men −60, −66, −73, −81, −90, −100 and +100 kg; women −48, −52, −57, −63, −70, −78 and +78 kg — with CARE video review available to referees (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Among the leading women in 2026 are the reigning world and Olympic champions across the seven categories — including Mali's Deniba Konare, silver medallist at the 2023 European Games — while the men's podiums are headed by the 2025 world champions such as Tajikistan's Obid Dzhebov (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Under the IJF contest rules, a judoka wins immediately with ippon — a decisive throw, a 20-second pin (osaekomi), a submission by choke or joint lock, or the opponent's disqualification; two waza-ari awards equal ippon, and yuko (reinstated in 2025) is the lowest score and no longer decides a contest alone (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a). A pin of 10 to 19 seconds scores waza-ari (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Penalties progress shido 1–3, and the third shido is an indirect hansoku-make; a direct hansoku-make is given for acts that endanger the opponent's safety, such as a direct dive onto the head or a submission applied to a bent arm in a dangerous way (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a). Normal time is four minutes for men and women alike; if no score is made, golden score continues indefinitely until the first score, with no time limit (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a). Gripping must be established quickly, false attacks and defensive postures are penalized, and a judoka may not touch the opponent's leg with the hands or apply leglocks in randori — leglocks are allowed only in specific ne-waza situations under current rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The contest area is 8 × 8 m to 10 × 10 m inside a minimum 14 × 14 m zone, and competitors wear a white or blue judogi (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a). Competitions are divided into the seven weight classes for men and for women, plus open and team events; kata (forms) competitions are also held at national level (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a). Women compete under identical rules to men — same mat, same time, same scoring — with the weight classes adjusted (−48 to +78 kg) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

History

Jigoro Kano, a jujutsu expert of the Tenjin Shinyo-ryu and Kito-ryu schools, opened the Kodokan — "hall for the study of the way" — in 1882 at Eisho-ji temple in Ueno, Tokyo, with nine disciples and twelve tatami mats, synthesizing techniques into judo and emphasizing randori (free practice) and the principles of seiryoku zenyo (maximum efficiency, minimum effort) and jita kyoei (mutual welfare) (Kodokan, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Kano introduced the kyu/dan ranking and the colored belts, and judo spread through the Japanese police, schools and universities (Kodokan, n.d.). It entered the Olympics at Tokyo 1964 — where the Dutchman Anton Geesink became the first non-Japanese Olympic champion — and permanently in 1972; women's judo joined in Barcelona 1992 after a demonstration in 1988 (International Olympic Committee, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The IJF was founded in 1951 and now counts more than 200 member federations (International Judo Federation, n.d.-a). Among the icons are Teddy Riner of France, ten-time world champion, Ryoko Tani of Japan — named by the IJF in 2011 as the best female judoka of all time, with two Olympic gold medals and five world titles — and Kaori Yamaguchi, the first Japanese woman to win a world title in 1984 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
