Judo

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Judo is a Japanese martial art, sport and philosophy founded by Jigoro Kano in 1882 at the Kodokan in Tokyo, built on throws, takedowns, pins and submissions rather than strikes. Two competitors (judoka) wearing a judogi face each other on a 8 m x 8 m contest area inside a larger safety zone and try to score ippon — a decisive throw, a 20-second pin or a submission — or to accumulate waza-ari and win on points. Judo has been an Olympic sport since the Tokyo 1964 Games (women since Barcelona 1992) and is governed worldwide by the International Judo Federation (IJF), founded in 1951.

Rules

Under IJF rules, a contest lasts four minutes of real time and ends immediately with ippon for a decisive throw, a hold-down (osaekomi) of 20 seconds, a choke or joint lock forcing submission, or disqualification of the opponent. A lesser score, waza-ari (including a 10-second hold-down), is worth half an ippon, and two waza-ari combine to ippon. Penalties (shido) are given for minor infringements, and three shidos mean hansoku-make, a loss by disqualification; if no score is made in normal time, golden score continues until the first score. Competitions are divided into weight categories, and kata (pre-arranged forms) are also contested. Gripping, blocking with a stiff arm and false attacks are restricted to keep the fight dynamic.

History

Jigoro Kano, a jujutsu expert, opened the Kodokan ("hall for the study of the way") in 1882 and synthesized techniques from the old jujutsu schools into judo, emphasizing randori (free practice) and the principle of seiryoku zenyo, maximum efficiency with minimum effort. Kano introduced the colored belt (kyu/dan) ranking system, and judo spread through Japan's police and schools. It was included in the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, where the Dutchman Anton Geesink became the first non-Japanese Olympic champion, and women's judo joined the program in 1992. The IJF was founded in 1951 and now counts over 200 national federations. Legendary champions include Yasuhiro Yamashita, Teddy Riner, who has won ten world titles, and Japan's Ryoko Tani (now Ryoko Tamura), a seven-time world champion and five-time Olympic medalist.
