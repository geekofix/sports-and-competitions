Muay Thai

≈ 6 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Muay Thai (muaythai), the Thai national martial art known as "the art of eight limbs", is a full-contact striking sport that uses punches, kicks, knees, elbows, sweeps and the clinch; its ancient precursor is Muay Boran (International Federation of Muaythai Associations, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Amateur muaythai is governed by the International Federation of Muaythai Associations (IFMA), founded in Bangkok in 1993 and granted full IOC recognition on 20 July 2021 at the 138th IOC Session in Tokyo, while the professional side is coordinated by the World Muaythai Council (WMC), established by the Thai government in 1995 (International Federation of Muaythai Associations, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Professional bouts are staged in Bangkok's legendary stadiums — Rajadamnern, opened 1945, and Lumpinee, opened 8 December 1956 — and worldwide through promotions such as ONE Championship, founded on 14 July 2011 by Chatri Sityodtong and Victor Cui, which holds weekly Friday Fights at Lumpinee (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; ONE Championship, n.d.). Muaythai has been on the World Games programme, where Austria's Stella Hemetsberger won gold at Birmingham 2022 and the sport returned at Chengdu 2025 (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). At the end of the 2026 season the leading women in the sport are ONE strawweight Muay Thai champion Stella Hemetsberger of Austria — who won the vacant title unanimously over Jackie Buntan at ONE Fight Night 35 on 6 September 2025 — plus Sweden's Smilla Sundell, who at 17 became ONE's youngest-ever world champion in January 2023, Allycia Rodrigues, who dethroned Stamp Fairtex in August 2020, and Stamp Fairtex herself, ONE's first three-sport world champion; Somratsamee Manopgym was the first woman to win a Rajadamnern Stadium title (ONE Championship, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

Rules

Under the IFMA Rules and Regulations (v3.057, 2026), elite amateur bouts consist of three rounds of three minutes with one-minute rests, while professional stadium and world-title bouts run five rounds of three minutes with one-minute rests; other formats from two two-minute rounds up to five three-minute rounds apply at junior and development levels (International Federation of Muaythai Associations, n.d.-b). Fighters wear the mongkol headband and perform the wai kru ram muay pre-fight ritual, and in amateur bouts also wear head guards, shin guards and groin protection alongside gloves. Legal techniques are the eight limbs — fists, elbows, knees and feet — plus sweeps and the clinch; fouls include biting, eye gouging, groin strikes, headbutts, attacking a downed opponent and holding the ropes, and bring warnings, point deductions or disqualification (International Federation of Muaythai Associations, n.d.-b). Bouts are scored round by round by judges, and a fighter wins by knockout, technical knockout, decision or disqualification of the opponent; weight classes and weigh-ins apply at all sanctioned events. IFMA sanctions several disciplines — Muaythai Combat (full contact), Technical (semi-contact), Mae Mai Muaythai (classical forms), Muay Boran/Kad Chuek, Krabi Krabong and beach and beam muay — and women compete in dedicated women's divisions at every level, from juniors to the elite world championships (International Federation of Muaythai Associations, n.d.-a).

History

Muay thai's documented history runs from the warrior training of the Ayutthaya period and the regional schools of Korat, Lopburi, Chaiya and Thasao through the first permanent ring in 1921 and the introduction of gloves in 1929, to the opening of Rajadamnern Stadium in 1945 and Lumpinee in 1956, which made stadium muay a national institution (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The IFMA was inaugurated in Bangkok in 1993 and now counts more than 128 member federations, the WMC was created in 1995 for the professional side, muaythai became a SEA Games medal sport and joined the World Games programme, and the IOC granted IFMA full recognition on 20 July 2021 (International Federation of Muaythai Associations, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-c). The legendary icon of the art is Nai Khanomtom, whose 1774 victory over the Burmese champion is commemorated every 17 March as Muay Thai Day, alongside ring legends such as Dieselnoi Chor Thanasukarn, the undefeated 1980s phenomenon, and Samart Payakaroon, the only fighter to win both Lumpinee and Rajadamnern titles at the highest weight (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
