Benchrest Shooting

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Benchrest shooting is the precision rifle discipline in which the firearm is fired from a solid bench rest, and competitors are judged on the size of their shot groups at 100–200 yards (or metres) — the smallest group wins (Wikipedia, n.d.). It belongs to the long-range and precision side of the target-shooting family, alongside F-Class and silhouette shooting.

Figure: PretoriaTravel. (2012-06-17). Benchrest rifle with front rest on the firing bench [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. CC BY-SA 3.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Benchrest_mauser_rifle.jpg

The international body is the World Benchrest Shooting Federation (WBSF), which runs world championships in classes such as Light Varmint, Heavy Varmint and Hunter; in the United States the International Benchrest Shooters (IBS) is the major organization (Wikipedia, n.d.). A standard match is five 5-shot groups per card, and the group size is measured in thousandths of an inch between the two most widely separated shots; aggregates (group average, match score or grand aggregate over several distances) decide the winner (WBSF, n.d.).

Rules

Rifles are built for the discipline — heavy barrels, target stocks with adjustable front rests and rear bags, high-power scopes; ammunition is handloaded for extreme consistency (Wikipedia, n.d.). Shooting is from the prone or seated position behind the bench, one shooter per relay, with commands given by the range officer (WBSF, n.d.). Calibres are typically 6 mm and .308-class cartridges; rimfire benchrest exists at 50 yards/metres (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men and women compete together in the same matches — there are no separate gender classes — and junior categories exist at national level.

History

Benchrest shooting as a scored sport developed in the United States in the mid-20th century, when shooters at the Harrisburg, Pennsylvania area began competing for group size rather than bullseye score; the IBS was founded in the 1950s and the WBSF later created a world championship structure (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Norwegian discipline of 500 m benchrest — a crossover toward long range — has been shot since 2007 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Today world championships rotate between continents and the sport remains strongest in the US, Australia, South Africa and Scandinavia.
