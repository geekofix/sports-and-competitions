Tricklining

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Tricklining is the acrobatic branch of slacklining: athletes bounce on a short, highly tensioned 2-inch (5 cm) polyester webbing rigged low between anchors — typically 15–30 m long at about 1 m off the ground over padding — and perform flips, rotations and bounce tricks such as the butt bounce and chest bounce families at height (International Slackline Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Freestyle trickline competitions are judged on five criteria — difficulty, technique, diversity, amplitude and performance — in solo runs and battles, with contests graded Masters, World Cup and World Championship by the International Slackline Association (ISA), which maintains the ISA Slackline World Ranking List (International Slackline Association, n.d.). Women and men compete in the same classes, and the 2025 ISA trickline ranking titles went to Japan's Kanon Ito in the women's category — first at the Trickline World Cups in Munich and Stuttgart, the 13th Nippon Open and the Gibbon Cups — and to Rikuto Nakamura in the men's category, his fourth consecutive yearly title (2022–2025) (International Slackline Association, n.d.). Commercial kit maker Gibbon Slacklines runs the Gibbon World Cup series of trickline events, which began in 2011 (Gibbon Slacklines, n.d.).

Rules

A trickline is a 2-inch (5 cm) wide polyester webbing, 15–30 m long, tensioned very tight and rigged at roughly 1 m height over crash padding or airbags, with anchors and backups following ISA safety standards (International Slackline Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). In competition, athletes perform runs of set length — bouncing the line and throwing tricks from the gymnastics, skate and parkour vocabulary (chest and butt bounces, rotations, corkscrew and gainer-style moves off the line) — and judges score difficulty, technique, diversity, amplitude and performance, with deductions for falls or uncontrolled landings; solo finals and head-to-head battles are both used (International Slackline Association, n.d.). ISA-recognized contests are graded Masters, World Cup and World Championship, and results feed the ISA World Ranking List, whose yearly winners are crowned per discipline (International Slackline Association, n.d.). Safety rules require padded landings, inspected anchors and line backups, and venue supervision (International Slackline Association, n.d.). Women and men compete in the same classes, line specs and judging criteria — there are no separate technical rules by gender (International Slackline Association, n.d.).

History

Slacklining's roots lie in Yosemite Valley, where climbers in the 1960s and 1970s passed rest days balancing on parking-lot chains, handrails and ropes at Camp 4; in the early 1980s Adam Grosowsky and Jeff Ellington transferred the idea to flat tubular climbing webbing, walking it at the climbers' camps from about 1979–1983 (International Slackline Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Scott Balcom made the first highline walk on webbing across Yosemite's Lost Arrow Spire on 13 July 1985 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Through the 1990s and 2000s the activity spread worldwide, and in 2007 Robert and Jan Kaeding founded Gibbon in Germany, producing the first simplified slackline kits and starting the first slacklining company (Gibbon Slacklines, n.d.). Tricklining crystallized as a distinct discipline in the 2000s, as short, high-tension lines turned walking lines into bouncing platforms; one of the first tricklining competitions was hosted in Humboldt County, California, in 2008 by Andy Lewis, who became the first World Tricklining Champion that year and later performed with Madonna at Super Bowl XLVI's halftime show in 2012 (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Gibbon World Cup has run trickline contests since 2011, and the International Slackline Association, founded in 2015 and based in Bern, Switzerland, now grades international contests and publishes the annual ranking list winners (International Slackline Association, n.d.).
