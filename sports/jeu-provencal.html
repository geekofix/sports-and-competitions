Jeu Provençal

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Jeu provençal — also called la longue or les trois pas — is the historic Provençal boules game from which pétanque developed: instead of throwing from a standing circle, players take a short running approach and release the boule from behind a fixed line (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It is governed together with pétanque by the Fédération Internationale de Pétanque et Jeu Provençal (FIPJP), which organizes world championships, and the official rules in force since 1 January 2021 were confirmed unchanged for 2026 (Fédération Internationale de Pétanque et Jeu Provençal, n.d.). Games are played on a terrain 15–20 m long — twice as long as a pétanque terrain — in triples with two boules per player, to 13 points (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The boules are hollow steel, 70.5–80 mm in diameter, and the jack (but) is 25–35 mm (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In 2026 the leading nations are France, Madagascar, Thailand and Cambodia, with the women's world championship contested on the same rules and the reigning women's world champions from Madagascar among the top sides (Fédération Internationale de Pétanque et Jeu Provençal, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Under the FIPJP rules, a jeu provençal terrain is 15–20 m long and at least 4 m wide, marked with a throwing line (the donné) at one end and a back line; the jack is thrown from the back line to between 6 and 10 m (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The defining feature is the run-up: the player may take up to three steps of approach and must release the boule from behind the throwing line, with the feet not crossing it at the moment of release (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Games are played in triples, each player with two boules, to 13 points; singles and doubles are also played with three boules each (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Boules are hollow steel, 70.5–80 mm diameter and 650–800 g; the jack is 25–35 mm and 10–18 g (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Scoring follows pétanque rules: after all boules are played, only the team with the closest boule scores, one point per boule closer than the opponent's nearest (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Fouls — releasing over the line, moving the jack, or playing the wrong boule — cost a point or the annulment of the throw (Fédération Internationale de Pétanque et Jeu Provençal, n.d.). Men and women play under identical rules in separate competitions, and the women's world championship has been held since the 1980s (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

History

Boules games have been played in Provence since the Middle Ages, and the running-approach form — la longue — was the dominant competitive variant in the 19th and early 20th centuries, documented in postcards from Annot (1909), Cannes and Avignon (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Pétanque was invented in 1910 in La Ciotat by Ernest Pitiot, who, unable to run up because of his rheumatism, played from a standing circle with the feet together — the direct ancestor of modern pétanque — and the FIPJP, founded in the 1940s, later governed both games (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Jeu provençal remains the competitive discipline in the south of France, especially in Provence and in pétanque's international strongholds Madagascar, Thailand and Cambodia, and the FIPJP world championships include the la longue discipline alongside pétanque (Fédération Internationale de Pétanque et Jeu Provençal, n.d.). Icons of the game are the great boulistes of the early-20th-century concours, immortalized on the postcards of the era, and the modern world champions of the la longue discipline (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
