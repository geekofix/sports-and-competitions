Go

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information

Go (Chinese weiqi, Korean baduk) is a two-player abstract strategy board game in which black and white stones are placed alternately on the intersections of a 19 × 19 grid (361 points; smaller 13 × 13 and 9 × 9 boards are used for beginners and faster games) to surround more territory than the opponent; it was invented in China more than 2,500 years ago and is the oldest board game still played, with over 46 million people in the 75 member nations of the International Go Federation (IGF) knowing how to play and over 20 million current players (International Go Federation [IGF], n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Stones are captured when a group is surrounded on all orthogonally adjacent points, and the game ends when neither player wishes to move; the score is territory plus captured stones (Japanese/Korean rules) or area — stones plus surrounded territory (Chinese/AGA rules) — plus komi, a compensation of 6.5 points (Japanese/Korean) or 7.5 (Chinese, Ing, AGA) added to White for moving second (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Handicap games give the weaker player black stones placed on the star points (2–9 stones, about one per rank of difference) with komi of 0.5 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Professional players hold kyu and dan ranks awarded by national professional associations (Nihon Ki-in, Korean Baduk Association, Chinese Weiqi Association); the IGF organizes the World Amateur Go Championship and the World Mind Sports Games (IGF, n.d.-a). In 2026 the world's leading professional is Shin Jin-seo of South Korea, who won the 3rd Quzhou-Lanke Cup in 2026 and the 30th LG Cup (2–1 over Ichiriki Ryo), while Shin Min-jun won the 30th LG Cup as well in domestic standings — the major 2026 titles went to Shin Jin-seo and contemporaries such as Ding Hao (IGF, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-c) (International Go Federation, n.d.-a) (International Go Federation, n.d.-b).

At the end of 2026 the leading women are the top female professionals of South Korea, China and Japan — the women's professional ranks reach 9 dan, and stars such as Choi Jeong of Korea and the Chinese women's professionals head the women's world rankings maintained by GoRatings (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). On the men's side, Shin Jin-seo (9 dan, born 2000) is ranked world number one, ahead of Ke Jie, Ding Hao and Park Jung-hwan (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

Rules

The rules of Go are minimal: players alternate placing one stone of their colour on an empty intersection; a stone or connected group with no adjacent empty point (liberty) is captured and removed; the ko rule forbids recreating the immediately previous board position (situational superko in Chinese/AGA rules forbids any repetition); suicide moves are illegal under Chinese/AGA rules; both players may pass, and two consecutive passes end the game, after which dead stones are removed by agreement and the score is counted (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Territory scoring (Japanese/Korean) counts surrounded empty points plus prisoners, with komi 6.5; area scoring (Chinese/AGA) counts live stones on the board plus surrounded territory, with komi 7.5 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Handicap stones are placed on the star points (up to nine), and the stronger player takes white; in a handicap game komi is 0.5 so white wins ties (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Tournament games use time controls (byo-yomi in Japanese rules) and komi as set by the organizer; the IGF recommends 6.5 or 7.5 komi for even games (IGF, n.d.-a). There are no separate rules for men and women — all play the same board and rules — and age categories (youth, senior, veterans) exist in amateur championships (IGF, n.d.-a).

History

Go was invented in China more than 2,500 years ago — the earliest written reference is the Zuo Zhuan annal (c. 4th century BCE) — and was one of the four essential arts of the cultured Chinese scholar; the 19 × 19 board became standard after the game reached Korea in the 5th century CE and Japan in the 7th century CE, where it became go (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In Japan, the Edo-period (1603–1868) go-dokoro (government Go office) professional system — with the four great houses (Hon'inbō, Yasui, Inoue, Hayashi) and the annual castle games before the shogun — produced legendary players such as Hon'inbō Shūsaku (1829–1862), who went 19–0 in castle games, and the 20th-century rivalry of Go Seigen and Kitani Minoru; the Nihon Ki-in was founded in 1924 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In Korea, Cho Hun-hyun (born 1953) won the first Ing Cup in 1988, and in China the 1990s–2000s rise produced Ke Jie (born 1997), world number one from 2014 to 2017 (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). The computer era began in March 2016, when Google DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol 4–1 — Lee's only win came in game four with the famous "divine move" White 78 — and in July 2026 Shin Jin-seo became the first professional to beat the elite engine KataGo in a match series, defeating it by 4.5 points (Wikipedia, n.d.-c; KED Global, n.d.).
