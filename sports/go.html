Go

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Go is an ancient abstract strategy board game for two players, played on a 19x19 grid (smaller 13x13 and 9x9 boards for beginners), in which players alternately place black and white stones to surround territory and capture enemy stones. It is governed internationally by the International Go Federation (IGF), which organizes the World Amateur Go Championship and recognizes professional ranks (dan) awarded by the professional associations of China (CBA), Japan (Nihon Ki-in), Korea (KBaduk) and Taiwan.

Rules

Under the official Rules of Go (Japanese/Nihon Ki-in rules, used internationally), players alternate placing one stone on an empty intersection; stones of a group with no liberties (adjacent empty points) are captured and removed. A player may pass; two consecutive passes end the game. Suicide moves (playing into a point with no liberties unless capturing) are illegal under Japanese rules but allowed under Chinese rules (which are used at the World Amateur Championship). Ko (repetition) is prohibited: a play may not recreate the previous board position. After the game, dead stones are removed by agreement and territory is counted (area scoring under Chinese rules, territory scoring under Japanese rules); komi (compensation points, typically 6.5-7.5) is added to White to balance Black's first-move advantage.

History

Go originated in China over 4,000 years ago (legend dates it to Emperor Yao, c. 2300 BC) and spread to Korea and Japan, where it became a pillar of aristocratic culture; in Japan it was professionalized in the Edo period (1603-1868) with the four great Go houses (Honinbo, Yasui, Inoue, Hayashi) patronized by the shogunate. The Nihon Ki-in (Japan Go Association) was founded in 1924, the Korean Baduk Association in 1954, and the Chinese Weiqi Association in 1952. The International Go Federation was founded in 1982 in Tokyo. In 2016, the Korean professional Lee Sedol lost a landmark match to Google DeepMind's AlphaGo, bringing AI into the game; AlphaGo's successor AlphaZero (2017) mastered the game from self-play alone. Today top professionals like Shin Jinseo (Korea) and Ke Jie (China) compete for world titles.
