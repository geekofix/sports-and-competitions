Canal Vaulting

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Canal vaulting — Dutch fierljeppen, West Frisian for "far leaping", also known as polsstokverspringen ("pole jumping far") — is the traditional Dutch sport of crossing a canal or ditch with a long pole. The jumper sprints along the bank, plants a pole 8–13 m (26–43 ft) long — now made of carbon fiber, formerly wood or aluminum — with a flat round plate at the bottom to keep it from sinking into the mud, climbs the pole while controlling its movement over the water, and lands on a sand bed on the far side; the distance from take-off to landing is measured (Polsstokbond Holland, n.d.). The sport is governed in the Netherlands by the Polsstokbond Holland (PB Holland), which organizes the annual Nationale Fierljepping Manifestatie (NFM) and the Dutch Championship (NK); the NK 2026 was held in Vlist (Polsstokbond Holland, n.d.). Current Dutch record holders by category include Jaco de Groot (men's senior, 22.21 m, Zegveld, 12 August 2017), Marrit van der Wal (women, 18.19 m, Burgum, 31 July 2019), Germ Terpstra (juniors under 20, 21.87 m, Jaarsveld, 5 July 2025), Thewis Hobma (veterans, 20.70 m, Burgum, 21 June 2025), Wisse Broekstra (boys under 16, 20.72 m, 2020), Hanneke Westert (girls under 16, 17.63 m, 2022) and Alan Barth of Canada (foreigners, 19.64 m, 2002); about 532 jumpers are registered worldwide, 190 of them in the Netherlands (Wikipedia, n.d.). Women compete in their own categories — the "dame-klimmen" (ladies' climbing) class — under the same rules as men (Polsstokbond Holland, n.d.).

Rules

In competition, each jumper gets a set number of attempts (usually three) on a marked course: a straight waterway with a take-off bank, the pole plant area and a sand landing bed. The measured distance runs from the take-off edge to the point where the jumper first touches the sand; a jump is only valid if the jumper releases the pole correctly and lands on the bed without falling into the water beforehand — a "plons" (splash) scores zero for that attempt (Polsstokbond Holland, n.d.). Poles are classed by length and stiffness; the current carbon poles are 8–13 m with a flat plate at the base, and in the separate klimmen (climbing) class jumpers try to reach the highest point on the pole. Categories include men's and women's senior, veterans, juniors (under 20), boys and girls (under 16), and a foreigners' class; the NFM and NK award titles per class, and records are kept per category (Polsstokbond Holland, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Fouls include losing the pole before take-off, stepping over the take-off line, or touching the water with the body before the measured landing. The related East Frisian variant is called Pultstockspringen (Wikipedia, n.d.).

History

Frisian farmers used poles to cross the countless drainage ditches of the lowlands, and the practical custom evolved into a sport; the first official match is recorded in 1771, though the sport was not properly structured until 1957, and it took its modern competitive form in the 1950s and 1960s according to fierljeppen historian Wim Roskam. The Polsstokbond Holland was founded in the 20th century to organize the sport, which today is contested in six leagues and numerous clubs, with the NFM as the national showpiece (Wikipedia, n.d.). The sport spread with Frisian emigrants and is now practiced in the Netherlands (especially Friesland and Utrecht, home of record holders Jaco de Groot and Theo van Kooten), and at lower levels wherever visitors tried it — Americans first encountered it as "ditch vaulting" on The Amazing Race (Wikipedia, n.d.).
