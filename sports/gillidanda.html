Gillidanda

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Gilli danda (gillidanda) is the traditional bat-and-ball street game of South Asia: a batter flicks a small tapered stick (gilli) into the air off a stone and hits it with a long stick (danda), and the fielding side tries to catch it or hit the danda with a thrown gilli. Known by many names — tipcat in English, viti dandu in Marathi, danguli in Bengali, kitti pul in Tamil — it is played across India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bangladesh and beyond, and a Gilli Danda International Federation (headquartered in Noida, India, since 2016) is working on a unified rulebook.

Rules

The batter balances the gilli on a stone so one end is raised, flicks it into the air with the danda, and then strikes it as far as possible while airborne. If the gilli is caught before touching the ground, the batter is out; if it lands, the nearest fielder may throw the gilli to hit the danda lying across the batting circle (a "run out" in cricket terms) — a hit means the batter is out, a miss means the batter scores a point and bats again. Three consecutive misses (fails to hit the gilli) is a strikeout-style dismissal. Scoring can be measured in danda-lengths of distance. There are no fixed dimensions or player numbers, making it an impromptu street game.

History

Gilli danda is among the oldest known bat-and-ball games, with origins traced to the Maurya period in India (around 2,500 years ago), and it is often described as a possible ancestor of cricket, baseball and softball. It is referenced in literature — Premchand's short story "Gilli-danda" and the film Lagaan both feature it — and the 1934 Laurel & Hardy film Babes in Toyland shows its Western cousin "Pee Wee." The Traditional Games Federation of India and the Traditional Sports & Games Pakistan Association organize tournaments and publish rulebooks, and the 2014 Marathi film Vitti Dandu and 2021 Nepali film Damaru Ko Dandibiyo celebrate the game on screen.
