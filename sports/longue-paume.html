Longue Paume

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Longue paume ("long palm game") is the outdoor version of jeu de paume and a direct ancestor of modern lawn tennis: played without a net on a long field, with a long-handled racket and a light cork ball covered in flannel. It has been played in France for several centuries, is still played in the Picardy region, and was included in the program of the 1900 Paris Olympic Games (its medal status is disputed). The sport is governed by the Fédération Française de Longue Paume, headquartered in Amiens.

Rules

Longue paume is played 1 v 1, 2 v 2 or 4 v 4 on an open field of variable length, divided by a center line (the "corde") that acts as the net. Players use a racket with a long handle and a small stringed head (tamis) to hit a light ball of 15-20 g, and the game is scored like tennis — 15, 30, 40, advantage — with a match played over five sets. A distinctive feature is the "chase": a line marked on the field, and when the ball is hit beyond it (or, in defense, cannot be reached), the chase moves and play restarts from there, changing sides when two chases are made. Players may stop the ball after its second bounce to mark the chase.

History

Like jeu de paume, longue paume was played in France from the Middle Ages and flourished in the 17th-18th centuries, especially in Paris and Picardy; a competition was held in the Jardin du Luxembourg in 1889. It was included at the 1900 Paris Olympics, where the French team won, though the medals are officially disputed. The Fédération Française de Longue Paume, based in Amiens, governs the modern game, which is still played competitively in Picardy and at the Olympics centenary commemorations; the sport directly influenced the development of lawn tennis in England in the 1870s.
