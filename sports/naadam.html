Naadam

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Naadam (Mongolian for “games”), locally called eriin gurvan naadam — “the three games of men” — is Mongolia's traditional national festival, centered on Mongolian wrestling (bokh), horse racing and archery, with ankle-bone shooting (shagai) added as a fourth contest. The national Naadam in Ulaanbaatar ran 11–13 July 2026 (public holiday 11–15 July), with the opening ceremony at the National Sports Stadium, wrestling at the stadium, horse races at Khui Doloon Khudag about 30 km west of the city, and archery at the archery field beside the stadium; smaller aimag and sum Naadams are held across the country in July. UNESCO inscribed Naadam on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010. Women now compete in horse racing, archery and shagai shooting, but not in wrestling. Within the Central Asian nomad games on this site, its closest relatives are Mongolian Horse Racing, Mongolian Archery, Shagai, Bökh; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

Wrestling: 512 (or 1,024 in anniversary years) wrestlers meet in a single-elimination tournament of nine or ten rounds at the National Sports Stadium; a bout is untimed and is lost when any body part other than feet or hands touches the ground; wrestlers wear the zodog (shoulder vest) and shuudag (shorts), and winners earn lifelong titles from falcon (nachin) through hawk, elephant, garuda and lion (arslan) to grand champion (avraga). Horse racing: cross-country races of 15–30 km whose length depends on the horse's age class (for example about 16 km for two-year-olds and up to 27–30 km for older horses), ridden by child jockeys aged roughly 5–13; up to about 1,000 horses can start, the top five earn the title airgiyn tav, the winning jockey is praised as tumny ekh, and the last horse (bayan khodood) is honored with a song. Archery: Khalkh-style archers shoot blunt arrows at a khashaa target — a low wall of small leather cylinders — from about 75 m for men (40 arrows) and 60–65 m for women (20 arrows); Buriad style (30–45 m, mixed) and Uriankhai style (30–40 m, men only) are also contested, and judges sing the uukhai praise call when an arrow strikes home. Shagai shooting: teams flick ankle bones at a target bone about 10 m away over a khashlaga rail.

History

The three games are recorded in the 13th-century Secret History of the Mongols, and Naadam grew out of the military parades and contests that followed weddings and spiritual gatherings; it also served to train soldiers for battle. It became an annual festival in 1639 at Shireet White Lake, where Bokh Lama won the wrestling and Bonkhor Donir's horse won the racing; the 1772 Festival of Ten Governments worshipped Khentii mountain, and in 1912 the Ten Government Games became an annual state game held 125 times until the death of Bogd Khan in 1925. The modern national holiday Naadam commemorates the 1921 revolution. Archery feats are legendary: the Chinggis Khan Stone inscription records that in 1225 the archer Esukhei (Yisünge) hit a target from 335 ald — over half a kilometre. Competitive archery was revived with the 1922 army Naadam and the 1924 Buriat sur-kharvaa games, and women began competing in the 1960s. UNESCO inscribed Naadam in 2010.
