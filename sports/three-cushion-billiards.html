Three-Cushion Billiards

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Three-cushion billiards is the expert discipline of carom billiards: played on a pocketless table with three balls (one red, two white), the shooter must contact both object balls with the cue ball so that the cue ball or the struck ball touches at least three cushions before the second object ball is hit. One of the most difficult precision games ever devised, its world championships rank among cue sport's most prestigious titles.

Rules

A point (count) is scored when the cue ball contacts the first object ball, then at least three cushions anywhere on the table, and then the second object ball, all in one stroke; misses leave the balls where they lie and the inning passes. The "natural" (cuing the red first off two long rails for a three-cushion path) is one classic path, but professional play draws from a vast shot library of rails, systems and spin; games are played to a set number of points (commonly 40 to 50 in tournament play) with players alternating innings, and the diamond system of table markings anchors the most-used aiming mathematics. Rules govern break shots, jump and mass fouls, and the use of the cue's ferrule, with international federations hosting world championships in singles, team and doubles formats.

History

Carom billiards grew out of the cushioned tables of revolutionary-era Europe, and by the 1870s players were counting caroms without cushions in "straight rail"; three-cushion emerged as the master variant in the early twentieth century, championed as the ultimate test of angle and spin. Its world championships began in the 1950s, with European and world federations organizing the title, national scenes flourishing in the Low Countries, Turkey, Spain, the Americas and East Asia, and the great champions' averages rising from under one point per inning to nearly two. The modern game, with international circuits and the World Games on its calendar, remains the connoisseur's cue sport: fewer points per match than any pocket game, but each one a geometry problem solved at high speed.
