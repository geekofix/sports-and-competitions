Field Hockey

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Field hockey is a team sport played with hooked sticks on a water-based or sand-based artificial turf pitch: two teams of eleven players (ten outfield players plus a goalkeeper) try to hit a hard plastic ball into the opponent's goal, defended by a goalkeeper in full protective kit (FIH, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.). It is governed worldwide by the International Hockey Federation (FIH), founded on 7 January 1924 in Paris by seven founding nations and headquartered in Lausanne, Switzerland, with more than 130 member associations; the FIH organizes the Hockey World Cup and the Olympic hockey tournament, and the sport has been on the Olympic program for men since London 1908 and for women since Moscow 1980 (FIH, n.d.-a; Olympics.com, n.d.). The 2026 FIH Hockey World Cup in Belgium and the Netherlands (15–30 August 2026) was the first edition to stage the men's and women's tournaments jointly, with 16 teams each, alongside the first Para Hockey World Cup (FIH, n.d.-a). See also Ice Hockey, Hockey5s and Bandy on this site (Fédération Internationale de Hockey, n.d.-a) (Fédération Internationale de Hockey, n.d.-b).

At the 2026 World Cup, the leading women were Argentina's Las Leonas: they beat co-hosts the Netherlands 1–1 (3–1 on penalty shootout) in the final at Wagener Stadium, Amstelveen, on 29 August 2026, with captain María José Granatto scoring the equalizer and goalkeeper Cristina Cosentino named player of the match after saving in the shootout — Argentina's third world title after 2002 and 2010; among the Dutch stars was Yibbi Jansen (FIH, n.d.-a). On the men's side, Germany beat Spain 1–0 in the final in Wavre on 30 August 2026, with Michel Struthoff scoring, to claim a record fourth men's title (FIH, n.d.-a).

Rules

The pitch is 91.4 m × 55 m with a goal 3.66 m wide and 2.14 m high at each end; matches at FIH elite level are played in four 15-minute quarters with breaks after the first and third quarters, and each quarter is prolonged to complete a goal, penalty corner or penalty stroke (FIH, n.d.-b). A goal counts only if the ball was played by an attacker inside the shooting circle (FIH, n.d.-b). Each team fields ten outfield players and one goalkeeper; the goalkeeper wears helmet, kickers, chest and leg guards and may use any body part inside the shooting circle (the "D", a semicircle of 14.63 m radius) while outfield players may not enter it except to play the ball, and may not play the ball with the foot or the back of the stick (FIH, n.d.-b). Penalty corners are awarded for fouls inside the circle or for dangerous play, and are taken as a push or flick from a marked spot on the back line, with at most five attackers including the goalkeeper inside the circle and the first hit at goal required to cross the goal line below backboard height (460 mm); penalty strokes are taken from a spot 6.4 m in front of the goal with only the goalkeeper defending (FIH, n.d.-b). Free hits restart play after fouls outside the circles, and the game is restarted by hit-ins, long corners and 16-yard hits after the ball crosses the back or side lines (FIH, n.d.-b). Fouls are sanctioned with free hits, penalty corners and strokes, and with green, yellow (5–10 minute suspensions) and red cards (permanent exclusion); umpires may also use video referrals (FIH, n.d.-b). Men and women play under identical rules; the women's tournament joined the Olympics in 1980, and the FIH also runs junior (under-21 and under-18) World Cups, indoor hockey, Hockey5s and Para hockey, each with adapted rules (FIH, n.d.-a). Players use a composite stick with a flat playing side and a rounded back, and wear shin guards and mouthguards; the ball is a hard dimpled sphere of about 156–163 g (FIH, n.d.-b).

History

The modern game was codified in England in the 1870s–1880s — the Hockey Association was founded in London in 1886 — and spread through the British Empire, with India and Pakistan becoming the dominant nations of the early Olympic era (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Indian wizard Dhyan Chand (1905–1979) led his country to Olympic gold medals in 1928, 1932 and 1936 and scored more than 400 goals in his career, remaining the most iconic player in the sport's history (Wikipedia, n.d.). Field hockey debuted for men at London 1908 and for women at Moscow 1980; the Netherlands, Australia, Germany, India and Pakistan have been the most successful nations at World Cups and Olympic Games (Olympics.com, n.d.). On the women's side, the Argentine Luciana Aymar (b. 1977, retired 2010), eight-time FIH Player of the Year, is the most decorated player of the modern era, and the Netherlands' women — three-time Olympic champions — set the standard from the 1980s onward, with the 2026 title going back to Argentina after 16 years (FIH, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.). The FIH was founded on 7 January 1924 in Paris by Belgium, Denmark, France, the Netherlands, Austria, Spain and Switzerland, and has been headquartered in Lausanne since 1970; its long-serving president Tayyab Ikram (Pakistan) guided the federation into the professional era with the FIH Pro League launched in 2012 (Wikipedia, n.d.).
