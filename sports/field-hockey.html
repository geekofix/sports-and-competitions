Field Hockey

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Field hockey is a team sport played on a 91.4 m x 55 m grass or artificial turf pitch, in which two teams of eleven use hooked sticks to hit a hard ball into the opponent's 2.14 m wide goal. It is governed by the International Hockey Federation (FIH), which runs the Hockey World Cup (men and women) and the FIH Hockey Pro League, and it is an Olympic sport since London 1908 (men) and 1980 (women).

Rules

Under the FIH Rules of Hockey (current edition effective 2026), a match is four quarters of 15 minutes (or two halves in some competitions). Each team has 11 players including a goalkeeper who may wear protective gear. The ball may only be played with the flat side of the stick; using the rounded side, the feet (except for goalkeepers), or raising the stick above shoulder height is a foul. Goals are scored from the 16-yard (14.63 m) circle ("the D"). Penalty corners are awarded for fouls in the circle or deliberate fouls outside it, and penalty strokes for fouls preventing a certain goal. Free hits restart play. Since 2015, self-start and self-pass rules allow quick restarts. Video umpire referrals are used at major events. Olympic and World Cup formats use pool stages followed by knockouts; shootouts decide drawn knockout matches.

History

Modern field hockey was codified in England in the 1870s-1880s, with the Hockey Association founded in 1886 in London; the first international match was England vs. Ireland in 1895. The FIH was founded in 1924 in Paris by seven nations. Hockey appeared at the 1908 and 1920 Olympics and has been contested at every Games since 1928. India and Pakistan dominated the men's game in the mid-20th century (India won 8 Olympic golds, 1928-1960 unbeaten streak with Dhyan Chand). The Netherlands and Australia dominate the modern women's and men's games; artificial turf replaced grass at the 1976 Montreal Olympics. The FIH moved to its current domain fih.hockey in 2023 and publishes the Rules of Hockey, updated every four years (2026 edition).
