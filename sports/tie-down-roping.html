Tie-Down Roping

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Tie-down roping (formerly calf roping) is the rodeo timed event in which a mounted roper catches a running calf around the neck with a lasso, dismounts, runs down the rope, throws the calf by hand and ties any three legs together with a pigging string: when the roper throws his hands up, the clock stops, and the fastest legal time wins. A classic solo test of horsemanship, roping skill and athleticism from the ranching traditions of the American West.

Rules

The calf is released from the chute with a head start protected by a barrier; breaking the barrier adds ten seconds. The roper throws the loop, dallies the rope around the saddle horn to snub the calf, dismounts while his trained horse keeps the rope taut, flanks the calf and ties three legs with the pigging string; the clock stops when the roper throws his hands up after the tie is complete, and the calf must stay tied for six seconds after the roper remounts and slack is given, or the run is disqualified. Rules govern legal catches, the barrier, tie requirements and animal welfare standards enforced by judges.

History

The event descends directly from ranch work, where a cowboy roped and tied a calf on the open range for branding or doctoring; the same skills entered early rodeo as calf roping, one of the standard events at professional rodeo's founding and a fixture ever since. The sport professionalized with the Rodeo Cowboys Association (1936) into today's PRCA, and tie-down roping remains one of the timed-event staples at the National Finals Rodeo, while the tie-down roper's horse (trained to keep the rope taut precisely while the roper works) is celebrated as the sport's great equine athlete. Champions come largely from the roping dynasties of the American Southwest, and the event's winning times have compressed to the seven-to-nine-second range at the elite level.
