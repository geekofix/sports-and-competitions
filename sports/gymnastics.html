Gymnastics

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Artistic gymnastics is the Olympic discipline in which gymnasts perform short choreographed routines on apparatus, combining strength, flexibility, balance and artistry; it is governed worldwide by the Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique (FIG), founded in 1881 in Liège as the European Gymnastics Federation (FEG) by Nicolas Cupérus with three founding nations and renamed FIG in 1921 (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique [FIG], n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men compete on six apparatus — floor exercise, pommel horse, still rings, vault, parallel bars and horizontal bar — and women on four — vault, uneven bars, balance beam and floor exercise — with team, all-around and apparatus events at the Olympic Games and the World Championships (FIG, n.d.-a). At the 54th Artistic Gymnastics World Championships in Rotterdam (Ahoy), 17–25 October 2026, the men's team title went to Japan for the third consecutive time (251.928) and the women's team title to China (165.674, 0.233 ahead of the United States), while Skye Blakely of the United States won the women's all-around final with 55.733 — the event also served as the first qualifier for the Los Angeles 2028 Olympic team competition (FIG, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b) (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-a) (Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, n.d.-b) (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

As of late 2026, the leading women in the sport are Skye Blakely, the 2026 world all-around champion, and the Olympic medallists Simone Biles (USA) and Rebeca Andrade (BRA) of the Paris 2024 podium, together with Sunisa Lee (USA), while on the men's side the 2026 world team champions of Japan lead the field (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Olympics, n.d.).

Rules

The official rulebook is the FIG Code of Points, renewed every Olympic cycle — the 2025–2028 edition applies at the 2026 World Championships; it defines the difficulty (D-score) and execution (E-score) evaluation, with the execution score starting from 10.0 and deductions taken by the judges, plus neutral deductions for time and behaviour, and it reduced the number of counting elements in men's routines from ten to eight and credits the dismount first from 1 January 2026 with a bonus for stuck landings (FIG, n.d.-a). Each routine is limited in time, vault and floor routines are accompanied by music, and in finals the team competition uses the 5–4–4 format (five gymnasts per team, four scores counting on each apparatus), the all-around final is decided by the total over all apparatus, and the vault final by the average of two vaults (FIG, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). At the Olympic Games and World Championships, qualification, team final, all-around final and apparatus finals are held, with the top qualifiers advancing (limited per nation); age categories distinguish senior and junior (U18) gymnasts, and men's and women's competitions use different apparatus sets as listed above (FIG, n.d.-a). The sport's rules and apparatus specifications are published by the FIG as the world federation (FIG, n.d.-a).

History

Modern artistic gymnastics was codified in the 19th century — Friedrich Ludwig Jahn's turnen in Germany and Swedish gymnastics shaped the apparatus — and the FIG was founded in 1881 in Liège; men's artistic gymnastics has been on the Olympic programme since the first modern Games in Athens 1896, women joined in Amsterdam 1928, and the first World Championships were held in Antwerp in 1903 for men, with women added in Budapest in 1934 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-c). Among the icons are Nadia Comăneci of Romania, who scored the first perfect 10.0 in Olympic history at Montreal 1976 and won five Olympic gold medals, Kohei Uchimura of Japan, who won the all-around at the 2012 and 2016 Olympics and is regarded as the greatest male gymnast of his era, and Simone Biles of the United States, the most decorated gymnast in World Championship history (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-c).
