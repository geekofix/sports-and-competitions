Shogi

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shogi — Japanese chess — is played on a 9×9 board with 20 pieces per side, and its defining rule is the drop: captured pieces change sides and may be placed back on any empty square, making shogi one of the most tactical board games in the world (JSA, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The professional game is governed by the Japan Shogi Association (JSA) and revolves around eight major open titles — Meijin, Ryūō, Ōshō, Ōza, Kisei, Kiō, Eiō and Ōi — plus a parallel women's professional system established in 1974 (JSA, n.d.). The women's game is currently led by Tomoka Nishiyama (born 1995), holder of the Women's Ōshō, Hakurei, Women's Meijin and Seirei titles, who on 26 October 2026 became the first woman ever admitted as a regular professional player after winning the Hakurei title for a fifth time, beating Kana Fukuma 4–1 in the best-of-seven series decided in Kanazawa on 10 October 2026; Fukuma remains the holder of the Joō title, while the men's game is led by Sōta Fujii (born 2002), the first eight-crown holder in 2023 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

Among today's top players, the women's professional field is headed by Tomoka Nishiyama — four women's titles and, since October 2026, the first female regular professional — and by Kana Fukuma, the Joō titleholder, while the men's game is dominated by Sōta Fujii, who became the youngest professional at 14 years 2 months in 2016, won 29 consecutive games as a rookie and became the youngest Meijin at 20 years 10 months (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

Rules

The board has 81 squares and each player starts with 20 pieces — king, rook, bishop, two gold generals, two silvers, two knights, two lances, nine pawns — which promote when entering, occupying or leaving the opponent's three promotion rows (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). A captured piece is held in hand and may be dropped on any empty square as one's own piece instead of moving, which produces the characteristic tactical motifs: uchifuzume (a pawn-drop mate is illegal and the game continues), sennichite (fourfold repetition is a draw) and jishōgi (impasse rules when both kings reach the opponent's camp) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Professional games use time controls with byo-yomi overtime, and the eight major titles are decided by best-of-five or best-of-seven matches between the holder and a challenger from the ranking leagues (JSA, n.d.). Women have had their own professional system since 1974, with eight women's major titles headed by the Women's Meijin, the oldest, and in 2026 Nishiyama became the first woman to enter the open professional ranks (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Amateur players hold kyu and dan ranks, and men and women may compete in the same open tournaments (JSA, n.d.).

History

Shogi descended from the Chinese game xiangqi, which reached Japan via Korea between the 6th and 8th centuries, and by the 16th century the modern hon-shogi with piece drops was established in Japan (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The Meijin title system began in 1937, and its first holder was Yoshio Kimura (1905–1986), who won the title eight times, became a Lifetime Meijin and served as the first president of the Japan Shogi Association in 1947–48 (Wikipedia, n.d.-d). Yoshiharu Habu (born 1970) became the first player in history to hold all seven titles simultaneously in 1996, set the all-time record of 81 major titles in 2012, and received Japan's People's Honour Award in 2018 before retiring from professional play in 2022 (Wikipedia, n.d.-d).
