Shogi

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shogi is Japanese chess, a two-player strategy board game played on a 9\u00d79 board with 20 pieces per side. Its defining feature — found in no other major chess variant at such depth — is the drop rule: captured pieces change allegiance and can be returned to the board almost anywhere as the capturer's own forces, so shogi is sometimes called "the chess with armies in reserve". It is the most popular board game in Japan, organised professionally under the Japan Shogi Association (JSA) through a title-match system whose champions are national celebrities.

Rules

Both players command the same twenty pieces: one king, one rook, one bishop, two gold generals, two silver generals, two knights, two lances and nine pawns. Pieces move in characteristic ways — the lance slides straight forward only, the knight jumps forward to either of two squares, the silver and gold generals step on different short-range patterns, while the rook and bishop are identical to their Western chess counterparts. Every piece except the gold general and the king can promote on entering, leaving or moving within the promotion zone of the opponent's three ranks, trading its original move for a stronger one. Unlike Western chess there is no castling, and the decisive innovation is the drop: captured pieces are held in reserve and, on a turn, may be dropped onto almost any empty square as one's own (with a few restrictions, such as no pawn drop giving immediate checkmate); drops make the endgame explosive and ensure that drawn games are rare.

History

Shogi descends, like Western chess, from the Indian game chaturanga, reaching Japan by way of China by around the eighth century; the modern 9\u00d79, 20-piece form was standardised in the sixteenth century, at about the time the drop rule was introduced, transforming an earlier large-board game into the dynamic modern form. In the Edo period the game was organised as an official court-supported pursuit under hereditary Meijin masters, and the modern Meijin championship — a tournament title rather than an inherited office — began in 1937, when Yoshio Kimura became its first holder. The Japan Shogi Association, founded in 1924 as the professional players' body, today runs eight major title matches: the Meijin (the oldest), the Ryuo (founded 1988, with the largest prize fund in shogi), the Osho, Oza, Kisei, Kio, Oi and Eio titles, alongside the NHK Cup tournament and a league pyramid of professional ranks. The post-war game was dominated by Yasuharu Oyama, who held many titles simultaneously for decades; in 1996 Yoshiharu Habu achieved the unprecedented feat of holding all seven major titles at once. In 2024 Sota Fujii, the youngest player ever to reach the top professional rank, became the first to hold all eight major titles simultaneously, the strongest single dominance in shogi history. Women professionals compete under their own title system, whose flagship is the Women's Meijin championship, founded in 1974.
