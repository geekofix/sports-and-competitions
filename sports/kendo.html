Kendo

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Kendo ("the way of the sword") is the modern Japanese martial art of the samurai's sword, practiced with a bamboo sword (shinai) and protective armor (bogu): a men (helmet with grill), kote (gloves), do (chest protector) and tare (thigh guards). Two kendoka face each other on a 9-12 m square floor and try to score ippon — a valid, decisive strike with the shinai to an allowed target — while three referees judge with flags. The All Japan Kendo Federation (AJKF), founded in 1952, governs the art in Japan, and the International Kendo Federation (FIK), founded in 1970, runs the World Kendo Championships.

Rules

Under AJKF/FIK rules, a valid strike (datotsu) must land with the striking part of the shinai on men (head), kote (wrists), do (sides of the trunk) or tsuki (throat thrust), delivered with ki-ken-tai-itchi — the unity of spirit, sword and body — and followed by zanshin (lingering awareness). An individual match is usually three minutes with the first two ippon winning (hantei, a referees' decision, if tied); team matches consist of five or seven bouts. Three referees raise white or red flags to award ippon, hantei or no-decision; waza-ari (minor valid technique) counts in some team formats. Kendo also includes kata performed with wooden swords (bokken), and ranks run from kyu up to 8 dan. Gripping the sword incorrectly, stepping out of the court or unsportsmanlike conduct bring penalties.

History

Kendo descends from the kenjutsu schools of the samurai. After the Meiji Restoration banned the wearing of swords in 1876, sword practice continued in police academies and private dojo using bamboo shinai; the protective bogu was developed around 1820 by Naganuma Shiroemon of the Hokushin Ittō-ryū school. The Dai Nippon Butoku Kai was founded in 1895 to promote martial arts, and after World War II the All Japan Kendo Federation was established in 1952. The FIK was founded in 1970 and the first World Kendo Championships were held the same year in Tokyo. Kendo is not an Olympic sport, but it is practiced in more than 50 countries and is valued for building discipline, etiquette and respect.
