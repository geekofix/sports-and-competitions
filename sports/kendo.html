Kendo

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Kendo is the Japanese art of fencing with the shinai (a split-bamboo sword) and protective bogu armor — men (helmet with grille), kote (gloves), do (chest protector) and tare — fought on a square shiai-jo competition area of about 11 × 11 m (International Kendo Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The four valid striking targets are the men (head), kote (wrists), do (trunk) and tsuki (throat); a point (ippon) requires a correct strike to a valid target with the striking part of the shinai (datotsu-bu), proper blade angle (hasuji), vigorous posture and follow-through spirit (zanshin), and is awarded when at least two of the three referees raise their flags (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The International Kendo Federation (FIK), founded in 1970 with 17 national federations and now 62 member countries, governs kendo internationally and holds the World Kendo Championships every three years — the 19th edition ran in Milan, Italy, from 4–7 July 2024 (International Kendo Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). At the top of the women's game, Kondo Mihiro of Japan won the women's individual title at the 2024 World Championships ahead of compatriots Suenaga Mari, Sato Minori and Senoo Maika, and South Korea's Jang Young-sook became the first non-Japanese woman to win the individual world title in 2018 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Kendo matches are governed by the FIK Regulations of Kendo Shiai and Shinpan and the AJKF match rules (International Kendo Federation, n.d.-a). A team (senshu) is typically five members (three in some formats) and individual bouts are fought under a set time limit — three minutes at World Championship level — with the competitor scoring two ippon first, or the higher score when time expires, winning (International Kendo Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). An ippon is valid only with the correct target, the correct part of the shinai, proper edge alignment and convincing spirit, confirmed by at least two of three shinpan (referees); two hansoku (major penalties) against a competitor award ippon to the opponent, and serious misconduct can disqualify immediately (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The shinai for men is 114–120 cm long and weighs at least 520 g (women's slightly shorter), and the bogu must be worn correctly; gripping the opponent, stepping out of the shiai-jo and other prohibited acts are penalized as hansoku (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Alongside shiai, competitors practice the ten Nihon Kendo Kata — paired forms with wooden swords first composed in 1912 and finalized in 1933 — which are performed and judged at championships (International Kendo Federation, n.d.-b). Ranks run from kyu grades up to dan, and the All Japan Kendo Federation no longer awards 9th and 10th dan (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Men and women compete in separate individual and team events under identical rules, with women's individual and team championships a full part of the World Championships since 1990 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Rule source: International Kendo Federation, https://www.kendo-fik.org/ (International Kendo Federation, n.d.-a).

History

Kendo descends from the kenjutsu schools of the samurai: from the 18th century onward, duels were fought with bamboo shinai and protective gear in Edo-period dojos (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). After the Meiji Restoration of 1868 and the Haitōrei edict of 1876 banning the carrying of swords, kendo was reorganized as a physical-education art; the first modern shinai-kyogi bouts were held in 1877, and kendo entered the police and schools in the 1880s–1890s (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). After a postwar ban, kendo returned to schools in 1950 as shinai-kyogi and the All Japan Kendo Federation (AJKF/ZNKR) was founded in 1952 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The International Kendo Federation was founded in 1970 and held the first World Kendo Championships in Tokyo the same year; the event has been held every three years since, with the 19th edition in Milan in July 2024, where Japan won every men's team title and Kondo Mihiro took the women's individual gold (International Kendo Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Icons of the modern art include the founders of the standardized kata and the generations of Japanese and Korean champions, notably Jang Young-sook, the first non-Japanese women's individual world champion (2018) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
