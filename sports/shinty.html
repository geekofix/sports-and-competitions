Shinty

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shinty (Scottish Gaelic: camanachd or iomain) is a Scottish team sport played with a curved stick called a caman and a small leather ball, in which two teams of twelve compete to drive the ball through the opponents' goal, known as the hail. Sometimes described as the fastest, most physical field game in the world, it is rooted in the Scottish Highlands and governed by the Camanachd Association, founded in 1893. It is a cousin of Ireland's hurling — both descend from the common Celtic stick-and-ball tradition — and international matches between Scotland and Ireland are still played under composite rules.

Rules

Each team fields twelve players (including the goalkeeper) with a caman, a stick of hard wood, curved and with a face that may not carry any slights; the caman must pass through a ring of two and a half inches in diameter, so the stick is slender. Players may play the ball in the air and swing the caman above shoulder height, using both sides of the slender stick, whose wedge-shaped head must be able to pass through a ring two and a half inches in diameter; a player may block, hook or cleek an opponent's caman within striking distance of the ball, but coming down on an opponent's stick (hacking) is foul play, as is pushing an opponent in the back, while body tackling is permitted only shoulder-to-shoulder. The ball is a seamed leather sphere of seven and a half to eight inches circumference, and the goal is the hail, defended by a goalkeeper who is the only player allowed to handle the ball, within a marked ten-yard area. Play is continuous — there is no offside — on a rectangular pitch 140 to 170 yards long and 70 to 80 yards wide, with matches of two 45-minute halves; foul play is punished with free hits, penalties at the hail and, for serious offences, caution or sending off.

History

Stick-and-ball games were played across the Gaelic world for centuries — the Scottish camanachd, the Irish hurling, the Manx cammag and the Welsh bando all share a common Celtic ancestry — and shinty was a fixture of Highland New Year festivities such as New Year's Day games between neighbouring parishes. The modern, organised sport dates from 1893, when the Camanachd Association was founded at a meeting in Kingussie with Lord Lovat as its first president to unify the rules of a game that varied from glen to glen. The Camanachd Cup, the sport's premier knockout competition, was first contested in 1896 and won by Kingussie, which with its neighbour Newtonmore has dominated the sport ever since — Kingussie's run of consecutive Camanachd Cups in the 1990s is among the longest winning streaks in any team sport. An international series against Ireland under composite shinty\u2013hurling rules has been played since 1897, and a women's cup competition, the Valerie Fraser Cup, supports the growing women's game. Shinty remains a true community game of the Highlands — its heartlands are Lochaber, Badenoch, Skye and Argyll, and the sport's heart is the weekly fixtures played in small towns and villages across the G\u00e0idhealtachd.
