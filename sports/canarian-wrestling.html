Canarian Wrestling

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Canarian wrestling — lucha canaria — is the indigenous folk wrestling style of the Canary Islands, Spain. Two wrestlers, the luchadores, face each other in a sand circle called the terrero and try to make the opponent touch the ground with any part of the body except the soles of the feet, using techniques called mañas; punching, hitting and strangling are forbidden, and no holds or ground fighting are allowed (Federación de Lucha Canaria, 2019). The sport is governed by the Federación de Lucha Canaria, which organizes the top-flight Torneo Disa Gobierno de Canarias (2025/26 season) with men's and women's categories — the federation explicitly tracks luchadores y luchadoras — and the island and regional championships; the rules were last modified by the extraordinary general assembly of 5 October 2019 (Federación de Lucha Canaria, n.d.). The terrero consists of two concentric circles of 15 and 17 m diameter; wrestlers start in the center, shake hands — a sign of nobility — and begin in the agarre (grip) with the left hand in the opponent's right sleeve. A bout (luchada) lasts less than 90 seconds per agarrada, and two falls are required to win an individual bout; team matches are decided by which team keeps more wrestlers standing, in formats such as three-agarrada series, lucha corrida or all-against-all (Federación de Lucha Canaria, 2019; Gobierno de Canarias, n.d.).

Rules

Under the Reglas del Deporte de la Lucha Canaria (last modified 5 October 2019), the bout begins with the agarre: left hand in the opponent's right trouser sleeve, right arms perpendicular, palms together, and the referee (árbitro) signals the start with a whistle. Wrestlers use mañas — grasping, blocking and deflecting moves with hands, feet and body — to unbalance the opponent; a fall is valid when the fallen wrestler has any part of the body inside or touching the outer line of the terrero, and a fall outside the line does not count. A wrestler who concedes before the first agarrada is counted as beaten; the referee may interrupt an agarrada for safety, and leaving the terrero during an interruption counts as a loss except for torn clothing or evident medical need. Penalties (amonestaciones) do not accumulate between agarradas; ties in the established series are resolved in favor of the wrestler with fewer penalties. Team roles include the puntal (the team's strongest wrestler) and the mandador (who selects who enters the terrero) (Federación de Lucha Canaria, 2019). Matches are fought by teams of about twelve wrestlers pairing off, or individually by weight, category or challenge; men's and women's competitions follow the same rules, and the federation runs a women's competition program (Federación de Lucha Canaria, n.d.). The related stick-fighting art lucha del garrote is practiced mainly on El Hierro, Gran Canaria, Tenerife and La Palma (Gobierno de Canarias, n.d.).

History

Lucha canaria descends from the wrestling of the Guanches, the pre-Hispanic inhabitants of the archipelago; because the islands had limited contact with each other, each developed its own rules. In 1420, shortly after the Castilian conquest began, Alvar García de Santa María first recorded the wrestling techniques, including referees called hombres de honor (men of honor). The first written rules date to 1872 — notably written in Matanzas, Cuba — and a common unified regulation (Reglamento General Orgánico) was agreed in 1960; provincial federations formed from the 1940s and led to the Federación Española de Lucha in 1984, with today's Federación de Lucha Canaria continuing the work (Gobierno de Canarias, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Among the island's most celebrated figures is Juan Barbuzano Martín of El Hierro, widely regarded as the most complete luchador of all time. West African lutte traditionnelle migrants from Senegal, Mali and Mauritania have joined Canarian clubs, using the sport as a vehicle for integration (Wikipedia, n.d.).
