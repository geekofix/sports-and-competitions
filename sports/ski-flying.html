Ski Flying

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Ski flying is the extreme version of ski jumping, in which athletes launch from specially enormous hills — ski flying hills — and soar more than 200 metres through the air, with world-record jumps beyond 250 metres. Governed by the International Ski Federation (FIS), it has its own World Cup crystal globe and its own biennial world championships, contested on the sport's handful of giant hills in Norway, Slovenia, Austria and Germany.

Rules

Ski flying is scored exactly like ski jumping — distance points plus judges' style points, with wind and gate compensation equalising conditions — but on hills whose hill size (HS) exceeds 185\u2013225 metres, so that flight phases exceed four seconds and in-runs are reached at 100 km/h. The full flight requires an extremely stable "V" aerodynamic position over the mammoth snow slope and an exacting telemark landing to avoid falls at over 100 km/h; competitions consist of two rounds (with the second reversed by standings), and a team competition is contested at world championships on each occasion. The FIS recognises only five active ski flying hills — Vikersund (Norway), Planica (Slovenia), Oberstdorf (Germany), Kulm/Bad Mitterndorf (Austria) and Harrachov (Czech Republic) — and after a 2011 rule change permitted construction of hills to the largest sizes yet (HS 225\u2013235), while pausing further expansion; ski flying has never been an Olympic event, and is raced instead in a dedicated Ski Flying World Cup and World Ski Flying Championships.

History

Ski flying began in 1934\u20131935 in Planica, then in Yugoslavia, where the giant Bloudkova Velikanka hill was built to promote jumps beyond 100 metres and Josef Bradl achieved the first one in 1936; the FIS was long suspicious of the discipline, and world records in distance have since been set almost exclusively on the globe's few flying hills through a series of hill "arms races" between Planica, Vikersund, Oberstdorf and Kulm — the KOP working group (Kulm/Oberstdorf/Planica) formed in 1962 to liaise with the FIS. The FIS-sanctioned International Ski Flying Week was superseded in 1972 by the first Ski Flying World Championships in Planica, and a dedicated World Cup classification was awarded from 1991; after Bj\u00f8rn Einar Rom\u00f8ren's 239.5 m at Planica in 2005, hill upgrades at Vikersund saw Johan Remen Evensen's 246.5 m (2011), Peter Prevc's historic first 250 m (2015), Anders Fannemel's 251.5 m — and Stefan Kraft's still-standing 253.5 m at Vikersund in 2017, with Domen Prevc later landing 254.5 m at Planica in 2025, the longest jump measured in official competition. Ry\u014dy\u016b Kobayashi's 291 m landing in a private 2024 project in Iceland stands outside FIS ratification; the sport's profile today rests on its showpiece finales in Planica and Vikersund, which draw crowds of tens of thousands.
