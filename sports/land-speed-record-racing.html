Land Speed Record Racing

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Land speed record (LSR) racing is the pursuit of the highest speed ever achieved by a vehicle on land, over a measured mile or kilometre with two runs in opposite directions within one hour; the FIA and FIM recognize the same absolute record for cars and motorcycles since a 1964 agreement (Wikipedia, n.d.). It belongs to the motorsport family and is the ultimate speed discipline.

Figure: Tristan Surtel. (2018-04-27 12:57:23). ThrustSSC at the Coventry Transport Museum [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. CC BY-SA 4.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/ThrustSSC_at_Coventry_Transport_Museum.jpg

The current absolute land speed record is 1,227.986 km/h (763.035 mph, Mach 1.02), set by RAF pilot Andy Green in the ThrustSSC on 15 October 1997 at Black Rock Desert, Nevada — the first and only officially recognized supersonic land vehicle (Wikipedia, n.d.). A new record must exceed the previous one by at least 1% to be validated by the FIA (Wikipedia, n.d.). Only jet- and rocket-powered "special vehicles" (FIA Category C) have held the absolute record since 1963 (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Rules

Rules (FIA/FIM): the vehicle must be wheel-driven at least in part, carry its own fuel, and make two runs over the measured course in opposite directions within one hour, with timing by certified equipment; production-car and motorcycle records are kept in separate FIA/FIM classes (Wikipedia, n.d.). Attempts happen at Black Rock Desert (Nevada), Bonneville Salt Flats (Utah), Hakskeen Pan (South Africa) and Pendine Sands (Wales) (Wikipedia, n.d.). Drivers wear fire suits and helmets; support crews handle chase planes and telemetry. Men and women have held records — Jessi Combs set the women’s wheel-driven record (825 km/h) in 2019, tragically dying on a later run (Wikipedia, n.d.).

History

The LSR story began with steam cars in the 1890s; the 400 mph barrier fell to John Cobb’s Railton in 1947 (634 km/h), the wheel-driven era ended with Donald Campbell’s turbine Bluebird CN7 in 1964 (648 km/h) (Wikipedia, n.d.). Americans then dominated: Craig Breedlove broke 500 mph (1964) and 600 mph (15 November 1965, 966.574 km/h) in Spirit of America Sonic I, Gary Gabelich hit 1,001 km/h in Blue Flame (1970), and Richard Noble took Thrust2 to 1,019 km/h in 1983 before Green’s supersonic 1997 runs (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Bloodhound LSR project reached 1,011 km/h at Hakskeen Pan in November 2019 aiming at 1,600 km/h (Wikipedia, n.d.).
