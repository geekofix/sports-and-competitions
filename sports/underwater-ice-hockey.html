Underwater Ice Hockey

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Underwater ice hockey is the extreme upside-down form of hockey: divers wearing diving suits and no oxygen tanks (breathing through a support air line, or freediving with safety support) play on the underside of a frozen lake, floating (from the players' inverted point of view) along the slippery ceiling of ice while chasing a floating puck into goals anchored beneath the surface. Developed by an Austrian freediving pioneer as an extreme-sport spectacle, it is contested in demonstration events on frozen Alpine lakes.

Rules

The playing surface is the underside of a thick sheet of natural ice: a roughly 6-by-8-metre area of lake ice is reinforced (or played with an air-pocket rink built), goals are hung from the ice at each end, and the buoyant puck floats against the ice ceiling where players, inverted, flick it with short sticks. Players wear diving suits but no tanks, breathing periodically from a standard air line suspended beneath the ice (safety divers and a standby hole complete the protective apparatus applied by the events' organizers); matches run two short halves of 10 minutes with the puck kept in play against the ice, and the scoring standard mirrors simplified hockey. The sport's organizers take wellbeing seriously (safety divers, ice thickness checks and hole-based escapes), but the entire game's essence is the inversion of normal sport: gravity is up, the rink above, the surface escape a hole cut behind each goal.

History

Underwater ice hockey was invented around 2005 by the Austrian freediver and extreme-event organizer Christian Redl, who staged the world's first under-ice hockey game on a frozen Austrian lake as a media spectacle and followed it with official international demonstration matches (including a famous Austria-Germany duel) and a world-record chase of under-ice distance events alongside his freediving feats. The sport remains on the demonstration circuit (support-diver community safety, GoPro-era video and freediving events, with occasional revival matches staged by Redl and other extreme-scene promoters); it never sought mass participation, and its fame rests as one of the most physically extreme converted-sport formats ever staged.
