Shotokan Karate

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shotokan is a Japanese style of karate founded by Gichin Funakoshi (1868\u20131957), the Okinawan school teacher honoured as the "father of modern karate", who brought karate from Okinawa to mainland Japan in the 1920s. The name comes from Funakoshi's pen name Shoto ("pine waves") and describes the Shoto-kan, the hall ("kan") of his Tokyo dojo, opened in 1936. Characterised by deep, long stances and powerful linear techniques, shotokan is today the most widely practised form of traditional karate in the world, organised through large bodies such as the Japan Karate Association (JKA) and numerous international derivatives.

Rules

Shotokan training divides into three parts: kihon (basic techniques), kata (pre-arranged forms) and kumite (sparring). Kata \u2014 the prescribed sequences of blocks, punches, strikes, sweeps and jumps \u2014 form the heart of the style: the JKA syllabus codified by Masatoshi Nakayama lists 26\u201327 kata, from the introductory Heian and Tekki series to advanced forms such as Bassai dai, Kanku dai, Empi, Jion, Hangetsu and Gankaku, most of them renamed by Funakoshi from their older Okinawan names. Kumite competition under JKA and WKF-style rules is either free sparring (jiyu kumite) scored on controlled contact to the head, body or sides, or the fixed-step sparring sequences of the syllabus. Rank progresses through coloured kyu belts to black-belt dan grades \u2014 notably, Funakoshi himself never awarded a rank higher than godan (fifth dan). Funakoshi's Twenty Precepts (niju kun) and the dojo kun govern the ethical framework of training.

History

Gichin Funakoshi was born in 1868 in Shuri, Okinawa, studied under masters Anko Asato and Anko Itosu, and gave the first public karate demonstrations in Japan from 1917 and notably at the 1922 First Athletic Exhibition in Tokyo, remaining to teach karate at Japanese universities. Funakoshi never named his own style, calling it only karate, but his students' nickname for his dojo hall \u2014 Shoto-kan \u2014 became the style's name after he opened his first permanent honbu dojo in Tokyo's Mejiro district in 1936. His son Gigo (Yoshitaka) Funakoshi is widely credited with developing the deeper stances and dynamic kicking of the modern style, and his master text Karate-do Kyohan (1935) remains the style's reference.

After the master's death in 1957, his students organised the Japan Karate Association (JKA, founded 1949) as a formal body, and the first All Japan Karate Championships were held in 1957, while other senior students including Shigeru Egami and Tsutomu Ohshima founded the Shotokai and other organisations \u2014 leading to many world-scale institutions including the Shotokan Karate-Do International Federation and the JKA's global network, today teaching shotokan in more than 100 countries.
