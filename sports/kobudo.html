Kobudo

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Kobudo is the weapons art of Okinawa, where indigenous fighting methods merged with Chinese martial arts after the Satsuma invasion of 1609 and repeated weapons bans turned farming and fishing tools into weapons: the bō (six-foot staff), sai (trident), tonfa (rice-mill handle), nunchaku, kama (sickle), eku (boat oar), tekko (knuckle-dusters), tinbe and rochin (shield and spear) and the surujin (weighted chain) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The art was systematized in the 20th century by masters such as Taira Shinken, who compiled 42 kata, and is today organized internationally by the Nihon Kobudo Kyokai (founded 17 February 1979 at the Nippon Budokan) and the World Ryukyu Kobudo Federation, which runs the World Ryukyu Kobudo Championships with kata and koshiki kumite (kumi undo) competition (Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, n.d.-a; World Ryukyu Kobudo Federation, n.d.-a). Women train and compete on equal terms in kata and in protective-gear kumite at the world championships, and the annual Meiji Jingu Hono Nihon Kobudo Taikai in Tokyo showcases all major weapon schools (World Budokan, n.d.-a). At the top of the international scene, the 2026 World Ryukyu Kobudo Championships were held on 18 January 2026 at the Okinawa Karate Kaikan, with individual and team kata across all weapon lines (World Ryukyu Kobudo Federation, n.d.-a).

Rules

Kobudo is practiced through kata — fixed, pre-arranged forms for each weapon — and through paired applications (bunkai), and at World Ryukyu Kobudo Championships also through koshiki kumite, a light-contact sparring with protective gear (World Ryukyu Kobudo Federation, n.d.-a). The eight classical weapons each have their own kata set; Taira Shinken's compilation of 42 kata remains the reference syllabus of the Ryukyu Kobudo Hozon Shinko Kai, while the Matayoshi line (Matayoshi kobudo) adds its own forms with kama, sai and tinbe-rochin (Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, n.d.-a). In koshiki kumite, competitors wear a chest protector, groin protector and gloves; women additionally wear a chest protector under the men's-style gear, and men and women compete in separate divisions (World Ryukyu Kobudo Federation, n.d.-a). There are no points-based open matches like karate kumite in the traditional curriculum; gradings (kyu and dan) are awarded by the school or national federation after examination of kata, weapons handling and etiquette (World Budokan, n.d.-a). Training with live-edged or steel weapons is governed by strict safety rules — blunt iaitō-style blades are used for paired work. History

Weapon arts developed in the Ryukyu Kingdom from the 14th century onward, fed by trade and envoys from China and Southeast Asia; after the Satsuma invasion of 1609 and the weapons prohibitions that followed, peasants and fishermen disguised their arms as everyday tools — the origin of the kobudo weapons (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). In the early 20th century, masters such as Yabiku Moden and his student Taira Shinken (1897–1970) traveled the islands collecting and preserving weapon kata in preservation societies, and after World War II Taira founded the Ryukyu Kobudo Hozon Shinko Kai in 1955 to safeguard the 42 kata (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Matayoshi Shinkō (1888–1947) developed the separate Matayoshi line of kobudo, Chogi Kishaba and Kenwa Mabuni helped carry the art into the mainstream Japanese budō of the Shōwa era, and the Nippon Budokan (founded 1899) became the institutional home of kobudō in Japan, with the Nihon Kobudo Kyokai established there on 17 February 1979 (Nihon Kobudo Kyokai, n.d.-a). The art spread worldwide from the 1960s, with early demonstrations in the United States in Chicago in 1963 (World Budokan, n.d.-a).
