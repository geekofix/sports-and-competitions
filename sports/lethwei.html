Lethwei

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Lethwei (Burmese:/categories lethwei, "art of nine limbs") is the traditional bare-knuckle boxing of Myanmar, fought with wrapped fists and no gloves, in which punches, kicks, elbows, knees and even headbutts are all permitted (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; World Lethwei Championship, n.d.). A professional bout is contested over five rounds of three minutes, judged by ringside officials, and in modern rules a fighter who is knocked down three times in one round loses by technical knockout, while otherwise the decision can be a win, a loss or a draw (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The sport is governed in Myanmar by the Myanmar Traditional Lethwei Federation and internationally by promotions such as the World Lethwei Championship (WLC), which has also staged women's bouts, so women now compete at professional level in lethwei alongside men (World Lethwei Championship, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Lethwei is fought in a roped ring with corner posts, supervised by a referee (kardaw) and a panel of judges (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Fighters wear only hand wraps — no boxing gloves — and may strike with both fists, both elbows, both knees, both feet and the head, which is why it is called the art of nine limbs (World Lethwei Championship, n.d.). Professional bouts run five three-minute rounds with one-minute rests; under modern unified rules there is no standing eight-count, three knockdowns in a single round end the bout by TKO, and if no knockout occurs the judges' scorecards decide, with a draw a possible verdict (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Weight classes are contracted per bout, weigh-ins are supervised, and traditional Myanmar rules additionally allow the wap (a traditional herbal liniment) and the pra jioud (headband) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Fouls such as biting, eye-gouging, groin strikes and strikes to a downed opponent are prohibited (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women's bouts follow the same round structure and scoring, with contracted weights adjusted per division, and women's fights have been featured on WLC and ONE Championship cards in recent years (World Lethwei Championship, n.d.).

History

Lethwei's roots stretch back centuries in Myanmar, where bare-knuckle fighting was practiced by soldiers and at village festivals, and the modern sport was codified in the mid-20th century with formal rules, weight divisions and a federation structure (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The art was carried abroad by Burmese fighters and gained international attention in the 2000s through the World Lethwei Championship, founded in 2013, which staged events in Myanmar, Thailand, China and the United States (World Lethwei Championship, n.d.). The Canadian Dave Leduc became the sport's most decorated foreign champion with six WLC world titles between 2009 and 2016, while Myanmar's Tun Tun Min was the country's dominant stadium fighter of the 2010s (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). Women have practiced lethwei in Myanmar for generations at local level, and the growth of televised women's bouts since the late 2010s has given female fighters a professional platform for the first time (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
