HEMA

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information

Historical European martial arts (HEMA) is the practice-based reconstruction of the fighting arts of medieval and Renaissance Europe, studied from the surviving fight books (Fechtbücher) of roughly 1300–1800 and trained today with blunted steel weapons and modern protective gear in clubs and at tournaments around the world (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Because there is no single world federation, the scene is organized around the two largest umbrella bodies — the HEMA Alliance (HEMAA) in the United States and the Historical European Martial Arts Council (HEMAC) in Europe — together with tournament platforms such as HEMA Scorecard and the worldwide rating system HEMA Ratings (HEMA Alliance, n.d.-a; Historical European Martial Arts Council, n.d.; HEMA Scorecard, n.d.). The most practiced weapon is the longsword of the German school, which dominates most clubs, followed by sword and buckler, rapier, messer, dagger, polearms and wrestling (Ringen); major annual tournaments include Swordfish in Gothenburg, Longpoint in Baltimore, SoCal Swordfight, the Örebro Open and WMAW (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; HEMA Alliance, n.d.-b). Tournaments are mixed-gender, and women regularly reach the later rounds and finals of the major longsword and rapier events, with dedicated women's brackets at some events (HEMA Alliance, n.d.-b) (HEMA Alliance, n.d.-a) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

HEMA has no single official rulebook — each tournament publishes its own ruleset, commonly based on the HEMAA tournament rules — but the shared principles are protective equipment (fencing mask, gorget, gloves, knee and elbow protection, plastron or jacket, with some styles using open-faced helmets), blunted steel simulators instead of sharp weapons, and a central referee with side judges (HEMA Alliance, n.d.-b). The longsword simulator (federschwert) weighs roughly 1.2–1.7 kg with a flexible thrusting tip; scoring typically awards points for clean hits to valid target areas (head and torso foremost), counts an afterblow within one tempo, penalizes doubles (both fencers hit), and in most longsword rulesets allows blade grabs while grappling is restricted; a disarm may score extra points; bouts are usually first to a set number of points or a time limit, run in pools followed by direct elimination (HEMA Alliance, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Safety rules require control of the thrust, a salute before and after, and immediate stop on the referee's command; women and men compete under identical rules, usually in mixed brackets, and age/size classes (e.g., women's or lightweight brackets) exist at larger events (HEMA Alliance, n.d.-b).

History

The European fighting arts were taught from the late Middle Ages onward in the German, Italian, Spanish, English, Scottish and French schools; around 50 German fight books survive, anchored by the Codex Germanicus 3227a (c. 1389) and the rhymed Zettel attributed to Johannes Liechtenauer (c. 1430s), whose tradition was glossed by the "Five Masters" (Fünf Meisterhau) and structured into the four wards (Vier Huten) with the Vor and Nach (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). The Italian school produced Fiore dei Liberi's Flos Duellatorum (c. 1409), the English tradition the Royal Armouries MS I.33 (c. 1300) and George Silver's Paradoxes of Defence (1599), and the German master Joachim Meyer published his Gründtliche Beschreibung der Kunst des Fechtens in 1570; the rapier tradition was codified by Capo Ferro and Agrippa (both 1553), Saviolo (1595) and Fabris (1606) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). After the arts fell out of use in the 18th and 19th centuries, the modern revival began in the 1990s through the early internet and printed transcriptions, and grew from the 2000s into today's club and tournament scene organized by HEMAA and HEMAC (HEMA Alliance, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
