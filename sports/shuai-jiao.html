Shuai Jiao

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shuai jiao is the traditional jacket-wrestling art of China, the name in modern Mandarin for Chinese wrestling as a whole and specifically for the codified style of Beijing, Tianjin and Baoding in the North China Plain. Wrestlers wear a short-sleeved, tight-wrapping jacket (da lian) with a canvas belt, grip each other's jackets and belts, and attempt clean throws — with sweeps, hip throws, leg hooks and pickups — on a raised or matted area. Claiming descent from ancient Chinese wrestling systems recorded over 2,000 years ago, it is one of the oldest documented jacket-wrestling styles in the world and today is promoted through bodies such as the Chinese Wrestling Association and national shuai jiao organisations worldwide.

Rules

Competitors wear the shuai jiao uniform — jacket, long trousers, soft shoes and a belt — with a red or blue belt assigned for scoring by officials. Legal techniques include throws, sweeps, trips and takedowns executed by gripping the opponent's jacket, belt or limbs (using techniques drawn from different families: standing throws, fast "shoot"-style throws, open-hand sweeping techniques and joint-manipulation throws), with striking, strangulation and leg-lock submissions barred. Matches are decided by points for throws that put the opponent on the ground, with superior points awarded for throws of greater amplitude onto the opponent's back — the judging panel consists of an executive referee, assistant referee and verification referee — and fouls are punished by warnings and point deductions, with a third violation or a serious intentional foul leading to disqualification. A match is won on points or by the opponent's disqualification; falls, split trousers or leaving the contest area also penalise the offender.

History

Chinese wrestling's lineage is traced by tradition to jiaodi — "horn butting" — a sport of the legendary Yellow Emperor's soldiers and a court spectacle from the Zhou dynasty, when the military system of jiao li was recorded in the Classic of Rites and wrestlers competed on raised lei tai platforms for imperial rewards. The style of the modern game crystallised under the Qing dynasty's imperial bodyguard unit, the Shan Pu Ying (the "Battalion of Excellency in Catching") of the Imperial Household, whose Buddhist\u2013military wrestling tradition shaped the regional styles of Beijing, Tianjin and Baoding. When the empire fell, the art was modernised in the Republican period: the educator Ma Liang published a wrestling manual in 1917 and demonstrated the art nationally, the Central Guoshu Institute standardised the rules, and in 1928 the name shuai jiao was officially adopted, with the first Chinese national shuai jiao competition held in 1935. After 1949 masters carried the style to Taiwan and overseas — most famously Grandmaster Chang Tung-Sheng — and today shuai jiao organisations including the United States Shuai-Chiao Association and the British Shuai Jiao Union keep the art alive, worldwide in diaspora communities as well as Mainland China.
