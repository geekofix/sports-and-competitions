Shuai Jiao

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Shuai jiao ("falling wrestling") is the traditional Chinese jacket-wrestling art practised standing on a raised circular platform (lei tai): competitors wear a short canvas jacket (da lian) and a belt, grip each other and use throws, sweeps and leg grabs, while groundwork is not allowed; it is one of China's oldest sports, with jiaodi/jiaoli contests recorded in the Zhou-dynasty Classic of Rites and codified in the Qing court by Shan Pu Ying of the Nei Wu Fu (Imperial Household), and it is contested internationally under the World Shuai Jiao Federation, which stages world championships and the World Shuai Jiao Games planned for 16–18 October 2026 (The World Games, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The term shuai jiao has been in use since 1928, and the first national championship was held in 1935 (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Women's shuai jiao is officially recognised with its own weight categories at national and world championships, and Chinese women have competed internationally since the 1990s; among the current elite, the Chinese women's and men's national teams dominate the world championships, and regional powerhouses such as Beijing, Tianjin and Baoding ("fast wrestling") keep the five classical styles alive (Wikipedia, n.d.; The World Games, n.d.).

Within the combat sports and martial arts family on this site, its closest relatives are Wrestling, Greco Roman Wrestling, Freestyle Wrestling, Judo, Combat Sambo; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

A shuai jiao bout takes place standing on the lei tai platform: the two wrestlers wear the da lian jacket and belt, may grip the jacket, belt, collar and sleeves, and may grab the opponent's legs, but may not take the fight to the ground — a clean throw that puts the opponent down scores points (an ipon-like score does not end the match), and pushing or throwing the opponent out of the platform also scores; the referee (jiaodi) stops and restarts the action (Wikipedia, n.d.). Competitions use weight classes for men and women, and women's categories are officially contested at world championships (Wikipedia, n.d.; The World Games, n.d.). Because the jacket grips and standing throws travelled with Chinese martial arts to Japan, shuai jiao is recognised as one of the direct ancestors of judo's jujutsu (Wikipedia, n.d.).

History

Jacket wrestling (jiaodi, jiaoli) is attested in China from the Zhou dynasty — the Classic of Rites describes contests — and Song-dynasty art shows wrestlers grappling; in the Qing court the Nei Wu Fu wrestlers, organised under masters such as Shan Pu Ying, codified the rules and trained the imperial guard (Wikipedia, n.d.). The six great Tianjin masters of the 1930s — Zhang Lianchen, Zhang Hongyu, Wang Wenhai, Shi Enfu, Ma Wenping and Wang Haizheng — are commemorated in a famous 1930 photograph; the modern sport name shuai jiao dates from 1928 and the first national championship was staged in 1935 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Five regional styles survive — Baoding's fast wrestling (kuai jiao), Beijing, Tianjin, Shanxi and Mongolian shuai jiao — and internationally the World Shuai Jiao Federation (a new world federation founded on 27 August 2019 by 26 national organisations) runs biennial world championships, with shuai jiao also showcased at the World Games (Wikipedia, n.d.; The World Games, n.d.).
