Toguzkumalak

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Togyzkumalak (toguz kumalak, “nine pebbles”) is the Kazakh and Kyrgyz sowing game of the mancala family, played on a board of two rows of nine pits (otau) plus two stores (kazan) with 162 stones (kumalak), nine per pit; a player wins by collecting 82 or more stones. It is governed by the Togyzkumalak Federation of Kazakhstan (formed 1974, re-established 2004) and the World Togyzkumalak Federation, which has held world championships every two years since 2010 (Astana 2010, Pardubice 2012, Almaty 2015, Astana 2017, Antalya 2019, Aqtobe 2022, Astana 2024 within the World Nomad Games) with separate men's and women's championships; by 2018 Kazakhstan counted around 190,000 registered players. In 2020 UNESCO inscribed “Togyzkumalak/Toguz Korgool/Mangala/Göçürme” for Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Türkiye. Men and women of all ages play, and there is a simplified children's variant (bestemshe).

Within the mind games on this site, its closest relatives are mancala, oware and bao; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

Players sow anticlockwise from one of their nine otaus, one stone per hole, including their own kazan (which sits at the opponent's end of their row) but not the opponent's. If the last stone lands in an opponent's pit making it an even number, those stones are captured to the kazan; landing on three makes the pit a tuzdik (captured accumulation), with the restrictions that each player may create only one tuzdik, the ninth hole cannot become one, and symmetry with the opponent's tuzdik is forbidden. If a player has no stones left (atsyz kalu), the opponent sweeps their remaining stones; the player with 82+ stones in the kazan wins, 81–81 is a draw.

History

The rules were first described scientifically by the Kazakh scholar Sarsen Amanzholov in 1936 (Russian ethnographers such as Boris Kuftin had noted the game in 1906), and tournament rules were codified in 1949 by Mukhtar Auezov and Kalibek Kuanyshbaev; the first modern tournament was held in Almaty in 1948. The Federation of Togyzkumalak of the Kazakh SSR was formed in 1974 and re-established in 2004. The game spread to Germany, Czechia (Pardubice hosts world championships), Lithuania and beyond; there are even world championships for togyzkumalak computer programs (first in Pardubice 2015). UNESCO inscribed the shared Turkic tradition in 2020.
