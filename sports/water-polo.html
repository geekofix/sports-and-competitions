Water Polo

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Water polo is a competitive team ball sport played in a swimming pool, in which teams of seven players (six field players plus a goalkeeper) swim, tread water and pass a ball with the aim of throwing it into the opponent's goal. It is one of the oldest Olympic team sports, contested since the 1900 Paris Games, and combines swimming endurance, wrestling-style physicality and handball-like tactics.

Matches are played in a pool at least 1.8 metres deep so players cannot touch the bottom, with the ball advancing by passing or swimming, and goals scored into nets measuring 3 metres wide and 90 centimetres high.

Rules

Each match consists of four periods, typically eight minutes of playing time each, with a 30-second shot clock requiring a shot on goal. Field players may only touch the ball with one hand at a time and must keep their heads above water, while goalkeepers may use both hands within five metres of their goal. Players swim using a head-up front crawl (water polo crawl) and hold position with eggbeater kick. Physical contact is permitted within the rules — shoulder-to-shoulder challenges, stealing the ball from a holder and controlled impeding — but fouls such as holding, sinking an opponent or striking the ball with a clenched fist are punished with ordinary or exclusion fouls, the latter sending a player to the penalty area for 20 seconds. Major fouls in the attacking area can bring a 5-metre penalty throw.

History

The sport emerged in late nineteenth-century England and Scotland as a form of aquatic rugby played in rivers and lakes; the London Water Polo Club drew up early rules in 1870 and the first recognised international took place in 1890 between Scotland and England. Men's water polo entered the Olympics in 1900 — making it the oldest Olympic team sport alongside football — and the women's tournament was added at Sydney 2000. Today it is governed internationally by World Aquatics, with strong professional leagues in Hungary, Italy, Spain, Greece, Croatia and Serbia's successor states, and world championship and World Cup competitions held on regular cycles.
