SpoGomi

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Spogomi (from Japanese sport and gomi, trash) is the Japanese competition sport of picking up litter while walking or jogging: teams score points for the trash they collect and correctly sort within a time limit and a set area. Invented in 2008, the sport has grown into a worldwide movement and now stages a biennial Spogomi World Cup that draws national teams from dozens of countries.

Rules

A spogomi team fields three players (some events allow three to five) who must stay within ten metres of each other at all times; running is forbidden, and teams may collect litter only from public ground, not from bins, collection points or private property. A competition typically gives teams one hour to gather litter in the designated area and twenty minutes to sort it correctly by category; points are scored both for the amount and the type of waste, with items such as cigarette butts commanding the highest scores while burnables, cans, bottles and PET bottles earn graduated rates. Bulky items, hazardous waste and anything fished out of rivers, oceans or gutters are prohibited, and breaking the rules can bring point deductions or disqualification (for lateness, non-walking travel, ballast stuffed into bags or unsporting conduct). The team with the most points after weighing and sorting wins.

History

Spogomi was invented in Japan in 2008 by runner Kenichi Mamitsuka, who turned picking up litter on his morning runs into a game with official rules and time limits. Annual contests followed across Japan, and the sport found national momentum when its organizers joined the Nippon Foundation's Ocean and Japan Project, running a nationwide tournament modelled on Japan's famed high school baseball event from 2019. Its international breakthrough came with the first Spogomi World Cup in Tokyo in November 2023, where the British team The North Will Rise Again beat national teams from twenty-one countries; the second World Cup followed in October 2025, won by Japan with Germany runner-up, and the next is planned for 2027. Today hundreds of thousands of participants have collected enormous quantities of litter, and organizers present the sport as a competitive answer to marine pollution, since most ocean waste begins on land.
