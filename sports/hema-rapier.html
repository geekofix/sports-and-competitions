HEMA Rapier

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

The rapier is the one-handed thrusting sword of the 16th to early 18th centuries — the weapon of the Italian, Spanish (destreza) and English schools of fence — and its modern reconstruction is one of the core disciplines of Historical European martial arts (HEMA), fought with blunted steel rapiers and smallswords at dedicated rapier tournaments within the major HEMA events (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The masters of the art include Camillo Agrippa (1553), Ridolfo Capo Ferro (1553), Vincentio Saviolo (1595), Salvator Fabris (1606) and the Englishmen George Silver (1599) and Joseph Swetnam, whose works are studied line by line in clubs today; by around 1715 the rapier had been replaced by the lighter smallsword in civilian use, and it survives ceremonially as the weapon of the Pontifical Swiss Guard (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b, n.d.-c). Rapier tournaments are mixed-gender with identical rules for women and men, and women regularly reach the finals of the major rapier and smallsword events (HEMA Alliance, n.d.) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Rules

HEMA rapier is fenced with blunted steel rapiers or smallswords (flexible thrusting tip), fencing mask, gorget, gloves and a protective jacket or plastron, under the published ruleset of each tournament — commonly the HEMAA rapier/smallsword rules (HEMA Alliance, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Scoring follows the thrust-oriented nature of the weapon: in common rulesets (for example the League of Hungarian Experts / LHO-style rules) a clean thrust to the head or torso scores 3 points, a cut to the head scores 3, and hits to the arms and legs score fewer points; grappling and blade grabs are not allowed in rapier — grabbing the opponent's blade means the hand is a valid target — and afterblows within one tempo count; bouts run to a point limit or time cap in pools and direct elimination with a central referee (HEMA Alliance, n.d.). The technical actions come from the sources: the Italian guards (guardia alta, coda lunga), the Spanish line of engagement (ificación/linea de cuerpo), the lunge and fleche, the beat, bind and prise de fer, the riposte and the counter-riposte (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Women and men compete under identical rules, usually in mixed brackets; the smallsword (the lighter successor weapon) is often fenced under the same or a linked ruleset at the same events (HEMA Alliance, n.d.). There is no single world federation for HEMA; the HEMA Alliance and HEMAC coordinate the tournament scene (HEMA Alliance, n.d.).

History

The rapier evolved in the 16th century from the civilian sidearm of the Renaissance into a thrusting weapon optimized for the duel; the Spanish school of destreza, founded by Gerard Thibault d'Anvers and systematized by Pacheco de Narváez and later Salvator Fabris, dominated theory, while the Italian masters Agrippa and Capo Ferro (both 1553) and Saviolo (1595) codified the rapier in print, and the English masters Silver and Swetnam contributed in the early 17th century (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). By about 1715 the rapier gave way to the smallsword in fashionable civilian use, and fencing masters turned to the épée and foil of sport fencing; the rapier remained in use by the Pontifical Swiss Guard, which still trains with it today (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). The modern HEMA revival of rapier and smallsword began in the 1990s and 2000s alongside the longsword revival, and today rapier is fenced competitively at dedicated brackets of Swordfish, Longpoint, SoCal Swordfight and other major HEMA events (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
