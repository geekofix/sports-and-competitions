Dog Sledding

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Dog sledding (mushing) is a winter sport in which a musher on a sled is pulled by a harnessed team of sled dogs over snow-covered trails; the first team to cross the finish line of a marked course wins (IFSS, n.d.). Competitive racing is governed internationally by the International Federation of Sleddog Sports (IFSS), which sanctions sprint, middle-distance and long-distance World Championships, while the most famous races \u2014 the Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race from Anchorage to Nome, Alaska (about 1,000 miles), and the Yukon Quest between Alaska and Canada's Yukon \u2014 are stage races with mandatory rests at checkpoints (Iditarod Trail, Inc., n.d.-b; Yukon Quest, n.d.). The 54th Iditarod ran in 2026 with the ceremonial start in Anchorage on 7 March and the northern route from Willow; Jessie Holmes of Huskycha, Alaska, defended her 2025 title and reached Nome on 17 March 2026 in 9 days, 7 hours, 32 minutes and 51 seconds, becoming the first musher in a decade to win back-to-back Iditarods (Iditarod Trail, Inc., n.d.-a). The 2026 Yukon Quest Alaska 750, won by Josi Shelley of Fairbanks in 9 days, 9 hours and 11 minutes on 16 February 2026, ran on the roughly 750-mile Fairbanks loop after the race split between an Alaska and a Canada edition in 2022 (Yukon Quest, n.d.). At the end of 2026, the leading current mushers are \u2014 among women \u2014 two-time Iditarod champion Jessie Holmes and 2026 Yukon Quest winner Josi Shelley, and \u2014 among men \u2014 five-time Iditarod champion Dallas Seavey, the youngest winner of the race in 2012 (Iditarod Trail, Inc., n.d.-a; Yukon Quest, n.d.).

Rules

Under IFSS regulations, race classes are defined by team size \u2014 from two-dog skijoring-linked sprint classes up to open-class teams of 12\u201316 dogs \u2014 and by distance: sprint races cover roughly 4\u201312 miles per day over several days with mass or interval starts, middle-distance races run 30\u201375 miles per day, and long-distance stage races such as the Iditarod cover about 1,000 miles in 8\u201315 days with mandatory rests at checkpoints (IFSS, n.d.; Iditarod Trail, Inc., n.d.-b). The musher stands on the runners or toboggan and may push, brake and help the team, but outside assistance, drafting behind another team and doping are prohibited; dogs must be vaccinated, microchipped and pass veterinary checks at every checkpoint (IFSS, n.d.). Races use a staggered start procedure, and the first team across the finish line wins; at the Iditarod the teams start 2 minutes apart in Willow and race through 20+ checkpoints including the halfway rest in Nulato on the northern route (Iditarod Trail, Inc., n.d.-b). Men and women compete in the same races \u2014 women have won the Iditarod (Susan Butcher, four times between 1986 and 1990) \u2014 and junior races exist for young mushers (IFSS, n.d.; Yukon Quest, n.d.).

History

Sled dogs have pulled loads across the Arctic for thousands of years, used by the Indigenous peoples of Alaska, Canada, Greenland and Siberia (Wikipedia, n.d.). Competitive racing began in Nome, Alaska, in the early 1900s with events such as the All Alaska Sweepstakes, and the first Iditarod was run in 1973 \u2014 won by Dick Wilmarth in about 20 days \u2014 to commemorate the historic mail trail; the Yukon Quest began in 1984 as a 1,000-mile race between Fairbanks and Whitehorse, self-billed as the toughest sled dog race in the world with ten checkpoints (Iditarod Trail, Inc., n.d.-b; Yukon Quest, n.d.). The 1925 serum run to Nome, when sled teams delivered diphtheria antitoxin across 674 miles in a relay, made sled dogs national heroes; the lead dog of the final team, Togo, and musher Leonhard Seppala, pictured below, are commemorated by statues and the Iditarod itself (Wikipedia, n.d.). The IFSS, founded in the 1960s, has organized World Championships in sprint and distance formats since the 1970s (IFSS, n.d.). Legendary mushers include Joe Redington Sr., the "father of modern sled dog racing," five-time Iditarod winner Rick Swenson and four-time champion Susan Butcher (Iditarod Trail, Inc., n.d.-b).
