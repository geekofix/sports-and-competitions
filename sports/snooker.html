Snooker

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Snooker is the cue sport played on a large, baize-covered billiards table with six pockets, in which players score points by potting reds and colours with a cue ball under strict alternation rules. Invented by British Army officers in India in the late 19th century, it became the flagship professional cue sport of the United Kingdom and — via the World Snooker Championship at Sheffield's Crucible Theatre and a global professional tour — of East and Southeast Asia; it is governed professionally by the World Professional Billiards and Snooker Association (WPBSA).

Rules

A standard table measures 12 by 6 feet, and a full game uses 15 reds (one point each) and six colours: yellow (2), green (3), brown (4), blue (5), pink (6) and black (7), plus the white cue ball. The striker must pot a red first, then nominate and pot a colour (which is re-spotted), alternating red and colour until the reds are gone, after which the colours are potted in ascending order and stay off the table; points accrue for each legally potted ball, and a "break" is the total scored in one turn at the table. Fouls (striking the wrong ball first, potting the cue ball, going in-off, failing to hit a ball on when snookered) concede a minimum of four penalty points, up to seven for fouls involving the black, and leave the table safe to the unpenalised player; tactics revolve around building breaks while leaving "snookers" — obstacles — when safety play is needed. A "frame" ends when the table is cleared or one player concedes; matches are the best of a set number of frames, and the maximum break of 147 (15 reds with 15 blacks, then all six colours) is the sport's most celebrated achievement (a 148 by Jamie Burnett in 2004 with a free ball is the only higher officially recognised break).

History

Snooker was devised around 1875 by British Army officers stationed at Jubbulpore (Jabalpur) in India, who had earlier played the black-pool variant "pyramids" and added coloured balls and a new scoring sequence; the story links the game to Sir Neville Chamberlain, who reportedly named it after cadet "snookers" (first-year soldiers), and led by the billiards equipment firm Burroughs and Watts the game spread through the Empire to England. Joe Davis — the sport's first great — persuaded the Billiards Association and Control Council to found a professional world championship in 1927 and won the first fifteen (1927\u201346) while retiring undefeated, scoring the first officially recognised maximum break of 147 in a 1955 exhibition match against Willie Smith; Davis also promoted "Snooker Plus", an experimental variant with two extra balls (see Snooker plus). The modern era began with the BBC's Pot Black (first broadcast 1969, launched in colour in the 1970s by David Attenborough's BBC2) which made players household names, and the World Championship — moved to the Crucible Theatre, Sheffield in 1977 — anchored the professional circuit: the 1980s of Steve Davis, the 1990s of Stephen Hendry (seven Crucible titles) and 2000s\u20132020s of Ronnie O'Sullivan (seven world titles, the fastest ever 147 in five minutes 20 seconds in 1997) gave the sport its icons, while sponsorship (Embassy, then betting companies) and, since 2010, the growth of Chinese and Asian professional circuits have created a sport played — and watched — all over the world.
