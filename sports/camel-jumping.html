Camel Jumping

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Camel jumping is a traditional sport of the Zaraniq (al-Zaraneeq) tribe on the Tihama plain along the Red Sea coast of western Yemen. Barefoot men sprint down a runway and vault in a single bound over a row of standing dromedary camels; the competitor who clears the most camels wins. Events are held at weddings and festivals such as the al-Khamis festival at the end of the palm season, and camel jumping was also shown at the Sanaa Summer Festival. There is no formal governing body; the custom is maintained by the tribe itself.

Rules

For a contest, camels of equal height are lined up closely in a single row and held by their reins and tail. The jumper takes a running start on flat ground, uses a small mound of packed mud and dried weeds as a takeoff ramp, and attempts to clear the whole row in one leap, landing on the far side. There are no points for style; only the number of camels cleared counts. Each competitor gets one try. Ordinary competitors clear four to five camels, while professionals have been known to clear six or seven. The unofficial record of six camels is held by Zaid Omer of Yemen. Women traditionally do not take part in the competitions.

History

The sport is said to have begun generations ago as a dare between two Zaraniq tribesmen haggling over the price of a camel. It remained a tribal tradition in the Tihama desert for centuries, declined over the 20th century, and has recently been revived among younger tribe members. Photojournalists Adam Reynolds and Ed Ou documented the jumpers in the 2000s and 2010s, bringing international attention, and Reuters photographs showed camel jumping at the Sanaa Summer Festival in 2013. The jumpers are famous throughout Yemen for their speed, strength and courage, and the sport is accompanied by traditional singing and dancing.
