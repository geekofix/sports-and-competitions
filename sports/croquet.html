Croquet

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Croquet is a precision lawn game in which players hit wooden balls with mallets through a course of hoops and finally at a central peg; the competitive form, Association Croquet, is governed worldwide by the World Croquet Federation (WCF), founded in 1986, which publishes the Laws of Association Croquet and runs the World Championship (first held in July 1989) and the Women's World Championship (World Croquet Federation [WCF], n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The historic rivalry between Australia and England for the MacRobertson Shield — the oldest international trophy in croquet, first contested in 1925 — saw its 101st-anniversary edition in 2026 won by England, with the next match scheduled for 2030 in New Zealand; the shield itself is pictured below (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). In England, the Croquet Association (founded 1867) governs the sport with clubs across the country and publishes the laws used internationally (Croquet Association, n.d.). Association Croquet is played in singles and doubles, and is a genuinely mixed-gender sport — men and women compete together in open events as well as in the dedicated Women's World Championship, and there are no gender-specific rules (WCF, n.d.). Club play uses smaller lawns, while international matches are played on a full-size lawn; a handicap system from 0 to 12 lets players of different ability compete on equal terms, and senior events exist for older players (Croquet Association, n.d.). At the end of 2026, the leading current players are the members of England's 2026 MacRobertson Shield-winning team alongside the reigning Association Croquet World Champion and Women's World Champion, who head the WCF world rankings (WCF, n.d.).

Rules

Association Croquet is played under the Laws of Association Croquet (current 7th edition), published by the WCF and in England by the Croquet Association; the game uses a rectangular lawn — 35 × 28 yards (32 × 25.6 m) for full-size international play — with six hoops, a central peg and boundary lines (WCF, n.d.; Croquet Association, n.d.). Each player (or pair in doubles, playing four balls) takes a turn, striking their own ball with a mallet; the objective is to run each of the six hoops in the prescribed order in both directions and finally to hit the peg ("peg out"), scoring one point per hoop run and one for pegging out; a "roquet" — hitting another ball — gives bonus shots, and a "bisque" is awarded when a ball is hit out of bounds or the striker's ball is hit by another, letting weaker players stay in contention (WCF, n.d.). After running a hoop, the player earns an extra shot; play alternates between sides, and a singles game has a time limit with the higher score winning. Equipment: balls 3.625 inches (92 mm) in diameter weighing about 1 lb (454 g), mallets, hoops of spring steel set 12 inches (30 cm) above the ground, and a 18-inch peg (Croquet Association, n.d.). Formats and classifications: open singles/doubles, women's singles/doubles, mixed doubles, and age categories from juniors to seniors; handicap grades (0–12 in half-point steps) equalize club competition, while international team matches — the MacRobertson Shield between Australia and England, the Solomon Trophy with New Zealand and the world team events — play at scratch handicap (WCF, n.d.). The laws are identical for men and women; separate women's championships exist alongside the open ones (WCF, n.d.).

History

Croquet takes its name from the French word for a shepherd's crook (croquet) and emerged from the French game of pall-mall in the 1850s, spreading rapidly through England with the first rules drawn up in the 1850s and the Croquet Association founded in 1867 to standardize the game (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The sport became a Victorian sensation, played on lawns from palace gardens to public parks, and spread through the British Empire — in Australia and New Zealand it grew into a fiercely contested rivalry, formalized in 1925 with the first MacRobertson Shield match between Australia and England (Wikipedia, n.d.-c). The modern competitive era began with the founding of the World Croquet Federation in 1986 and the first Association Croquet World Championship in July 1989 (WCF, n.d.). The most iconic players are the multiple world champions: England's Chris Clarke, Association Croquet world champion in 1995 and 2008; New Zealand's Robert Fulford, one of the most decorated internationals of the modern era and a long-time MacRobertson Shield winner; and England's Reg Bamford, whose elegant stroke defined an era of the game (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). On the women's side, the Women's World Championship has been contested since the late 1980s, with champions from England, Australia and New Zealand establishing a deep international rivalry (WCF, n.d.). A related simpler code, Golf Croquet (first to 7 points around six hoops and the peg), has exploded in club popularity since the 2000s, but Association Croquet remains the standard for international championship play (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
