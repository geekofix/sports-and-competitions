Fell Running

≈ 2 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Fell running (hill running) is the sport of racing off-road over the uplands ("fells") of northern Britain, where the climb is a significant part of the difficulty (Wikipedia, n.d.). It belongs to the mountain-running family with trail running, the vertical kilometer and cross-country running.

Figure: Paul dobson from ENGLAND. (2014-01-01 13:51). Runners at the 2014 Nine Standards Fell Race, Yorkshire [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. CC BY 2.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/2014_Nine_Standards_Fell_Race_Hugh_Symonds.jpg

The sport is governed by the Fell Runners Association (FRA, founded 1970) in England, with separate associations in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland (Wikipedia, n.d.). Races are categorized by distance and ascent (e.g. Ben Nevis Race: 16 km, 1,340 m ascent), run over marked or unmarked terrain with marshals at checkpoints; the fastest time wins, and races often include a "not north of the line" rule on some courses (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Rules

The Ben Nevis Race, run since 1937 (first timed ascent in 1895 by Fort William barber William Swan) from Fort William to the summit (1,345 m) and back, is the most iconic event: up to 600 runners start on the first Saturday of September (Ben Nevis Race, n.d.). Records: men 1:25:34 (Kenny Stuart, 1984), women 1:43:01 (Victoria Wilkinson, 2018) (Wikipedia, n.d.). Equipment is minimal — trail shoes, waterproofs compulsory in some races; FRA rules require kit checks at major races (FRA, n.d.). Men and women race separately in most events with equal prize structures.

History

Fell running has roots in the shepherds’ and miners’ hill races of the Lake District and Pennines in the 19th century; the FRA was founded in 1970 to standardize races and records (Wikipedia, n.d.). Legends include Kenny Stuart (6-time Ben Nevis winner, record holder) and Jasmin Paris, whose 2019 Montane Spine Race exploits while breastfeeding made her a global icon of ultra racing (Wikipedia, n.d.).
