Indoor Cricket

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Indoor cricket is cricket adapted for sports halls and netted courts, with evidence of play in the United Kingdom from the 1920s–30s and a winter boom in the 1960s; the first organised league was founded in North Shropshire in 1970, the first national tournament followed in 1976 with more than 400 clubs, and by 1979 over 1,000 clubs were playing, with major finals at Lord's in London (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Two main forms exist: the British six-a-side hall game and the netted eight-a-side game of Australia and South Africa, whose international rules are published by the World Indoor Cricket Federation (WICF), a form with origins in Western Australia in the late 1970s (World Indoor Cricket Federation, n.d.-c; World Indoor Cricket Federation, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Indoor cricket is played in men's, women's and mixed competitions at every level, from school leagues to international tournaments, so women compete in their own teams and in mixed sides under identical rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; World Indoor Cricket Federation, n.d.-b).

Rules

The British six-a-side version is played in a sports hall: each team has six players, an innings lasts 12 overs of six balls bowled from one end, no bowler may bowl more than three overs, and a batter retires on 25 runs (but may return if the side is all out); a catch is allowed off the side walls and roof if the ball has not touched the floor first (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

The netted WICF game is eight-a-side (minimum six): each innings has 16 overs of eight balls, batters bat in pairs for four overs at a time, and all eight players bowl two overs each; a dismissal costs the batting side five runs but the batter stays in and continues; bonus runs are scored when the ball hits the nets — zone A (front net behind the keeper) 0, zone B (side net, front half) 1, zone C (side net, back half) 2, zone D (back net) 4 on the bounce and 6 on the full, and 3 from zone B or C onto zone D — but only if at least one physical run is also completed; a catch off a net (except a six) before the ball touches the ground is a dismissal (World Indoor Cricket Federation, n.d.-b; World Indoor Cricket Federation, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Most netted competitions use the skins system, in which each four-over batting partnership is a “skin” and the higher-scoring pair wins it, plus optional dot-ball and jackpot-ball rules; the umpire officiates from a raised platform about 3 m high (World Indoor Cricket Federation, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

History

Indoor cricket grew out of winter practice in the United Kingdom in the 1920s–30s and became organised in 1970 with the first league in North Shropshire; the first national tournament in 1976 attracted more than 400 clubs and Lord's hosted major events, while the netted eight-a-side form developed in Western Australia in the late 1970s and spread to South Africa (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; World Indoor Cricket Federation, n.d.-c).

The World Indoor Cricket Federation codified the international netted rules — 16 overs, four-over pairs, the five-run dismissal penalty and the zone bonus system — and international competitions are now staged under its rulebook, alongside Cricket Australia's official indoor rules (World Indoor Cricket Federation, n.d.-b; World Indoor Cricket Federation, n.d.-a).
