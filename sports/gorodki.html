Gorodki

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Gorodki (Russian: "townlets") is a traditional Russian throwing sport in which players throw a wooden bat (up to 85-100 cm) at five wooden cylinders (gorodki) arranged in one of 15 standard figures inside a 2 m x 2 m square ("the city" or gorod), aiming to knock all cylinders out of the square in the fewest throws. It is organized in Russia by the Russian Federation of Gorodki Sport (gorodki-russia.ru) and internationally by the International Federation of Gorodki Sport (IFGS, founded 1993), which holds world championships.

Rules

The playing field is 22 m x 12 m with two throwing lines: the far line ("kon", 13 m from the city) and the near line ("poloukon", 6.5 m). Players throw from kon first; once at least one gorodok is knocked out, throws continue from poloukon until the figure is cleared. The 15 figures (cannon, fork, star, arrow, well, crankshaft, artillery, racquet, machine-gun nest, lobster, watchmen, sickle, shooting gallery, airplane, letter) must be cleared in a set order. The winner clears all 15 figures in the fewest throws; fouls (touching the lines, throwing over 30 seconds, touching the ground in front) add penalty throws or reset the figure. Matches are individual or team (3-5 players per team).

History

Gorodki is documented from at least the 17th century — Peter the Great played it — and was played by Russian cultural figures from Tolstoy and Chekhov to Gorky and Pavlov. It became an organized sport in the USSR in 1923 (first all-Union competition), with official rules finalized in 1933 fixing the 15 figures; it was included in the first Spartakiad of the Peoples of the USSR in 1928 and peaked in popularity in the 1950s-70s, with an estimated 350,000 players. After a decline in the 1990s, the International Federation of Gorodki Sport was founded in 1993 (Moscow) with members including Russia, Belarus, Estonia, Finland, Germany, Latvia, Moldova, Sweden and Ukraine, and world championships are held regularly. The game spread to Finland (as kyykkä) and Sweden.
