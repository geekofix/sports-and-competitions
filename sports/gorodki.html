Gorodki

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Gorodki (Russian: gorodki, "little towns") is a traditional Russian bat-and-pin throwing game: a player throws a wooden bat (bita) at five wooden cylinders (gorodki) arranged in one of 15 standard figures inside a 2 m × 2 m square — the "city" (gorod) — aiming to knock all five pins out of the square in as few throws as possible (International Federation of Gorodki Sport [IFGS], n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). In Russia the sport is governed by the Russian Federation of Gorodki Sport (FGSR, founded 15 January 1993 in St. Petersburg), and internationally by the IFGS, founded in Moscow in 1993 with twelve member countries, which stages annual world championships (Russian Federation of Gorodki Sport, n.d.; IFGS, n.d.; Russian-Sports.ru, n.d.). Both men and women compete, in individual and team events (IFGS, n.d.). The Finnish variant of the game survives as kyykkä (Wikipedia, n.d.).

At the end of 2026 the leading women are the Russian, Belarusian and Baltic throwers who head the women's standings at the annual IFGS World Championships, while on the men's side the title is contested among the top players of the same nations, with Russian athletes setting the benchmark marks of the sport (IFGS, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).

Rules

The playing area is a flat strip about 30 m × 15 m (22 m × 12 m at some venues) with the gorod — a 2 m × 2 m square — at the far end and two throwing lines: the kon ("end line"), 13 m from the gorod, and the polukon ("semi-end line"), 6.5 m from it (IFGS, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Each figure begins with throws from the kon; once at least one pin has been knocked out of the square, the remaining throws are made from the polukon — except the figure "letter" (pis'mo), which is always played from the kon (IFGS, n.d.). The 15 figures — cannon, fork, star, arrow, well, crankshaft, artillery, racquet, machine-gun nest, lobster, watchmen, sickle, shooting gallery, airplane and letter — are cleared in a set order, and the winner is the player, or the team of three to five players, that clears all 15 figures in the fewest throws; the standing record is 15 figures in 17 throws, set in 1974 (IFGS, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The pins are 20 cm long and 4.5–5 cm in diameter, turned from birch or beech; the bat is at most 1 m long (typically 85–100 cm) and is made of oak, hawthorn or dogwood, with metal inserts permitted since 1933 (IFGS, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). A player has at most 30 seconds to prepare and deliver a throw; a throw is lost if the bat touches the foul line or the ground in front of it, or if the thrower steps over the line, and such fouls can add penalty throws or reset the figure (IFGS, n.d.; Russian Federation of Gorodki Sport, n.d.). Men and women compete under identical rules in separate categories, with junior and veteran divisions at national events (IFGS, n.d.).

History

Gorodki is documented in Russia from at least the 17th century and was a favourite pastime of Peter the Great; it was also played by the general Alexander Suvorov and by cultural figures including Leo Tolstoy, Anton Chekhov, Maxim Gorky and the physiologist Ivan Pavlov, as well as by Soviet leaders Lenin and Stalin (Wikipedia, n.d.; Russia Beyond, 2013). The first all-Union championship was held in Moscow on 19 August 1923 — the date celebrated as the birthday of competitive gorodki — and the game appeared at the first Spartakiad of the Peoples of the USSR in 1928; official rules were codified in 1923 and revised in 1933, when 15 figures were fixed out of more than 220 known arrangements and play on hard surfaces was permitted (Russian-Sports.ru, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). From 1936 the All-Union federation organized individual and team championships and awarded sports ranks and titles, and the game reached its peak popularity in the 1950s–1970s with hundreds of thousands of players (Russian-Sports.ru, n.d.). After a decline in the 1980s and 1990s, the International Federation of Gorodki Sport was founded in 1993 and world championships have been held annually since 1994; the game also spread to Finland, where it survives as kyykkä, and to Sweden (IFGS, n.d.; Russia Beyond, 2013).
