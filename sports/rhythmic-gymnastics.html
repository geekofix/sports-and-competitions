Rhythmic Gymnastics

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Rhythmic gymnastics is a judged gymnastics discipline contested by women, in which individual gymnasts or groups of five perform choreographed routines to music with one of five hand apparatus — rope, hoop, ball, clubs and ribbon — combining dance, ballet, flexibility, jumps, balances and complex apparatus handling (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It is governed worldwide by the International Gymnastics Federation (FIG), has been an Olympic women's event since the 1984 Los Angeles Games for the individual all-around, and the group competition was added at Atlanta 1996; world championships have been staged since 1963, when the first edition took place in Budapest (FIG, n.d.). The floor area measures 13 × 13 m, and the eastern European and Asian schools — above all Russia, Bulgaria and Italy in the group ranks — have historically collected most of the titles (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

At the 42nd Rhythmic Gymnastics World Championships, held 12–16 August 2026 at the Festhalle in Frankfurt with about 300 athletes, Germany's Darja Varfolomeev won her third consecutive individual all-around title, ahead of Stiliana Nikolova of Bulgaria and Sofia Raffaeli of Italy, and also took gold with ball and ribbon for her 12th world title overall, while Brazil won both group apparatus finals — the first world titles for a nation from the Americas (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Under the FIG Code of Points, every routine is scored on Difficulty — body difficulty, apparatus difficulty and, in groups, dance steps — and on Execution, which starts from 10.00 and is reduced by deductions; group routines are additionally judged on Artistry (FIG, n.d.). The apparatus are strictly regulated: the hoop is 80–90 cm in diameter, the ball 18–19 cm in diameter and about 400 g, the ribbon 6 m long, the clubs 40–50 cm long, and the rope is proportional to the gymnast's height (FIG, n.d.). An individual routine lasts 75–90 seconds, a group routine 2:15–2:30; individual gymnasts compete with each apparatus in the all-around, and the top eight advance to each apparatus final, while groups perform two apparatus finals after the all-around (FIG, n.d.). At the Olympic Games and world championships the programme is exclusively for women — individual and group — with senior gymnasts aged 16 and over and a junior category for 13- to 15-year-olds; men practice rhythmic gymnastics mainly at grassroots level in a few countries, but the FIG elite programme is women-only (FIG, n.d.). Competitions are run as World Championships, World Cup series and continental championships, with team and individual standings (FIG, n.d.).

History

Rhythmic gymnastics grew out of 19th-century movement systems — Émile Jaques-Dalcroze's eurhythmics and Rudolf von Laban's movement analysis — and was codified as a competitive women's discipline in the Soviet Union and eastern Europe in the 1930s–1950s (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first World Championships were held in 1963 in Budapest, and the sport joined the Olympic programme in 1984 at Los Angeles with the individual all-around, with the group event added at Atlanta 1996 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Among the icons of the sport are Russia's Evgenia Kanaeva (born 1990), the first gymnast to defend the Olympic all-around title (Beijing 2008 and London 2012), Alina Kabaeva (born 1983), Olympic champion at Athens 2004 and multiple world all-around champion, the Azerbaijani-born pair of twin sisters Dina and Arina Averina (born 1998), with Arina taking Olympic gold at Tokyo 2020, and Yana Kudryavtseva (born 1997), who collected 13 world titles before retiring in 2017 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
