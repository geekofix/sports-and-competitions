Fire-Ball Soccer

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Fire-ball soccer (also called fire football or flaming-ball football) is an informal street and beach game in which a football-sized ball is wrapped in cloth soaked in a flammable liquid, set on fire, and then kicked around like a normal football — the fire is usually extinguished before the kick or the ball is kicked while burning, depending on the local variant (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is a novelty and party game rather than an organized sport: there is no world federation, no official rules and no regular competitions; it derives from the tradition of playing with burning balls, most famously the Purépecha pasáratakua fire-ball game of Michoacán, Mexico, see Fire Hockey and Pelota Purépecha on this site (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Because a burning ball can cause serious burns, the game is only safe with wet balls, fire-extinguishing water nearby and adult supervision, and it is not recommended for children (Wikipedia, n.d.). As a street variant it belongs to the informal side of the football family — Football, Beach Soccer, Swamp Soccer, Futsal and the other variants listed under Important Links.

The game is played equally by men and women at informal gatherings, beaches and festivals, in mixed teams without any classification, simply for entertainment (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Rules

There is no codified rule set; local variants share a common pattern: a ball (usually an old football or a ball of rags) is wrapped in absorbent cloth, soaked with kerosene, petrol or lighter fluid, set alight and placed on open ground away from flammable materials, and players then play a casual game of Football — five-a-side or small-sided — with the burning ball, typically 5–10 minutes per half (Wikipedia, n.d.). In some variants the ball is doused with water immediately before kick-off so that only smoke and sparks remain; in others it is genuinely kicked while burning. Goals, kick-ins and throw-ins follow ordinary football conventions, with no offside in most street versions (Wikipedia, n.d.). Safety rules that responsible organizers apply are: play only on sand, wet grass or bare earth; keep a bucket of water and a fire extinguisher at the touchline; never use synthetic balls that can melt and drip burning plastic; and stop play immediately if the ball splits open (Wikipedia, n.d.). There are no age, gender or skill classifications — everyone plays at their own risk, and formal youth play is discouraged because of the burn hazard (Wikipedia, n.d.).

History

Playing with fire has ancient roots in fire festivals across Europe and in the Mesoamerican burning-ball games, but fire-ball soccer as a street pastime became known in the late 20th century, when videos of young people kicking flaming balls on beaches and at parties spread through early internet video platforms (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Mexican Purépecha pasáratakua — a hockey-like game with a burning wooden ball played at night — was broadcast internationally in the 1980s and inspired imitations in which a football was set on fire (Wikipedia, n.d.). No governing body was ever founded; the "sport" exists only as a recreational stunt, and many fire departments and safety authorities explicitly warn against it because of severe burn injuries (Wikipedia, n.d.).
