Natural Track Luge

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Natural track luge (German: Naturbahn) is the original form of luge, raced on tracks adapted from existing mountain roads and paths, packed with snow and naturally iced, with no artificial banking or refrigeration (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is governed by the International Luge Federation (FIL), which also governs artificial-track luge, and it is not an Olympic discipline — at the Winter Games only artificial tracks are used (International Luge Federation, n.d.-a). Natural track racing is slower than kunstbahn luge but more physical and technical, because riders must brake hard and steer actively over rough, unbanked curves. Women compete in their own singles discipline and in the mixed team relay, just as the men do (International Luge Federation, n.d.-b).

Rules

Natural tracks are typically 400 to 1,200 m long, with a level (unbanked) cross-section, a maximum average gradient of about 13 percent and lane widths of at least 3 m; curves have a minimum radius of about 7 m and are delimited with wooden bands or foam mats (International Luge Federation, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.). Competitions are held in men's singles, women's singles, men's doubles and a mixed team relay, usually over two runs with the combined time deciding the result — doubles and the team event race once (International Luge Federation, n.d.-b). Sleds are simpler than artificial-track sleds, with runners and a seat, and athletes wear helmets and aerodynamic suits; timing is electronic to hundredths of a second (International Luge Federation, n.d.-b). The FIL World Luge Natural Track Championships have been held almost biennially since 1979, and the European Championships since 1970, with men's and women's events under identical regulations (International Luge Federation, n.d.-a).

History

Natural track luge is the ancient form of the sport: sledding on natural mountain tracks existed for centuries before the first artificial tracks were built in the 19th century, and racing on packed snow roads was a popular winter pastime in the Alpine regions of Austria, Italy, Germany and Switzerland (Wikipedia, n.d.). Organized natural track racing grew in the mid-20th century with national championships and the first international events, and the FIL — founded in 1957 as the world governing body of luge — took over governance of both artificial and natural track disciplines, holding the first FIL World Luge Natural Track Championships in 1979 (International Luge Federation, n.d.-a). Because the Olympic program has used only artificial tracks since 1964, natural track luge remains a non-Olympic but fiercely competitive discipline, dominated traditionally by Austria, Italy and Germany (Wikipedia, n.d.).
