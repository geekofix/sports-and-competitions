Hang Gliding

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Hang gliding is the air sport of flying a foot-launched flexible wing (hang glider), controlled by shifting body weight, using thermals and ridge lift to soar. It is governed internationally by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI, hang gliding under Section 1 of its Sporting Code), which sets world records and runs the FAI Hang Gliding World Championships (biennial since 1979); national clubs include the British Hang Gliding and Paragliding Association (BHPA) and the US Hang Gliding and Paragliding Association (USHPA).

Rules

At FAI Category 1 events (World and continental championships), tasks are either distance tasks (greatest straight distance, goal-and-return, or free distance around turnpoints) or race-to-goal tasks, flown with GNSS loggers that record the flight; scoring is automatic from the logger track. Pilots must hold a national pilot license (e.g., BHPA pilot rating, USHPA H2-H5) and pass medical and airworthiness requirements; gliders must be certified (EN/LTF or HGMA). FAI records cover straight distance (over 700 km), altitude gain and goal-and-return distance. Competitions use launch windows and start gates (cylinder or race start).

History

Modern hang gliding began in 1969-1972 in the US when Bill Moyes and others flew Rogallo-style flexible wings (the "batwing"), building on Francis Rogallo's NASA parawing research from the 1940s-60s; the first organized meet was in 1972 in the US, and the FAI has recognized hang gliding records since the 1970s. Otto Lilienthal's earlier experiments (1890s) are considered the precursor. The FAI Hang Gliding World Championships have been held biennially since the late 1970s. Record holders include Manfred Ruhmer of Austria, whose straight-distance record exceeds 700 km. Today paragliding (ram-air wings) is the more popular sister sport.
