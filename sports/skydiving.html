Skydiving

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Skydiving — sport parachuting — is the aerial sport of exiting an aircraft, falling through the sky in controlled freefall, and then deploying a steerable parachute to fly to a landing on a target. As an organised sport governed by the FAI's International Skydiving Commission (ISC, historically the IPC), its competitive disciplines span formation skydiving (belly and vertical), artistic events (freestyle and freefly), canopy piloting, style and accuracy, speed skydiving and — since 2014 — indoor skydiving in vertical wind tunnels, with world championships and world cups staged annually.

Rules

In formation skydiving, a team of four or eight flyers (plus a videographer) works through a drawn sequence of prescribed formations in a defined working time (35 seconds for outdoor 4-way, with points per completed formation) — scored on the number of correct formations built, with grips held correctly in each random or block formation; vertical formation skydiving flies the same logic head-down or head-up. Artistic events pair a performer and camera flyer: freestyle is judged on compulsory and free routines like aerial gymnastics, freefly mixes vertical orientations, and canopy piloting (swooping) tests speed, distance and accuracy of the parachute landing across courses including a waterside course. Style and accuracy lands freefall acrobatics combined with precision landings on a 2-centimetre dead centre target disc, and speed skydiving measures the highest average vertical speed in freefall (over 500 km/h in elite competition). All disciplines are contested under FAI competition rules with mandatory safety equipment, mandated deployment altitudes, and licenses issued by national bodies such as the USPA.

History

Competitive parachuting developed after the Second World War, when surplus equipment and military-trained jumpers seeded clubs in Europe and America; the FAI began administering international parachuting (record-setting and championships) in the 1950s\u20131970s, and the classic disciplines — style, accuracy and 4-way formation skydiving (once called "relative work") — were the sport's early core, with freefly and freestyle appearing as artistic events in the 1990s. Skydiving's golden age of mass participation came with commercial jump operations of the 1970s\u20132000s, and breakthroughs in equipment — ram-air square canopies, automatic activation devices and student-friendly tandem systems — made freefall accessible to millions; disciplines continued to evolve with canopy piloting (swooping) courses and vertical formation skydiving drawn from the freefly revolution. In 2014 the FAI added indoor skydiving to its competition structure, with the first FAI Indoor Skydiving World Cup held at Austin, Texas, in November 2014 and the first world championships following in Prague in 2015 — the wind-tunnel disciplines (formation, freestyle, dynamic) now attracting young athletes who train without ever leaving the ground while feeding the outdoor sport with talent. Today world championships in outdoor and indoor skydiving are staged annually, with traditional powers in France, Belgium, the United States, Russia (suspended from 2022 FAI events) and several Arab and Asian federations.
