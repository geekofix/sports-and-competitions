Vinkensport

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Vinkensport (vinkenzetting, "finch setting") is the traditional Flemish sport of chaffinch song contests: male chaffinches sit in individual cloth-covered cages lined up in a row, and over one hour each bird tries to deliver the most complete song phrases ending in the characteristic "susk-e-wiet", tallied by referees on a wooden stick (Wikipedia, n.d.; Natuurpunt, n.d.). Women take part as vinkeniers on equal terms with men — breeding, training and entering their own birds — and the sport's societies cooperate with animal-welfare organizations on cage, season and capture standards (Natuurpunt, n.d.). Organized in Flanders since the sixteenth century — documented from 1596, with some sources claiming 1593 — it counts more than 13,000 vinkeniers today and remains the subject of an active animal-welfare debate over the keeping of songbirds (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Within the lawn and throwing games family on this site, its closest relatives are Kubb, Molkky, Horseshoes, Cornhole, Washer Toss; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

In a vinkenzetting the cages are hung in a long row about 2.4 m (six feet) apart so the birds hear but do not see their rivals; the match lasts exactly one hour, started and ended by a red flag, and referees walk the row chalking each bird's valid songs onto a wooden stick that holds up to 800 marks (Wikipedia, n.d.). Only complete songs ending in "susk-e-wiet" count — cut-short phrases score nothing — and the bird with the highest tally at the final whistle wins the contest, with champions crowned as the "king finch" of their category; the standing record is 1,278 valid songs in one hour, set in 2007 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Rules govern cage dimensions and the cloth covers on three sides, the spacing of the row, rest requirements and the singing season; blinding of birds — once used to make them sing more — was banned by law in 1921 after a 1920 campaign by blinded First World War veterans, and the EU ban on capturing wild birds (1979), upheld by the Belgian Constitutional Court in 2002, means all contest birds are now bred in captivity (Natuurpunt, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Caribbean variants in Guyana, Trinidad, Suriname and Brazil place the cages only about one foot apart and play to 50 songs (Wikipedia, n.d.).

History

Bird-song contests have been documented in Flemish and Dutch culture since the sixteenth century, with chaffinches chosen because the male's fixed song phrase allows clean counting; the organized vinkensport of today traces to the Flemish merchant culture of 1596 (some sources say 1593) (Wikipedia, n.d.). The sport survived both world wars and dramatic rule changes: blinding was outlawed in 1921, doping accusations (testosterone-treated birds) and even a CD-player cheat have marked its modern era, and a dedicated vinkensport museum operated in Hulste until 2010 (Wikipedia, n.d.). Today more than 13,000 vinkeniers breed around 10,000 birds a year (2007 estimate), contests remain strongest in Flanders and Zeeland, and the future of the sport is debated between defenders who call it living heritage and animal-welfare opponents who question the keeping of songbirds in cages (Natuurpunt, n.d.; BBC, n.d.).
