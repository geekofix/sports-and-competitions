Dhanurveda

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Dhanurveda (Sanskrit: dhanus “bow” + veda “knowledge” — “science of archery”) is the ancient Indian treatise and living tradition on archery and the martial science of war, traditionally regarded as an upaveda attached to the Yajurveda and attributed to Bhrigu, Vishvamitra or Bharadwaja. It codifies bow- and arrow-making, training, and rules of engagement — including the injunctions that it was a sin to shoot a warrior in the back or to fight more than one warrior at a time. The Dhanurveda section of the Agni Purana (chapters 248–251, 8th–11th centuries) is one of the earliest extant manuals of Indian martial arts, cataloguing nine fighting stances, 32 sword-and-shield positions, and weapons from the bow and chakram to the mace. Traditional Indian archery lives on in competitions governed by the Archery Association of India; women compete on equal terms in modern Indian archery (e.g., Deepika Kumari, Ankita Bhakat).

Within the target and precision sports on this site, its closest relatives are archery, mounted-archery and run-archery; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

Dhanurveda classifies weapons into thrown and unthrown (muktamuktam) categories and trains five classes of warriors — charioteers, elephant-riders, horsemen, infantry and wrestlers — with nine named stances (samapada, vaisakhha, mandala and others) and detailed archery technique: bow types (vakra “curved”), string (jya) strung only when needed, arrow (ishu), quiver (ishudhi) and leather arm-guard (hastaghna). Later manuals (Brhat Sarngadhara Paddhati, 15th century) add target progression and breath control (pranayama/kumbhaka) for the release. Modern competitive archery follows World Archery rules; the traditional forms are demonstrated at cultural festivals.

History

Vedic hymns (Rigveda, Yajurveda, Atharvaveda) celebrate the bow, and the Mahabharata's heroes — Arjuna, Karna, Bhishma, Drona, Ekalavya — are legendary archers; the Charanavyuha of Shaunaka lists dhanurveda among the four upavedas. Nearchus, chronicler of Alexander's campaign in India, described the Indian bamboo bow that could pierce any armour of antiquity. The Kuvalayamala (8th century) records gurukula training in archery alongside sword and dagger fighting. Indian martial traditions codified in the Agni Purana and later texts influenced modern Indian archery, which today is an Olympic and world-level sport.
