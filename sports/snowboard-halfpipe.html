Snowboard Halfpipe

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Snowboard halfpipe is the oldest Olympic snowboard discipline: riders drop into a snow-built semi-circular channel \u2014 today's pipes have walls around 22 feet (6.7 m) high \u2014 carve from wall to wall and launch above the coping to perform spins, flips and grabs, each run judged on amplitude, difficulty, execution and style. It was one of the two events when snowboarding entered the Winter Olympics at Nagano 1998, and its Olympic champions \u2014 from Gian Simmen and Nicola Thost to Shaun White, Ayumu Hirano and Chloe Kim \u2014 are the sport's biggest names.

Rules

A competition halfpipe is roughly 150\u2013170 metres long with a fall-line gradient of about 17\u201318 degrees and walls ~22 feet high; riders take runs of constructed tricks from wall to wall \u2014 in Olympic finals, three runs with the best counting, each scored 0\u2013100 by judges on difficulty, execution, amplitude and overall impression (a fall or hand-down heavily penalises the run, and higher amplitude \u2014 riding out above the coping \u2014 is itself scored). Tricks are named by rotation (e.g. 1080s, 1440s, double corks) and grip/hand variations; FIS rules fix pipe dimensions, forbid waxing tricks and require helmets. Qualification/final formats seed athletes through judged runs; younger rider-development events use smaller pipes, but 22-foot "superpipes" are the elite standard and are shared with freeski halfpipe world cups and the X Games.

History

The halfpipe was snowboarding's crucible: the first halfpipes were dug at American resorts in the early-to-mid 1980s as surf- and skate-influenced riders transplanted skateboarding's vert ramps into snow, with independent contests soon following and the discipline forming the heart of the early professional tours. The International Snowboard Federation and then FIS (which took over Olympic governance in 1994\u201394) ran the discipline into the Olympics at Nagano 1998, where Switzerland's Gian Simmen and Germany's Nicola Thost became the first halfpipe Olympic champions and Ross Rebagliati's adjacent giant slalom gold made headlines; Shaun White's dynasty (2006, 2010, 2018 titles), Iouri Podladtchikov's 2014 YOLO flip win, Ayumu Hirano's back-to-back-to-back progression toward his 2022 gold, and Chloe Kim's women's dynasty (2018 and 2022) followed, with riders such as Australia's Scotty James and the USA's Maddie Mastro pushing amplitude, and the X Games meanwhile staging the discipline's most-watched invitationals. Halfpipe's history was added to by the freeskiing community, which adopted the pipe identically for its own Olympic discipline from Sochi 2014 \u2014 David Wise's titles \u2014 making the 22-foot snow channel the shared cathedral of both freestyle families.
