Real Tennis

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Real tennis — called court tennis in the United States and royal tennis in Britain and Australia — is the original racquet sport from which modern tennis descends, played on an enclosed, asymmetric indoor court with sloping penthouse roofs, spectator galleries, a tambour buttress and a net, where points are won through openings and chases rather than plain winners (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Only about 50 courts remain active worldwide, in the United Kingdom, United States, France and Australia, yet the game keeps a professional circuit and two world championships: the men's Real Tennis World Championship, the oldest world championship in any sport, and a women's championship contested since 1985 (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The current women's world champion is Claire Fahey, who defended her Ladies World Championship title in May 2025 at the International Tennis Hall of Fame in Newport, Rhode Island, for a record eighth consecutive victory in the 21st edition, and the current men's champion is Camden Riviere of the United States, who has held the title since 2022 and defended it at the 70th edition in 2025 at Newport, beating John Lumley 7 sets to 1; his next defence is scheduled for September 2027 at the Racquet Club of Philadelphia (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Real Tennis News, n.d.). Governing bodies are the International Real Tennis Professionals Association (IRTPA) for the professional game and the Tennis & Rackets Association in Britain (Tennis & Rackets Association, n.d.).

Rules

A jeu à dedans court measures roughly 110 by 39 feet overall, with a playing floor of about 96 by 32 feet; the penthouse roofs slope along three walls and beneath them sit the galleries with netted openings, the largest being the dedans behind the server, plus the tambour buttress and the grille (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The serve is struck from the service end onto the side penthouse so that it rebounds onto the hazard side of the net, and points are won by striking the ball into the winning openings — the dedans, the grille, the winning gallery — or by letting it bounce twice on the hazard side between the chase lines, and lost through faults such as serving into the net or out (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The signature rule is the chase: when a ball bounces twice untouched, the marker notes the second bounce against floor lines, and the chase is replayed from the other end, the point going to whoever places the ball nearer the chase (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Scoring uses the 15-30-40 and deuce system borrowed later by lawn tennis, a game and set cannot be concluded while chases are outstanding, a marker and a referee officiate with the marker's word final, matches are normally best of three or five sets, and the World Championship challenge is best of 13 sets over three days with a challenger chosen through eliminators among the top four of the World Race (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Handicap play via bisques is common in club tennis, and women compete in all events including the Ladies World Championship (Tennis & Rackets Association, n.d.).

History

The game grew out of the medieval jeu de paume played bare-handed and later with gloves and rackets in French monastery courtyards, taken up by French and English aristocracy, and by the 16th century purpose-built courts stood beside palaces such as the Louvre (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The Royal Tennis Court at Hampton Court Palace, built for Cardinal Wolsey between 1526 and 1529 and played on by Henry VIII (the present court dates from 1625), is the oldest court still in use, and Falkland Palace in Scotland (1539–41) holds the oldest jeu quarré court (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first recorded world champion was Clergé the Elder in the 1740s (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). In the 19th century the game nearly vanished until lawn tennis — invented in 1874 and borrowing real tennis's scoring, serve rotation and name — swept the world, leaving the elder game a continuous but small tradition (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The modern era was dominated by Australia's Robert Fahey, who held the men's title from 1994 to 2016, winning it twelve times, and regained it in 2018; Steve Virgona and Tim Chisholm share the doubles record with six world titles each (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Real Tennis News, n.d.).
