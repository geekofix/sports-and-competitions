Real Tennis

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Real tennis — also called court tennis in the United States and royal tennis in Britain and Australia — is the original racquet sport from which modern tennis descends, played on an enclosed, asymmetric indoor court with sloping penthouse roofs, galleries, a tambour buttress and a net that is higher on one end than the other. Only about 45 courts remain active worldwide, yet the game retains a professional circuit, a world championship and devoted amateur followings in Britain, France, Australia and the United States.

Rules

Courts (of the jeu à dedans type) measure roughly 110 by 39 feet overall, with a playing floor of about 96 by 32 feet; the penthouse roofs slope along three walls, and beneath them sit galleries with netted openings, the largest being the dedans behind the server. The serve is struck from the service end onto the side penthouse, so that it rebounds onto the hazard side of the net, and points are won by striking the ball into the winning openings (the dedans, the grille and the winning gallery), or by making it bounce twice on the hazard side between chase lines, and lost through faults such as serving into the net or out of court. The game's signature rule is the chase: when a ball bounces twice without being hit, the marker notes the second bounce relative to lines on the floor, and the players must later replay the chase from the other end, with the point decided by who places the ball nearer the chase. Scoring uses the 15-30-40 and deuce system, and games and sets cannot be concluded while chases remain outstanding; a marker and a referee officiate, and the marker's word on chases and score is final. Matches are normally best of three or five sets, with handicap play via bisques common in club tennis.

History

The game grew out of the medieval jeu de paume, played bare-handed and later with gloves and rackets in French monastery courtyards before the French and English aristocracy took it up; the penthouse roofs, galleries and chases record that courtyard heritage, and by the 16th century purpose-built courts stood beside palaces such as the Louvre. The Royal Tennis Court at Hampton Court Palace, built for Cardinal Wolsey between 1526 and 1529 and played on by Henry VIII, is the oldest tennis court still in use; Falkland Palace in Scotland (built for James V, 1539-41) is the oldest court of the unique jeu quarré design. In the 19th century the game nearly vanished before "lawn tennis" — invented in 1874 and borrowing real tennis's scoring, serve rotation and name — swept the world, leaving the elder game a small but continuous tradition organized under bodies such as the Tennis and Rackets Association in Britain and the International Real Tennis Professionals Association. The Real Tennis World Championship, challenged on a challenge-match basis, has been dominated in the modern era by Australia's Robert Fahey, who held the title from 1994 to 2016 and won it twelve times, with previous champions such as Chris Ronaldson and Wayne Davis before him; a Ladies' World Championship has been contested since 1985.
