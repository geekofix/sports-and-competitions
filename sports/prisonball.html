Prisonball

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Prisonball is a variation of dodgeball in which players who are hit are not eliminated but sent to a "prison", "jail" or "doghouse" behind the opposing team's back line, from which they can be released and rejoin the game. It is one of the most widely played schoolyard dodgeball variants, known by many names worldwide — prison dodgeball, jailball, jailbreak, battleball, trench, teamball, king's court in Canada, queimada in Brazil and balle au prisonnier in France.

Rules

Play starts like standard dodgeball: two teams face each other across a center line, and a player hit by a thrown ball, or whose throw is caught, is out — but instead of leaving the game, the player goes to a marked prison area behind the opposing team. The release rules vary by local ruleset, and this variation is the heart of the game: a common standard is that a prisoner is freed when a teammate throws a ball into the prison and a prisoner catches it, sometimes freeing all prisoners at once. In other versions the prisoner must hit an opposing player with a thrown ball to earn release, in which case that opponent goes to prison in turn; in the "ghost" version no one is released, but prisoners may collect balls and attack from behind while staying out of the court, so teams end up attacked from all sides. In basketball-court versions, sinking a dodgeball into the opposing basket frees all of a team's prisoners. The game ends when all members of one team are in prison; headshots are commonly banned, and hits do not count if the ball touches the ground first.

History

Prisonball descends directly from the folk tradition of dodgeball-type schoolyard and gym games that spread through American and European physical-education programs in the 20th century, with prison-style variants documented in physical-education literature for decades. The German Völkerball ("nation ball"), played with a single ball and a "king" who is jailed first and returns when all teammates are imprisoned, is essentially the same game known in continental Europe since the 19th century, and French balle au prisonnier is its standard school form. In the United States the variant thrives in schoolyards and summer camps under countless regional names, while formalized dodgeball organizations, such as the World Dodgeball Federation, play their own standardized rules. The game's enduring appeal lies in its second chances: no player is truly out, and a well-thrown rescue ball can swing a whole match.
