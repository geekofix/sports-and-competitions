Wheelchair Handball

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Wheelchair handball is the adaptation of team handball for wheelchair athletes — played by two teams of six (or seven in some rule sets) on a regular handball court with a smaller ball allowed in some versions, aiming to throw the ball into the opponent's goal while moving the chair as the wheelchair athlete's mobility tool. The sport exists in both six-a-side indoor versions and small-sided demonstration formats, played mainly at club and international tournament level in Europe and the Americas.

Unlike the other wheelchair Paralympic sports, wheelchair handball is developing rather than established on the Paralympic programme, so its rules and events are governed by national federations and the International Handball Federation's adaptive structures rather than a fully independent world federation.

Rules

The basic framework follows indoor handball: goals of the same dimensions at each end, a ball advanced by dribbling or passing (in the chair version dribbled by placing the ball on the lap between pushes), creases protecting the goalkeeper and no physical contact beyond what standing handball permits. Adapted regulations cover how many pushes may be made per bounce and how to handle the chair-handball transitions, with six field players plus a goalkeeper used and typically two halves of fifteen to twenty minutes. The specific competition rules vary between national bodies because the sport remains a development discipline, without a single lockstep international code.

History

Wheelball versions of handball developed in several countries during the 1970s and 1980s as part of the broader movement of adapted sports for athletes with disabilities, played at national disability sport events and demonstration tournaments. The International Handball Federation has promoted and tutored the discipline in recent years towards standardisation and inclusion at international multi-sport events, with international friendly tournaments and development campaigns helping establish common rules, and the sport continues its efforts towards major game recognition.
