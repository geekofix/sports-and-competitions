Hare and Hounds

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Hare and hounds — also called paper chase or "hunt the fox/hare" — is a cross-country running game in which a "hare" sets off ahead of a pack of "hounds" and lays a trail of paper, chalk or sawdust, which the hounds must follow; if they catch the hare before the finish, they "kill" the scent and win, and if the hare reaches the finish first the hounds are "lost" (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The game has been played in English public schools since the Elizabethan era — Shakespeare's Hamlet jokes about "hide, fox, and all after" — and around 1800 Shrewsbury School's "the Hunt" (later the Royal Shrewsbury School Hunt, the oldest cross-country running club, with records from 1831 and an establishment date of 1819) formalized the hounds, while at Bath the runners were called "hares", a usage popularized by Tom Brown's School Days in 1857 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-c). The oldest adult club is the Thames Hare and Hounds, founded in 1868 by Walter Rye, which meets on Wimbledon Common and has hosted the Oxford–Cambridge Varsity Match since 1880; the modern offshoot is the Hash House Harriers, founded in Kuala Lumpur in 1938 by British expatriates, which now has thousands of chapters worldwide (Hash House Harriers, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Packs are mixed-gender today, and women's hare-and-hounds clubs run their own races alongside the men's (Wikipedia, n.d.-a) (Wikipedia, n.d.-c).

Rules

The hare — or hares — gets a head start of typically 5 to 15 minutes and lays a "scent" trail of torn paper, chalk marks or sawdust on a course of roughly 5–10 km; the hounds start from the same point after the head start and must follow the trail, keeping together as a pack and calling "On! On!" when on the scent, "Tally ho!" on sighting the hare, "Forward!" to press on and "Lost!" when the trail is lost (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first hound to touch the hare wins the "kill" for the pack; if the hare reaches the designated finish first, the hounds are "lost" and the hare wins; the hare must not hide off the trail or use roads improperly, and false trails may be laid within the rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In the school tradition the start is by whistle with the hare given a fixed head start, and the run ends at a set finish; in Hash House Harriers chapters the format is looser, with a "hash" trail often ending at a pub, and men's, women's and mixed packs all run (Hash House Harriers, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Women compete in their own packs and in mixed packs under identical rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

History

Paper-chase running in English schools dates to the 16th century, and the two great school traditions emerged around 1800: Shrewsbury's "the Hounds" (the Royal Shrewsbury School Hunt, founded 1819, records from 1831) and Bath's "the hares", the latter popularized by the 1857 novel Tom Brown's School Days, in which the famous "paper chase" appears (Wikipedia, n.d.-a, n.d.-c). The Thames Hare and Hounds was founded in 1868 by Walter Rye as the first adult cross-country club, meeting on Wimbledon Common, where the annual Oxford–Cambridge Varsity Match has been held since 1880 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The Hash House Harriers were founded in Kuala Lumpur in 1938 by a group of British expatriates — including Geoffrey Bennett — who named the club after the "Hash House" dining hall where they met, and the movement spread worldwide after the Second World War (Hash House Harriers, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
