Hare and Hounds

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Hare and Hounds (also called the paper chase) is the traditional British cross-country running game in which one or two runners (the "hares") get a head start and lay a trail of torn paper ("scent"), while the pursuing runners (the "hounds") follow the trail and try to catch the hares before the finish. It is the ancestor of modern cross-country running and hash running; famous clubs include Thames Hare and Hounds (founded 1868, the oldest adult cross-country club in the world) and the Royal Shrewsbury School Hunt (records from 1831).

Rules

The hares start 5-15 minutes before the hounds, running a winding course over fields and lanes while dropping paper shreds at intervals (and sometimes false trails or "checks"). The hounds start later and must follow the scent exactly — shortcuts are not allowed — calling "On! On!" (from the fox-hunting cry "Tally ho!") when they find the trail. The first hound to catch (touch) a hare wins the "kill"; the caught hare (or the hare who reaches the finish) chooses the next hare. Races are typically several miles over open country, or run to a time limit. In modern club rules, the Huntsman and Whips direct the field, and chalk marks are used in urban settings.

History

The game dates to at least the 16th century in English public schools (Shakespeare alludes to it in Hamlet: "Hide, fox, and all after"); around 1800 Shrewsbury School organized it as "the Hunt," and Bath School's hare-coursing analogy gave us the "hare" terminology, popularized by Tom Brown's School Days (1857). Thames Hare and Hounds was founded in 1868 by members of Thames Rowing Club, and in 1938 British immigrants founded the Hash House Harriers in Kuala Lumpur on the same principle — the hash runs now exist worldwide. Hare and Hounds directly evolved into organized cross-country racing: the first Crick Run was in 1837, and the first national cross-country championship in England was 1867.
