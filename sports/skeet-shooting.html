Skeet Shooting

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Skeet shooting is one of the three major disciplines of competitive clay-target shooting, in which shotgunners break clay targets ("birds") launched from two fixed houses at opposite ends of a semicircular range featuring eight shooting stations. Its Olympic form, international or "Olympic skeet", has been on the Games programme on and off since 1968 and is governed by the International Shooting Sport Federation (ISSF).

Rules

A skeet field is a semicircle of seven stations spaced along the arc between a high house and a low house, with the eighth station mid-way between them; targets emerge from the houses at set points, crossing overhead at a fixed angle and height between the two houses. In American-format skeet, a round of 25 targets includes singles and doubles at specified stations plus the option of the 25th; in Olympic skeet, athletes shoot a qualification round of 125 targets (men) or 75 (women), with staggered release delays of up to three seconds after the athlete's call — the crucial Olympic innovation — and finals contested for medals on the highest totals. Scoring counts a "hit" only when the target visibly breaks; shotgun gauges are restricted (12-gauge is standard) and gun movement before target release is prohibited, requiring intense reaction and precision. Skeet differs from Olympic trap in having crossing targets and two release points rather than outgoing angled targets from a single trench.

History

Skeet was invented in 1920s America — around 1920, grouse hunter Charles Davis created "shooting around the clock" in Andover, Massachusetts, placing targets on all compass points, and by 1926 the format had been refined, renamed (a competition to coin a name yielded "skeet", from the Scandinavian for "shoot") and standardised into the semicircular field still used; the National Sportsman magazine and its successor bodies spread the game, and the National Skeet Shooting Association was formed in 1946. In international form, skeet entered the Olympic Games at Mexico City 1968 as a mixed open event and remained so through Atlanta 1996; its most celebrated moment came at Barcelona 1992, when China's Zhang Shan won the open gold against the men — the only woman ever to top an Olympic shooting podium against male rivals. The event was subsequently gender-split, and a dedicated women's skeet competition joined the Olympic programme at Sydney 2000, won by Kim Rhode of the United States, who went on to become the first athlete to win individual medals at six consecutive Summer Olympic Games. Today the ISSF governs Olympic skeet, alongside national bodies like the NSSA and UK bodies for the American-style club game, and skeet alongside trap and sporting clays forms the backbone of clay-target sport worldwide.
