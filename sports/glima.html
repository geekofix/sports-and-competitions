Glima

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Glima (Icelandic: glíma, "wrestling") is the traditional Icelandic folk wrestling style — brókartök — in which two standing wrestlers grip each other's belt and trousers and try to throw the opponent so that any part of the body between elbow and knee touches the ground, with a clean fall flat on the back (both shoulder blades) being the perfect result (Íþrótta- og Ólympíusamband Íslands [ÍSí], n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Glima has Viking-era origins and is first mentioned in the 1325 law code Jónsbók; the first modern trouser-grip competition in Iceland was organized in 1888 by the wrestling society Skjaldarglína Ármann, and the belt was introduced in 1905, with the first Grettisbelt (championship belt) competition in 1906 — its winner was crowned the "Glima King" of Iceland (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; ÍSí, n.d.-a). Glima was a demonstration sport at the 1912 Stockholm Olympics (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Today it is governed in Iceland by ÍSí and internationally promoted through ethnosport organizations; the annual Icelandic Glima Championships (Íslandsglímumeistaramót) award the Grettisbelt (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

At the end of 2026 the leading women are the top Icelandic glima wrestlers in the women's division of the national championships — women's glima has grown strongly since the 2010s with dedicated women's belts and international women's tournaments (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). On the men's side, the reigning Glima King is the winner of the most recent Grettisbelt tournament; the 2004–2012 era was dominated by Pétur Eyþórsson, also an international grappling champion (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Under the rules of the Icelandic Glima Association, wrestlers start standing, circle each other clockwise in the "waltz," and grip with one hand in the opponent's belt and the other in the trousers at the thigh — only these grips are legal (ÍSí, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The bout ends when one wrestler touches the ground with any body part between elbow and knee — landing on hands or feet does not count — or when a clean fall flat on the back (both shoulder blades touching simultaneously) is achieved; there is no ground fighting, and pushing the opponent forcefully to the ground is against the rules — the throw must come from a technique (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The classical system has eight main techniques (bragð) producing around 50 throws, and the code of honour drengskapur (fair play) governs conduct (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Bouts are officiated by a referee; the annual Icelandic championship awards the Grettisbelt to the overall winner, with weight and age classes at national events (ÍSí, n.d.-a). Men and women compete in separate divisions with identical grips and rules — women's glima now has its own championship belt and international tournaments (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

History

Glima is first recorded in the Icelandic law code Jónsbók of 1325, and sagas describe wrestling contests from the Viking Age (c. 800–1050); the oldest preserved description of a glima bout is in the sagas, where the hero Grettir Ásmundarson fought a berserk (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Modern competitive glima began in 1888 when the society Skjaldarglína Ármann organized the first trouser-grip competition in Iceland — held almost annually since — and in 1905 the belt was introduced, leading to the first Grettisbelt championship in 1906; the winner became the "Glima King" (Glímukóngur) of Iceland (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Glima was demonstrated at the 1912 Stockholm Olympics, and the Icelandic Glima Association (founded 1923) codified the rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Among the icons are Jónhannes Jósefsson (1883–1968), who published the first book on glima in 1908 and competed at the 1908 London Olympics in Greco-Roman wrestling, finishing fourth, and Pétur Eyþórsson, Glima King from 2004 to 2012 and a world champion in submission grappling (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
