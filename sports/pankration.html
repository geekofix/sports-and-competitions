Pankration

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Pankration (from the Greek pan, "all", and kratos, "power") was the full-contact unarmed combat sport of ancient Greece, combining boxing, wrestling, kicking, joint locks and chokes on the ground — the closest ancient equivalent of modern mixed martial arts (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). It was introduced at the 33rd Olympic Games in 648 BC, where the only prohibited techniques were biting and eye-gouging (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Today the name is revived for a modern full-contact combat sport with weight classes and protective equipment, recognized since 2010 by United World Wrestling (UWW), which runs World Pankration Championships with men's and women's divisions (United World Wrestling, n.d.). It is related to wrestling, boxing, kickboxing and Brazilian jiu-jitsu.

Rules

In the ancient Olympic pankration there were almost no rules: everything was allowed except biting and gouging the eyes, bouts had no time limit and no weight classes, and a fight ended only by submission (the loser raised a finger) or knockout; competitors fought in a dug-out pit called the skamma (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Modern pankration, as practiced under UWW and at the World Pankration Championships, is a regulated full-contact sport: bouts take place in a mat area or cage, with weight classes, rounds of limited time, a referee and protective gear (headguard, gumshield, groin guard, shin guards), and victory by knockout, submission, referee stoppage or points (United World Wrestling, n.d.). Fouls such as biting, eye-gouging, strikes to the groin and small-joint manipulation are penalized; women's and men's bouts follow the same rules with separate weight classes (United World Wrestling, n.d.). Youth classes restrict certain techniques for both genders (United World Wrestling, n.d.).

History

Pankration was introduced to the ancient Olympic Games in 648 BC as a blend of boxing (pygme) and wrestling (pale), and it remained on the Olympic program until the Games were abolished in 393 AD; victors such as the Spartan Lichas and the multiple Olympic champion Theagenes of Thasos became legendary (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The sport survived in folk forms and was revived in modern Greece in the 20th century; the Hellenic Pankration Federation brought it under UWW recognition in 2010, and the UWW World Pankration Championships have been held since (United World Wrestling, n.d.). Ancient depictions — such as the Panathenaic amphora painted by the Kleophrades Painter showing a single-arm leg hold — document the techniques used 2,500 years ago (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
