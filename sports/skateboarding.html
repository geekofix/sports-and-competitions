Skateboarding

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Skateboarding is a sport and pastime in which riders stand on a small board with four wheels, propel themselves with one foot and perform tricks, and it has been an Olympic sport since the Tokyo 2020 Games, where four medal events — street and park, each for men and for women — were contested for the first time; at Paris 2024 the events returned with 88 athletes at Place de la Concorde from 28 July to 7 August 2024, and skateboarding becomes a core Olympic sport at Los Angeles 2028 (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The world governing body is World Skate, which sets the competition rules and runs the Olympic qualifying rankings and the World Skate Games (World Skate, n.d.).

Figure: Andymiah. (2024, August 6). Sky Brown at the 2024 Summer Olympics (cropped) [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. CC BY-SA 4.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sky_Brown_at_the_2024_Summer_Olympics_%28cropped%29.jpg

At Paris 2024, Australia's Arisa Trew won women's park gold with 93.18 points ahead of Japan's Kokona Hiraki and Great Britain's Sky Brown, while Japan's Coco Yoshizawa won women's street ahead of Liz Akama and Rayssa Leal; among the men, Australia's Keegan Palmer defended his park title with 93.11 and Japan's Yuto Horigome defended his street title (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Among today's leading athletes, the women's side is headed by Arisa Trew — the 14-year-old Australian became the youngest Australian Olympic champion in Paris 2024 — and by Sky Brown of Great Britain, who won park bronze at both Tokyo 2020 and Paris 2024 and was Britain's youngest-ever Olympic medallist when she took bronze in Tokyo at age 13, alongside street champion Coco Yoshizawa of Japan; on the men's side, Keegan Palmer (two-time Olympic park champion) and Yuto Horigome (two-time Olympic street champion) set the standard (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Men and women compete in identical formats on the same courses, with no rule differences between genders (World Skate, n.d.).

Rules

Skateboarding is governed worldwide by World Skate (https://www.worldskate.org/), whose Street & Park Competition Rules and Judging Criteria define the Olympic format. In park, skaters ride a bowl-like course with steep curves and a central feature; in the final each skater completes three 45-second runs and is ranked by the single highest-scoring run, for a maximum of 100 points (World Skate, n.d.; Olympics, n.d.). In street, the course is a linear plaza of stairs, handrails, ledges, benches and banks; each skater completes two 45-second runs and five single-trick attempts, and the final score is the best run plus the two best tricks, for a maximum of 300 points (World Skate, n.d.; Olympics, n.d.). Five judges score every run and every trick on a 0–100 scale for overall impression — difficulty, variety and quality of execution, use of the course, consistency, flow and repetition — with the highest and lowest scores dropped and the remaining three averaged; in the trick phase a skater may use the scoring-refusal procedure to scrub one attempt and try again (Olympics, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). At Paris 2024, 22 skaters started each event and the top eight advanced to a final in which scores were reset; men and women skated identical courses and formats, with no gender-specific rules (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; World Skate, n.d.). The board itself is a concave maple deck, typically about 80 cm long, mounted on two trucks with four polyurethane wheels; there are no equipment restrictions beyond safety (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

History

Skateboarding began in California in the late 1950s, when surfers attached roller-skate wheels to wooden planks to "surf" on land when the waves were flat — the "sidewalk surfing" era, commercialized from 1959 with the Roller Derby skateboard and from 1962 by Larry Stevenson's Makaha company in Venice Beach (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The first boom collapsed in the late 1960s, but the 1973 invention of urethane wheels by Frank Nasworthy revived the sport, and in the mid-1970s the Z-Boys — the Zephyr team from Dogtown (Santa Monica/Venice), including Tony Alva and Stacy Peralta — pioneered vertical pool skating in the drought-empty backyards of Southern California, documented in Peralta's 2001 documentary Dogtown and Z-Boys and the 2005 feature film Lords of Dogtown (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The ollie, the foundational jump invented by Alan Gelfand in 1978 and adapted to flat ground by Rodney Mullen in 1982, unlocked modern street skating (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). ESPN's X Games, first held in 1995, made skateboarding a televised extreme sport — Tony Hawk (born 1968) won the inaugural vert gold and, on 27 June 1999 at the X Games in San Francisco, landed the first-ever 900, two and a half mid-air rotations, on his 11th attempt; the same year saw the release of the video game Tony Hawk's Pro Skater (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Women shaped the sport from the start: Patti McGee became the first women's skateboarding champion in the mid-1960s and Peggy Oki the first female professional skateboarder in 1976 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). After years of lobbying, the IOC approved skateboarding for Tokyo 2020 on 3 August 2016, with the Street League Skateboarding circuit (founded 2010 by Rob Dyrdek) building the competitive pipeline (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
