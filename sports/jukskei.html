Jukskei

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Jukskei is a traditional South African throwing game in which teams slide heavy iron skeis across the ground toward a peg stuck in a sandpit, scoring points for knocking the peg over or for landing closest to it (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The name comes from the ox-yoke pins — juk and skei — used in the original game: transport riders at the Cape of Good Hope around 1743 threw the iron pins of ox yokes at each other's yokes (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Organized sport began around 1939 with the first unions and formalized rules, and today the International Jukskei Federation (IJF) counts South Africa, the USA, New Zealand and Namibia as full members, with associate members including Hong Kong, the Basque Country, Benin, Uganda, Kenya, Lesotho and Botswana (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.). The annual Kroonstad tournament is the highlight of the South African calendar, and the game was included in the South African Indigenous Games Project in 2001; it is a direct predecessor of horseshoes (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Women play on equal terms — the skei weights vary by age and gender, and players such as Mervelien Manetti are among the leading women in 2026 (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.).

Rules

Under IJF rules, two sandpits are set opposite each other 9 m apart, and a peg is stuck into each sandpit at a distance of 10–16 m from the throwing line, depending on age and gender (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Teams consist of four players (one the captain), or pairs and singles in smaller formats; each team member throws two skeis per turn (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Scoring: if a skei knocks the peg over, the team scores 3 points; otherwise, for each skei that lies closer to the peg than the opponent's nearest skei, the team scores 1 point (the "lies"); a skei within 460 mm of the peg also scores 1 point; a peg knocked over plus a skei within 460 mm scores 3 + 1 points (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The first team to reach exactly 23 points wins; if a score exceeds 23, the team busts and scores 0 for that end (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Matches are limited to three sets or a time limit — 75 minutes for fours and 60 minutes for pairs under IJF rules (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.). The skei is a heavy iron bar; weights vary by age and gender category, from lighter skeis for juniors and women to heavier ones for men's open (90+ age categories included) (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men and women compete in separate categories with the same rules and scoring (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.).

History

Jukskei originated around 1743 at the Cape of Good Hope, when transport riders (voortrekkers) threw the iron juk pins of ox yokes at each other — the game's name combines juk (the yoke pin) and skei (the thrown pin) (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). It remained a farm and tavern pastime through the 18th and 19th centuries; organized play began around 1939 with the first jukskei unions and the first formal rules (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The International Jukskei Federation was founded to spread the game beyond South Africa — to the USA, New Zealand, Namibia and associate members from Hong Kong to the Basque Country — and the annual Kroonstad tournament became the flagship event (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.). The South African Indigenous Games Project adopted jukskei in 2001, and the game is recognized as a direct forerunner of horseshoes, which uses similar pitching at a peg (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Women have always taken part, with their own weight categories, and the leading women of 2026 include Mervelien Manetti of South Africa (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Because jukskei grew out of Afrikaner transport-rider culture rather than professional sport, its icons are the legendary Kroonstad tournament champions recorded in the IJF records (International Jukskei Federation, n.d.).
