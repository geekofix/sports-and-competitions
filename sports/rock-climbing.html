Rock Climbing

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Rock climbing is the sport of ascending natural rock faces and artificial walls using hands, feet and balance, protected either by a rope and anchors belayed by a partner (roped lead and sport climbing, traditional or trad climbing) or by crash pads alone on low boulders (bouldering); the umbrella also covers aid climbing and free soloing without any rope (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Competitive climbing is governed by the International Federation of Sport Climbing (IFSC), independent since 2007, which has staged world championships since 1991, made its Olympic debut at Tokyo 2020 and was renamed World Climbing in December 2025 (IFSC, n.d.). The three competition disciplines are lead (roped, bolted routes against a six-minute clock, highest hold wins), boulder (four to five problems, four minutes each, tops and zones) and speed (a standardized 15-m wall against the clock — world records 4.64 s for men by Sam Watson in Bali 2025 and 5.99 s for women by Emma Hunt in Kraków 2026) (IFSC, n.d.). At the 2025 World Championships in Seoul, Slovenia's Janja Garnbret won the lead and boulder double and Japan's Sorato Anraku took the first men's boulder world title (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Among today's leading athletes, women come first: Janja Garnbret, Olympic champion at Paris 2024 and double world champion 2025, followed by stars such as Ai Mori of Japan and Natalia Grossman of the USA, while the men's side is headed by Sorato Anraku and speed specialist Sam Watson (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

In lead climbing, the climber is roped, clips quickdraws on bolted anchors and has six minutes to reach the highest hold on the route; falls end the attempt and the ranking goes by height reached (IFSC, n.d.). In bouldering, the climber tackles four or five short problems within four minutes each, scoring a top (completed hold) and a zone (intermediate hold), with fewer attempts and fewer zones producing better results, all above crash pads (IFSC, n.d.). Speed climbing uses a standardized 15-m IFSC wall with auto-belays and identical routes worldwide; the clock starts on the light signal and a false start loses the race (IFSC, n.d.). The combined format multiplies the rankings of speed, boulder and lead and was used at Tokyo 2020, while since Paris 2024 lead and boulder are combined into one event and speed is separate, with 68 climbers in total competing at Paris 2024 at Le Bourget (IFSC, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). Grades use the French scale (5+ up to 9c) internationally and the Yosemite Decimal System in North America, and outdoor ethics distinguish redpoint (clean ascent after practice), onsight (first attempt, no prior knowledge) and flash, with the first free ascent proposing the grade (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In traditional climbing the leader places removable protection (cams and nuts); in sport climbing the bolts are fixed; free soloing uses no protection at all, and helmets, belay devices and certified ropes are mandatory safety equipment (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Men's and women's events are held in every discipline with equal prize money at IFSC events, and junior and youth age categories run alongside the seniors (IFSC, n.d.).

History

Modern rock climbing began with the first ascent of the Matterhorn in 1865 by Edward Whymper's team, and developed through the Dolomites in the 1880s and the golden age of Yosemite Valley in the 1950s and 1960s under Royal Robbins (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Milestones include Wolfgang Güllich's first 9a grade with Action Directe in 1991 and Adam Ondra's first ascent of the first 9c route, Silence, in Flatanger, Norway, in 2017 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Among the icons are Lynn Hill (born 1961), who in 1993 became the first person to free-climb The Nose on El Capitan, and Catherine Destivelle (born 1960), a pioneer of women's high-grade climbing (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Competition climbing emerged in the 1980s, the UIAA — the International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation founded in 1932 — organized the first World Cup in 1991, the International Council for Competition Climbing was created in 1997, and the independent IFSC took over in 2007 before the Olympic debut at Tokyo 2020 (UIAA, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
