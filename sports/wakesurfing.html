Wakesurfing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Wakesurfing is the towed water sport closest to ocean surfing: the rider starts on a short tow rope behind a specially configured boat, gets up on the wake, drops the rope and surfs the boat's endless wave — carving, snapping and airing in the pocket without any tow (International Waterski and Wakeboard Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). The IWWF runs the World Wakesurf Championships, and the sport has separate surf-style and skim-style divisions; women compete in their own divisions at the world championships — the fastest-growing towed board sport of the 2010s onward (International Waterski and Wakeboard Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.). Safety rules are explicit: only direct-drive or V-drive inboard boats may be used, riders must wear a Coast Guard-approved life vest, a spotter must watch from the boat, and minimum water depth is 9 feet (Wikipedia, n.d.).

Rules

Boats are purpose-configured with ballast, wake plates or wake gates and surf-speed systems, and riders use wakesurf boards — larger, buoyant surf-style shapes or smaller skim-style boards — with fins and no bindings (Wikipedia, n.d.). Competition is divided into surf style (carving the wave face, rail-to-rail turns, 360s, judged like a surf heat on maneuvers, variety and style) and skim style (technical tricks off the wake's lip — shuvits, spins, airs — judged like a skate run), with riders choosing their side (left or right wake) and run length fixed by the event rules (International Waterski and Wakeboard Federation, n.d.). Divisions run by age and gender — men's and women's pro and amateur classes — at the IWWF World Wakesurf Championships and the CWSA/WSIA circuits (International Waterski and Wakeboard Federation, n.d.).

History

Riding surfboards behind boats is documented from the 1950s–1960s — notably at Cypress Gardens, Florida, from 1956 — but the modern competitive sport took shape in the mid-1990s, when Rick Lee of Centurion Boats promoted wakesurfing with the bigger wakes of V-drive boats and organized the first World Championship series, prompting boat builders to engineer wake-shaping hulls and board makers to design purpose-built wakesurf boards; the first US design patent for a wakesurf board was granted to Alfonso Corona in 1997 (Wikipedia, n.d.). The Competitive Wake Surf Association (CWSA) and later the IWWF World Wakesurf Championships consolidated the professional structure, and the 2010s brought wakesurf-specific boats, surf gates and a global contest circuit, making wakesurfing the fastest-growing segment of the towed water sports industry (International Waterski and Wakeboard Federation, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).
