Axe Throwing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Axe throwing is a precision sport in which competitors throw a small axe — or hatchet — at a wooden target, aiming to stick the blade in and score points. The two dominant rule sets are those of the World Axe Throwing League (WATL), founded in 2017, which uses a five-ring target with a "clutch" (bullseye) worth 7 points, and the International Axe Throwing Federation (IATF), which uses a four-zone target. WATL now sanctions more than 300 venues in over 20 countries and holds a World Axe Throwing Championship — the 9th edition was staged in Tulsa, Oklahoma, in 2026, with the 10th World Axe & Knife Throwing Championship scheduled for April 2027 in Appleton, Wisconsin (World Axe Throwing League [WATL], n.d.-a). The sport has been broadcast on ESPN in the United States since 2018, and WATL's sister league WKTL covers knife throwing (WATL, n.d.-b).

Competitive axe throwing is played as head-to-head matches: each thrower gets ten throws, alternating, and the higher total wins; if tied, a clutch-off (sudden-death bullseye) decides. Because the sport is also a social league game, most venues run recreational leagues alongside the competitive circuit, and both women and men compete on equal terms in the same divisions — the 2026 world championships featured a deep women's field alongside the men's (WATL, n.d.-a; IATF, n.d.).

Rules

Under WATL rules, the thrower stands behind a foul line 15 ft (4.57 m) from the face of the target and throws ten times per match; each throw that sticks scores the value of the ring it lands in — 1, 3, 5 or 7 points for the clutch — and a throw that sticks but falls out scores 1 point. Stepping over the foul line scores zero for that throw. Matches are decided by the higher total over ten throws, with a clutch-off if tied (WATL, n.d.-a).

Under IATF rules, the throwing distance is 12 ft (3.66 m) with one full rotation of the axe; the target has four scoring zones, and each competitor also throws ten times per match. Both organizations require axes of a standard profile — typically a 16 oz (450 g) head on a hickory handle of about 13 inches (33 cm) — inspected before competition (International Axe Throwing Federation [IATF], n.d.).

There are no gender- or age-specific rule differences at WATL or IATF level — men and women throw from the same distance at the same targets — but youth divisions may shorten the distance, and hatchet divisions use smaller, lighter axes. Safety rules require closed-toe shoes, throwing only in one's own lane, and axes may not be retrieved until the target is clear.

History

Axe throwing as a sport grew out of lumberjack competitions in North America in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, where woodcutters competed in events such as tree felling, log rolling and axe throwing — most famously at the Great Alaskan Lumberjack Show in Ketchikan, Alaska, founded in 1955. The modern competitive format with standardized targets and distances emerged in the 2000s in Canada and the United States (WATL, n.d.-b).

The World Axe Throwing League was founded in 2017 by commissioner Mario Zelaya and quickly expanded from a handful of venues to an international league with a televised world championship; the first World Axe Throwing Championship was held in 2018. The International Axe Throwing Federation codified its own target and distance rules and sanctions national championships, particularly in the United States. The sport gained mainstream exposure through ESPN broadcasts starting in 2018 and through the popularity of axe-throwing bars, and knife throwing was added as a sister discipline under the WKTL banner (WATL, n.d.-a).

On the women's side, competitive axe throwing has been gender-integrated from the start of the modern leagues; top women throwers now regularly reach the semifinals of world championships, and WATL introduced a dedicated women's prize structure in its early seasons (WATL, n.d.-b).
