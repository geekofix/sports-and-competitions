Airship Racing

≈ 2 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Airship racing was the competitive racing of dirigibles (rigid and non-rigid airships) in the early 20th century, at a time when airships were the fastest aircraft and national prestige depended on them (Wikipedia, n.d.). It belongs to the lighter-than-air and air-sports family and is largely historical today.

Figure: Self Scanned. (1909). Postcard of the 1909 Allentown Fair airship race [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. Public domain. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/1909_-_Allentown_Fiar_-_Airship_Race_-_Postcard_-_Allentown_PA.jpg

Races were time trials or mass-start events around pylons or between cities; the Gordon Bennett air races (1906–1913) and the Allentown Fair airship races of 1909 are the best documented, with postcards and photographs showing the great ships competing (Wikipedia, n.d.). Airship classes were defined by FAI categories (C for rigid airships), and records were ratified internationally (FAI, n.d.).

Rules

Competing required an FAI airship pilot licence, a certified airship, and ground crews; races were typically flown over a closed circuit of tens of kilometres with turns around pylons (Wikipedia, n.d.). Men crewed the ships; there were no women’s crews in the era. Today airship "racing" survives only as historical reenactment and in model/RC airship competitions.

History

Airship racing peaked between 1906 and 1914: the Gordon Bennett Aviation Trophy (for airplanes, from 1909) ran parallel to airship contests, and events such as the 1909 Allentown Fair races and the 1910 races over Paris drew huge crowds (Wikipedia, n.d.). The 1910s brought disaster — storms, lightning and the losses of the 1920s (including the R101 in 1930) ended airship racing as a sport, and the FAI’s airship records now stand as memorials of the era (Wikipedia, n.d.).
