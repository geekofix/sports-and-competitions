Mini Tennis

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Mini tennis — branded Tennis10s by the International Tennis Federation (ITF) — is the modified entry format of tennis for children, generally aged 4 to 10, played on smaller courts with shorter rackets and slower red, orange and green balls (International Tennis Federation, n.d.-a). Since 2012 the ITF Rules of Tennis (Appendix VII) have required that all sanctioned competition for players aged 10 and under be played with red, orange or green balls on appropriately sized courts, so the standard yellow ball is no longer permitted in that age group (International Tennis Federation, n.d.-b). The format is the flagship of the ITF Play and Stay campaign launched in 2007, and competition is co-ed: girls and boys play together in the same draws at every stage (United States Tennis Association, n.d.). The pathway leads into the ITF World Tennis Tour Junior circuit, and at the 2026 World Tennis Tour Junior Finals in Chengdu the girls' title went to Sun Xinran of China and the boys' title to Jordan Lee of the United States (World Tennis, n.d.). Mini tennis (Tennis10s) is the junior entry format of Tennis, within the racket family — Touchtennis, POP Tennis, Paddle Tennis, Table Tennis — listed under Important Links.

Women and men compete alike at every level of mini-tennis, from junior to senior.

Rules

Under the ITF Rules of Tennis 2026 (Appendices VII and VIII), mini tennis is organized in three color-coded stages (International Tennis Federation, n.d.-b). Red stage (up to 8 years): a court of about 11 m × 5.5 m (36 ft × 18 ft; some federations such as the LTA allow 11–12 m × 5–6 m), a net at 80 cm (31.5 in), rackets no longer than 58 cm (23 in), and a red foam ball 8–9 cm in diameter (or a felt ball 7–8 cm) that bounces about 75 percent slower than a yellow ball. Orange stage (8–9 years): an 18 m × 6.4 m (60 ft × 21 ft) court with the net still at 80 cm and an orange ball about 50 percent slower than yellow. Green stage (9–10 years): the full-size court with a net at 91.4 cm (3 ft) at the center and a green ball about 25 percent slower than yellow. Scoring can be shortened — short sets, tie-breaks at 3–3 or 4–4, or a match tie-break — so that matches fit young players' attention spans; from age 11 the standard yellow ball and the full rules of tennis apply. Girls and boys compete together in the same events at all three stages, with no separate girls' or boys' draws (United States Tennis Association, n.d.).

History

The modern mini-tennis pathway began when the ITF launched its Play and Stay campaign in 2007 after research showed that children often quit tennis because the yellow ball and the full court were too difficult at the start (International Tennis Federation, n.d.-a). In the United States the USTA led the research into 10-and-under tennis and published its Tennis10s manual in 2010, making red-ball tennis for 8-and-unders the American standard (United States Tennis Association, n.d.). The ITF then codified the colored-ball pathway worldwide in the 2012 edition of the Rules of Tennis, and national associations — from the LTA in Britain to federations across Asia and the Americas — adopted the red, orange and green progression, so that today millions of girls and boys learn to serve, rally and score from their first lesson on a court sized to their bodies (Wikipedia, n.d.).
