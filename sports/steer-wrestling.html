Steer Wrestling

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Steer wrestling, also called bulldogging, is the rodeo event in which a rider on horseback chases a steer, leaps from the saddle onto the running animal, grabs its horns and wrestles it to the ground. A test of timing, strength and technique measured in seconds, it is one of professional rodeo's marquee events and among the most spectacular sights in western sport.

Rules

The run begins when the steer is released from the chute with a head start; the mounted wrestler and a second rider, the hazer, gallop alongside to keep the steer running straight. Within seconds, the wrestler dismounts from the horse onto the steer, hooks his arms around the horns, plants his feet and uses leverage "+E+" not brute strength alone, but the technique of turning the animal off balance "+E+" to flip it onto its side or back, halting the clock when all four legs point skyward. Rules mandate legal catches and humane handling; barrier penalties add ten seconds to broken-start runs, and modern sanctioning bodies such as the Professional Rodeo Cowboys Association enforce animal-welfare rules with veterinarians on site at sanctioned events.

History

Steer wrestling traces its roots to ancient bull-leaping feats of horsemen in the Mediterranean world, but the modern event was invented in the 1890s by Bill Pickett, the legendary Black cowboy and Wild West show performer who developed the bulldogging technique while working ranches in Texas. The event spread through Wild West shows and early rodeos, becoming a fixture of professional rodeo in the early twentieth century and a standard event of the Rodeo Cowboys Association (today's PRCA) from its founding in the 1930s. Steer wrestling remains a headline event at the National Finals Rodeo, with world champions crowned annually, and it is among rodeo's most-studied events from an animal-welfare perspective, with surveys documenting injury rates to livestock well below one in two thousand runs.
