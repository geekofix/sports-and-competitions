Tug of War

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Tug of war is the ancient team strength contest: two teams of eight pull opposite ends of a rope, victory going to the team that pulls the rope (marked at the centre) past a determined line or pulls the opposing team off balance. A sport of organized clubs and world championships for more than a century, it was contested at the Olympic Games from 1900 to 1920 and remains one of the world's most inclusive strength sports.

Rules

International rules set the standard: teams of eight pull on a roughly 40-metre rope, the centre of which starts over a ground line and marked pulling tape on each side; the winning team pulls the rope so that its marker tape crosses the four-metre line in its direction, the victory earned by combined power and technique: teams drive backward in unison behind an anchor man, a coach calling the pull, and referees award cautions for sitting, locking or improper grip before disqualifying (sitting, deliberate rope lock or improper anchoring). Weight classes from youth to super-heavyweight structure competition, with clubs racing best-of-three end pulls in bracket tournaments toward world championship finals.

History

The rope-pull is attested in ancient cultures worldwide (organized rope-pulls are documented in Chinese, African, Asian and European folk tradition alike), and nineteenth-century Britain gave it codified rules: the first organized clubs drew up rules in the 1880s and the sport entered the Olympic Games from 1900 to 1920 before its removal from the programme. International federation status (today's Tug of War International Federation, founded 1960) organized world championships from the 1960s, with the sport's heartlands in Britain, Ireland, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Scandinavia, the United States and Asia. The modern sport pairs tradition with genuine athletic specialization: elite teams train like track-and-field throwers and remain lean, with each eight-man side's combined pulling power exceeding a tonne in peak force.
