Footbag

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Footbag — popularly known as "hacky sack," a registered trademark of Wham-O, so the generic name footbag is used in competition — is played by keeping a small, soft bag (about 40–60 g, filled with plastic pellets or sand) in the air using only the feet and lower legs (IFPA, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The two competitive disciplines are net footbag, a volleyball-like game over a 5 ft net, and freestyle footbag, a judged art form of tricks, delays and acrobatics (IFPA, n.d.-b). The sport is governed by the International Footbag Players' Association (IFPA), a non-profit federation that sanctions the IFPA World Footbag Championships; the 45th edition was held August 10–15, 2026, at the Tsukuba Capio arena in Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan — the first world championships ever staged in Asia — with net events (singles, doubles and mixed, in Open, Women, Intermediate and Master classes) and freestyle events (request contest, Sick 3, last man standing, battles, circle contest and routines) (IFPA, n.d.-c). At the 44th championships in Nantes, France, in 2025, Kenny Shults (USA) won the open singles net title and Marilyn Demuy (CAN) the women's singles net title (IFPA, n.d.-c) (International Footbag Players' Association, n.d.-a) (International Footbag Players' Association, n.d.-b) (International Footbag Players' Association, n.d.-c).

At the end of 2026 the leading women include Marilyn Demuy (CAN), the 2025 world champion in women's net, and the top freestyle players from North America and Europe, while on the men's side Kenny Shults (USA) heads the net rankings and the 2026 freestyle titles in Tsukuba were shared among players from the USA, Japan and Europe (IFPA, n.d.-c).

Within the shuttlecock/footbag family on this site, its closest relatives are Jianzi, Kemari, Sipa, Chinlone; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

Under the IFPA Official Rules of Footbag Sports, the net game is played on a court 44 ft × 20 ft (13.4 m × 6.1 m) with a net 5 ft (1.5 m) high, divided into two halves; scoring is side-out — only the serving side can score a point — and the serve must be struck diagonally cross-court into the opponent's service quadrants (IFPA, n.d.-b; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). In singles each player may contact the bag at most twice before it must cross the net; in doubles the team has three contacts, with kicks alternating between partners; only the feet and legs below the knee may touch the bag — any contact with the knee or above, or with the hands or arms, is a fault (IFPA, n.d.-b). A game is played to 15 points, won by at least 2 (preliminary games to 11); at 20–20 play continues until one side leads by 2, with a ceiling of 25 points, and a match is the best of three games, with a single consolation game to 21 (ceiling 25) for third place (IFPA, n.d.-b). Freestyle competition is judged, not timed: in battle events two players face off head-to-head in alternating 30-second rounds while judges score difficulty, execution, style, variety and musicality; other formats include the pairs request contest (each player calls the trick the partner must perform), Sick 3 (three designated "sick" tricks), last man standing, the circle contest and choreographed routines (IFPA, n.d.-b). In all events the hands may not touch the bag below the elbows.

History

Kicking games with a small sack have ancient roots — the Chinese game jianzi dates back more than 2,000 years — but modern footbag emerged in the United States in the early 1970s, popularized by John Stalberger and Mike Marshall, and the name "Hacky Sack" was trademarked by Wham-O in the 1970s (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The first official world championship was held in 1986 in Golden, Colorado, under the World Footbag Association (WFA), and the event became the roving world championships of the IFPA in the 1990s; the IFPA itself was founded as a non-profit players' federation to govern net and freestyle competition (IFPA, n.d.-a; Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Among the sport's icons, Peter Irish of the USA stands out with a record five world freestyle singles titles, and the 2015 world championships in Copenhagen (36th edition) marked the event's growing European following (IFPA, n.d.-c; Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
