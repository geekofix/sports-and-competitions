Roller Skiing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Roller skiing is the off-snow equivalent of cross-country skiing: athletes ski on short frames with one wheel at each end, using the same classic and skating techniques as on snow to train and race on paved surfaces. Created as a summer training exercise for cross-country skiers, it is now a competitive sport in its own right governed by the International Ski and Snowboard Federation (FIS), which has recognised roller skiing as a distinct discipline since 1992 and stages World Cup races and World Championships in the sport.

Rules

Races are held on closed road circuits in the same formats as cross-country skiing — sprints, individual races, pursuit races, relays and team races — over courses from a few kilometres up to marathon distance, with senior men's and women's races and junior categories. Both ski techniques are used: classic, on longer and heavier roller skis with a ratchet mechanism in one wheel that prevents rolling backward and allows a kick, and skating, on shorter, lighter skis with faster, narrower wheels and no ratchets. Speeds are high — average speeds on flat World Cup courses can easily reach 30 km/h and top speeds on descents around 50 km/h — and safety rules are correspondingly strict: helmets are mandatory in all FIS competitions, protective glasses are required, and age-group equipment rules govern wheel hardness and ski dimensions set by the FIS roller skiing committee. Standard race distances at world championship level mirror cross-country skiing, with sprint events and distance races; the classic sprint courses may use special fast wheels by rule.

History

The first roller skis were built in the mid-1930s in Italy and northern Europe, and as cross-country skiing became a serious competition sport in the 1950s, athletes and coaches across Europe and North America experimented with skis on wheels for summer training; in the 1970s a de facto standard emerged, with three-wheeled skis (one wheel in front, two behind) on 70–100 cm metal frames. In 1976 the Italian pilot Giustino Del Vecchio set a 24-hour record of 240.5 km in Monza on roller skis built from aircraft industry materials, and through the 1980s the rise of skating technique in cross-country skiing pushed the modern two-wheel design that could serve both techniques. Competition structures followed: the European Rollerski Federation was established around 1985, the first European Championships were held in the Netherlands in 1988, and FIS recognised roller skiing as a distinct sport in 1992; the first World Games-style event was held in The Hague in 1993, the first FIS Roller Ski World Cup took place the same year, and in 1998 the FIS congress in Prague granted the sport official FIS World Championships, first staged in the Netherlands in 2000. National cross-country and biathlon teams worldwide rely on roller skiing as their main dryland training, and the FIS Roller Ski World Cup continues each summer and autumn, by 2025 opening its season in Madona, Latvia.
