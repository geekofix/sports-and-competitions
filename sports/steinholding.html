Steinholding

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Steinholding (from the German Stein, stone, and halten, to hold) is a strength sport in which an athlete holds a heavy stone — classically a stone mug or "beer stein" — with a fully extended straight arm for as long as possible, with the heaviest hold or the longest time deciding the winner; it belongs to the "stones of strength" tradition of the Bavarian and Alpine strongman scene and to the heavy athletics of the Highland games (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). There is no single world federation for steinholding; it is contested at Highland games, strongman meets and dedicated steinholding competitions organized by national Highland games associations such as the Scottish Highland Games Association (SHAG) and the North American Scottish Games Association (NASGA), plus promoters of strongman events (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Both men and women compete, in separate divisions with lighter stones for women, and age classes from junior to masters exist at Highland games (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

Rules

In the classic steinholding event each competitor gets up to three attempts; the stone — a filled beer-stein mug or a dedicated steel-and-lead stone of 10–25 kg for men and lighter variants for women — is lifted to shoulder height with a straight arm and held horizontally with the arm fully extended, the clock running until the stone drops below the horizontal or the arm bends (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The longest valid hold wins; in "max hold" variants the heaviest stone held for a minimum time (often 5 seconds) wins instead. Fouls include resting the stone on the body, bending the elbow or wrist to support it, or stepping out of the marked lifting area; judges time each attempt and the best of three stands. Highland games run men's and women's heavies with the same apparatus scaled by weight class, and youth divisions use lighter stones; strongman shows add timed team relays (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).

History

Stones of strength have been lifted in Scotland and the Alps for centuries — the "Manhood Stone" (Clach Cuid Fir, over 100 lb) had to be lifted onto a wall or to a set height at Highland games — and the timed hold variant developed in Bavaria, where holding a full Maß beer stein at arm's length became a folk test of strength at village festivals (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The modern competitive form is embedded in the Highland games movement revived in the 19th century and in the strongman contests popularized in the 20th century; one of the most famous strength icons of the era, the Canadian strongman Louis Cyr (1863–1912), who could lift a 4,337 lb stone and was called the strongest man in the world, exemplifies the stones-of-strength tradition from which steinholding grew (Wikipedia, n.d.-a).
