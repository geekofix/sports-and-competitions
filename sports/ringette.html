Ringette

≈ 5 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Ringette is a winter team ice sport invented in Canada in 1963 for girls and women by Sam Jacks of North Bay, Ontario, with the first official rules drafted in 1964–1965 by Mirl Arthur "Red" McCarthy in Espanola, Ontario (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Teams of six — five skaters and a goaltender — use straight sticks to pass, carry and shoot a blue rubber ring into the opponent's net on a standard ice hockey rink, and the sport is governed internationally by the International Ringette Federation (IRF), founded as the World Ringette Council and renamed in 1991, with member nations Canada, Finland, Sweden and the United States, and nationally by Ringette Canada, founded in 1974 with June Tiessen as its first president (Wikipedia, n.d.-a; Ringette Canada, n.d.). Ringette's distinctive feature is that at the elite level all competitors are women, unlike most international ice team sports (Wikipedia, n.d.-b). The premier event is the biennial World Ringette Championship, first held in 1990 in Gloucester, Ontario, where the Calgary Debs won the inaugural Sam Jacks Pool; at the 2025 championship in Lahti, Finland, in November 2025, Finland beat Canada 7–3 in the final to take its ninth consecutive Sam Jacks Pool title, Canada won the U21 pool 4–3 over Finland, and Sweden won the President's Pool in overtime against the USA (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Rules

Ringette is played six-a-side — five skaters plus a goaltender — on a standard ice hockey rink of about 60 × 26–30 m, with a hollow blue rubber ring 16.5 cm (6.5 in) in diameter and straight sticks without a pocket (Ringette Canada, n.d.). The hallmark rule is the blue-line rule: a player may not carry the ring across either blue line, and doing so awards the opponents a free pass (Ringette Canada, n.d.). After goals, icings and certain stoppages, play resumes with a free pass from one of five marked circles on the ice, with a five-second count to pass the ring out of the circle (Ringette Canada, n.d.). A 30-second shot clock, introduced in 2002, requires a team in possession in the offensive zone to shoot on goal within 30 seconds (Ringette Canada, n.d.). Elite games are played in four quarters of about 13–15 minutes; checking and body contact are prohibited, and there is no offside or icing beyond the free-pass system (Ringette Canada, n.d.). The goaltender wears a helmet with cage and may catch the ring with the hand but may not throw it past the centre red line (Ringette Canada, n.d.). International competition is split into the elite Sam Jacks Pool, the President's Pool and the U21 pool, with the goaltender's crease, minor and major penalties, and shootouts deciding ties; the sport is contested by women at every elite level, from club leagues such as Canada's National Ringette League and Finland's SM Ringette to the national teams (Wikipedia, n.d.-b; Ringette Canada, n.d.).

History

Sam Jacks (born 23 April 1915 in Glasgow, Scotland; died 14 May 1975), a recreation director in North Bay, Ontario, created ringette in 1963 so that girls could play an ice game of their own; he was inducted into Canada's Sports Hall of Fame in 2007, and the elite world championship trophy, the Sam Jacks Pool, bears his name (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Mirl Arthur "Red" McCarthy wrote the first official rules in 1964–1965 in Espanola, Ontario, and the first recorded game was played in 1963 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Sam's wife Agnes Jacks promoted the sport internationally, helped bring it to Finland through coach Juhani Wahlsten in 1979, and received the Order of Canada in 2002; the Agnes Jacks Trophy has been awarded to the World Ringette Championship MVP since 1992 (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). Ringette Canada was founded in 1974, the first World Ringette Championship took place in 1990 in Gloucester, Ontario, won by the Calgary Debs, and the tournament has run biennially since, with the 2023 edition in Calgary marking the sport's 60th anniversary (Wikipedia, n.d.-b).
