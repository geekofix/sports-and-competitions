Polo

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Polo is a mounted team sport in which two teams of four riders use long-handled mallets to drive a small ball between the opposing team's goal posts (Britannica, n.d.). Often called "the sport of kings," it is one of the oldest known team sports, tracing back to the Persian equestrian game of chovgan, and its modern form was standardized in Britain in the 19th century (Wikipedia, n.d.). Outdoor polo is played on a grass pitch up to 300 by 160 yards — the largest field in organized sport — while arena polo uses a smaller enclosed ground with three players a side. The international federation is the Federation of International Polo (FIP), which counts more than 100 member countries today (FIP, n.d.). Polo is played by women and men at every level — the FIP Women's World Cup and the growing women's high-goal circuit, led by players such as England's Nina Clarkin, one of the highest-rated female players in the world, show the women's game at its peak — and mixed teams are common at club level. Rules

Matches are divided into periods called chukkas of seven minutes (7½ under US rules), with three-minute breaks between chukkas and a five-minute halftime; high-goal matches are played over six chukkas and club matches over four (Hurlingham Polo Association, 2019). Players change ponies between chukkas, as each mount is only played for a single period. Goals are scored by driving the ball between goal posts set eight yards apart; teams change ends after each goal. Players are rated on a handicap scale from -2 up to 10 goals, and matches are commonly played with a set handicap difference between teams. The central safety rule governs the "line of the ball," the imaginary path of a traveling ball which a player may not cross in a way that endangers others; right-of-way belongs to the player on the line, and rules prohibit riding into an opponent at an unsafe angle. Umpires on horseback enforce the rules and award penalties as free hits at goal (Hurlingham Polo Association, 2019). Arena polo uses three players a side on a 100-by-50-yard indoor or outdoor arena with a smaller ball and enclosed boards; the line-of-the-ball rule is replaced by the arena's boundary rules. Men's and women's outdoor matches follow identical rules and field dimensions (FIP, n.d.).

History

The ancestor of polo, chovgan, was played in Persia and Central Asia from roughly the 6th century BCE and was patronized by Parthian and Sasanian kings, including Shapur II, who learned the game as a child in the 4th century (Wikipedia, n.d.). From Persia the game spread through Asia — its polo-like cousin buzkashi survives in Central Asia — and it reached a sophisticated form in Manipur, India, where British tea planters and army officers encountered it in the 1850s. Lieutenant Joseph Sherer co-founded the Silchar Polo Club in 1859 (the oldest club still operating), the Calcutta Polo Club followed, and the game was brought to Britain in the 1860s–70s; the first formal British rules were drawn up by the Hurlingham Polo Committee in 1875, and the Hurlingham Polo Association (HPA) was created in 1925 as today's governing body for the UK, Ireland and other countries (Wikipedia, n.d.). Polo was an Olympic sport five times between 1900 and 1936. Argentina has produced many of the greatest players, including the 10-goal star Adolfo Cambiaso, the sport's most celebrated modern player, and the Argentine Open at Palermo in Buenos Aires is considered the most prestigious tournament in the world (Britannica, n.d.).
