Latrones

≈ 3 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Latrunculus — ludus latrunculorum, “the game of little soldiers/brigands” — is the two-player war game of the Roman Empire, played on a grid board (finds suggest boards of 8×8 up to 8×12 cells) with pieces captured by enclosing them between two enemies on opposite sides. It is believed to derive from the Greek games petteia/polis and is described by Roman authors from Varro (116–27 BC) onward; it was especially popular among Roman soldiers, and boards carved into buildings have been found from Hadrian's Wall to Egypt. Reconstruction tournaments are played today, and men and women play on equal terms. Within the mind games on this site, its closest relatives are Seega, Dash Guti, Morabaraba, Chess; the related pages are listed under Important Links.

Rules

No complete ancient rule text survives, so modern reconstructions (R. C. Bell's being the classic) agree on the core: two players command equal forces — often twelve soldiers plus a commander (dux) per side — set up on opposite ranks. Pieces move along the grid lines (Bell's reconstruction gives soldiers rook-like slides, the dux a king-like step or short jumps); a piece is captured when it is sandwiched between two enemy pieces on opposite sides of a straight horizontal or vertical line (custodial capture); the board edge can serve as one side. The player who captures the opponent's dux, eliminates their pieces, or leaves them without a legal move wins.

History

The first Roman mention is by Marcus Terentius Varro in book 10 of De Lingua Latina, where he compares the game's grid to the grid used for declensions; the 1st-century AD Laus Pisonis gives an account of a game, and Martial and Ovid mention it; Macrobius still wrote of it in the 5th century AD. Julius Pollux's Onomasticon describes the related Greek polis (“city”) with pieces called “dogs” captured by enclosure. Archaeology confirms the game's spread: a limestone board with ceramic cylindrical pieces was excavated at Complutum (Alcalá de Henares, Spain) in 2024, and carved boards survive at many Roman sites. In the Middle Ages ludus latrunculorum was sometimes used as a Latin name for chess.
