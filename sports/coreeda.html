Coreeda

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Coreeda is an Australian Aboriginal team wrestling sport that combines traditional dance with combat: after a kangaroo-mimicry warm-up, two teams of six wrestle single bouts in a 4.5 metre circle with black and red borders echoing the Aboriginal flag. Points from each match-up combine into a team score, and athletes win by pushing or pulling the opponent out of the circle or controlling them on the ground.

The sport draws on oral traditions of Ngiyampaa Country in western New South Wales and is promoted today by the Coreeda Association of Australia, working with schools and Indigenous communities in western Sydney.

Rules

Teams field six wrestlers across weight categories named after kangaroo species (from pademelons at 60 kg to boomers in the open class), matching each athlete in single bouts. Bouts are scored like folk wrestling: wins come from pushing or pulling the opponent from the circle or pinning or controlling them, with points for takedowns scored across two rounds; the dance component opens each event and is judged as part of the team presentation. International club rules forbid joint locks and strikes, keeping the emphasis on throws, balance and control in the circle.

History

Coreeda means kangaroo spirit in Ngiyampaa language, and rock art at Mount Grenfell near Cobar is cited by the sport's promoters as evidence of wrestling tournaments of great age. The modern sport was created in 1998 by Gavin Dickson and Shane Parker of the Coreeda Association of Australia, combining Aboriginal dance and combat movements with freestyle wrestling technique, and teaching it to young people — including Commonwealth Games wrestler Shane Parker — in Mount Druitt and beyond. The association aims for a First Nations championship with teams representing Aboriginal nations, and the sport is taught in school programmes.
