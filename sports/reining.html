Reining

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Reining is a western riding competition in which horse and rider perform one of a set of prescribed patterns — circles at speed and slow, flying lead changes, spins, rollbacks and the discipline's hallmark sliding stops — judged on the precision, attitude and athleticism of the "reined" horse within the arena. It is often described as the western equivalent of dressage, and it is governed worldwide by the National Reining Horse Association (NRHA), founded in 1966, with FEI recognition making it the first western discipline contested at the World Equestrian Games.

Rules

Under the NRHA Handbook, each run is scored from 0 with each maneuver judged on execution and attitude, with penalties applied for visible disobedience and faults: half-point and one-point deductions for incorrect or missing parts of maneuvers, two-point penalties for frozen spins or rollbacks, and five-point penalties for spurring in front of the cinch, holding the saddle or blatant disobedience; a score of 70 marks a correct, workmanlike run on average pattern difficulty. Contestants ride one of the NRHA's approved patterns — the association publishes a regularly updated pattern book — with required elements including small slow circles, large fast circles, lead changes, rollbacks over the hocks, 360-degree spins in place and sliding stops from a lope. Horses work in western tack with reining-specific shoes, and the open, non-pro, youth and entry-level class structure (from Rookie levels to Level 4 Open) lets riders of every standard compete, plus freestyle reining set to music.

History

Reining developed from the maneuvers a working cow horse needed on the ranch — fast circles to rate cattle, rollbacks to turn a herd and hard, sliding stops — and formal competition grew out of American stock-horse shows, where reining classes rewarded the best-trained animal. The National Reining Horse Association was founded in 1966 and staged its first Futurity, modeled on cutting's aged events, soon after; when the Futurity moved to Oklahoma City in 1986, the sport's center of gravity shifted nationwide and the event has grown into a multi-million-dollar show featuring the world's best three-year-olds. Reining gained international standing when the Federation Equestre Internationale accepted it in 2000 and it became the first western discipline inducted into the World Equestrian Games in 2002, with the United States Equestrian Team recognizing it in 1998 — milestones that spread the sport to Europe, Brazil and beyond.
