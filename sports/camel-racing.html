Camel Racing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Camel racing is a traditional sport of the Arabian Peninsula in which dromedary camels sprint over sand tracks — typically about 6 km for Emirati owners, up to 8 km for the season finale and up to 10 km in Saudi Arabia — with speeds of up to 65 km/h in sprints and around 40 km/h sustained for an hour. The sport is run by national camel racing clubs, above all the Dubai Camel Racing Club (DCRC), and is an element of Emirati heritage celebrated at festivals such as the Al Marmoom Heritage Festival (Dubai Camel Racing Club [DCRC], n.d.). The 2026 Al Marmoom Heritage Festival at the Al Marmoom Camel Racetrack on the Dubai–Al Ain Road (E66) at Al Lisaili ran March 22–April 2, 2026, with 12 race days, 333 races, more than 6,000 camels, a prize pool of AED 9 million (about US$2.46 million) and an 8 km finale; the Sword of the Emirates finale carried a purse of about US$544,000. The racing season runs from October to April, with electronic timing and chipping, doping controls and veterinary checks at the track and the Dubai Camel Hospital (DCRC, n.d.). Since 2005 the camels are ridden by lightweight robot jockeys (2–3 kg UAE-built models, replacing the Swiss K-Team originals first tested in Qatar in 2001), after child jockeys under 15 were banned in the UAE from September 1, 2002 — a ban announced by Sheikh Hamdan bin Zayed on July 29, 2002 — and in 2009 the UAE paid compensation to 879 former child jockeys (Human Rights Watch [HRW], n.d.). Saeed Mubarak Al-Ahbabi is one of the best-known Emirati camel racing specialists and public figures of the sport today (DCRC, n.d.).

On the gender side, camel racing has traditionally been a male-dominated sport — jockeys were boys and later robots, and owners and trainers are predominantly men — but women participate as owners and spectators, and women's participation in Emirati sport in general has grown rapidly; there are no women's jockey categories because the jockey is a robot (DCRC, n.d.) (HRW, n.d.). Note for readers: the sport's history includes the documented abuse of child jockeys, which led to the 2002 ban and the introduction of robot jockeys — an animal-welfare and human-rights reform widely reported by human rights organizations (HRW, n.d.).

Rules

Under the rules of the national camel racing clubs (DCRC in Dubai; the Saudi Camel Club in Saudi Arabia), a race is run on a sand track of about 6–10 km with a standing start; the camel must cross the finish line with its chipped nose first, timed electronically. Owners enter camels by age and sex classes — the classic categories are 2-year-olds up to 6-year-olds and separate races for male and female camels (in Dubai, races are typically for camels aged 4 and up, with "mafhoum" races for younger animals); each camel may run only once per race day, and handlers present the animals at the paddock for veterinary inspection (DCRC, n.d.). Jockeys are robot jockeys weighing 2–3 kg, controlled remotely by a follow car; the whip is electric and regulated, and striking the camel in the face or neck is forbidden. Doping is controlled: blood and urine samples are tested, and offenders (owner and camel) are suspended; camels are checked at the Dubai Camel Hospital before and after racing (DCRC, n.d.) (HRW, n.d.). Fouls: obstructing another camel, using a non-registered robot or exceeding weight limits; penalties range from fines to disqualification and multi-year bans. Prize money is paid to the owner; top purses exceed half a million dollars at the season's marquee races (DCRC, n.d.). Formats: daily races during the October–April season, the Al Marmoom Heritage Festival in Dubai, the King Abdulaziz Camel Festival in Saudi Arabia (the "Miss Beauty" of camels, with races), and international events; men's and women's camels race in separate categories under the same rules (DCRC, n.d.).

History

Camel racing has been part of Bedouin culture on the Arabian Peninsula for centuries — a 2016 report on Yemeni camel festivals noted racing, wrestling, jumping and beauty contests side by side (The World, 2016) — and it became a professional sport in the Gulf states in the 20th century, with organized tracks in Dubai (Al Marmoom, opened 1991) and Riyadh. For decades the camels were ridden by child jockeys, many trafficked from South Asia; under international pressure, Sheikh Hamdan bin Zayed announced on July 29, 2002 that jockeys under 15 were banned in the UAE from September 1, 2002, and robot jockeys were introduced in Qatar in 2001 and in the UAE from 2005, ending the use of children; in 2009 the UAE paid compensation to 879 former child jockeys (HRW, n.d.). The sport is inscribed in the UAE's intangible cultural heritage and celebrated at the Al Marmoom Heritage Festival, which in 2026 offered AED 9 million in prizes across 333 races (DCRC, n.d.).
