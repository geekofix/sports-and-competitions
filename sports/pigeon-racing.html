Pigeon Racing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Pigeon racing is the sport of releasing specially trained homing pigeons at a distant release point, after which the birds race back to their home lofts. It has been described as "the sport with a single starting gate and a thousand finish lines". The birds fly distances of up to about 1,000 km at average speeds of around 97 km/h, with the winner determined by the highest average velocity rather than the absolute finishing time. The sport is overseen by national bodies such as the Royal Pigeon Racing Association (RPRA) in the United Kingdom and the Fédération Columbophile Internationale at world level.

Rules

Competing pigeons are entered at the organizing club and transported in specially modified trucks to the race point, whose distance to each loft is measured by GPS. All birds are released simultaneously and the time each bird takes to return home is recorded using either traditional rubber rings and manual clocks or modern electronic timing rings. Because lofts are at different distances from the release point, each bird's average velocity (distance divided by time) is calculated and the fastest velocity wins. Birds must return to their own loft to clock in. Modern fixtures include one-loft races, in which all entered pigeons are housed together in a single loft from about six weeks of age, trained together, and released from the same point to race back to that loft, so that the test is of the individual bird rather than the loft manager.

History

Homing pigeons carried messages from antiquity—the practice is documented in ancient Egypt, in medieval Baghdad and as late as 1860, when Paul Reuter used pigeons to carry stock prices between Brussels and Aachen. Competitive racing grew out of this tradition in 19th-century Belgium, where the first long-distance races were held. In Britain, a meeting of pigeon keepers at the White Swan in Leeds on 28 March 1896 led to the formation of the National Homing Union, later the Royal National Homing Union and today's Royal Pigeon Racing Association, which counts around 21,000 members in roughly 1,500 clubs. The sport's war record is famous: 32 RPRA pigeons received the Dickin Medal for gallantry, including Gustav, who brought first news of the D-Day landings in 1944. Racing pigeons' homing ability relies on magnetoreception and other senses refined over thousands of years.
