Pehlwani

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Pehlwani, also known as kushti, is the traditional grappling style of the Indian subcontinent, practiced in India, Pakistan and Bangladesh. A practitioner is called a pehlwan and a teacher is known as an ustad or guru. The style developed in the Mughal Empire as a synthesis of the Persian varzesh-e pahlavani and the ancient Indian malla-yuddha, and it is closely related to northern Indian kushti wrestling, which has its own page on this site. Pehlwani has influenced catch wrestling and, through it, modern folkstyle and freestyle wrestling.

Rules

Traditional pehlwani bouts, known as dangals, are contested in a circular earthen pit called an akhara, filled with specially prepared red clay that is treated with oil and other substances. Wrestlers wear a loincloth and the bout is won by pinning the opponent's back and shoulders to the ground; where no pin occurs, victory is awarded by decision of the judges, by knockout or by submission. Elaborate folk rules apply: wrestlers salute the pit before entering, techniques such as throws, lifts and holds from malla-yuddha and Persian wrestling are permitted, and strikes are not. Village dangals may agree their own variations of the rules beforehand, including time limits or a set number of scoring rounds.

History

The roots of the sport lie in malla-yuddha, described in the Mahabharata, the Ramayana and the 13th-century treatise Malla Purana, and in the Persian tradition of pahlavani wrestling celebrated in the Shahnama. Under the Mughal emperors, many of whom patronized wrestling, Iranian and Mongolian techniques were blended with malla-yuddha into modern pehlwani. Maratha and Rajput rulers supported the sport with rich prize money, and during the colonial period princes kept wrestling champions at their courts, with Uttar Pradesh and Punjab the greatest centers. The legendary Gama, called Rustam-e-Zamana after defeating the Pole Stanislaus Zbyszko in 1910, remains the most famous pehlwan, and wrestling titles such as Rustam-e-Hind are still awarded at Indian-style championships, including the Hind Kesari event held since 1958. Traditions survive in akharas such as Guru Hanuman's gymnasium in Delhi, which produced Olympic-calibre wrestlers, and Kolhapur in Maharashtra, where the Khasbaug wrestling stadium was built on the model of the Roman Colosseum.
