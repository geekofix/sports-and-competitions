Sport Stacking

≈ 4 min read

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Sport stacking (also called speed stacking or cup stacking) is a timed dexterity sport in which competitors stack and unstack pyramids of 9 or 12 plastic cups in set patterns — 3-3-3, 3-6-3, 6-6 and the cycle 1-10-1 plus 3-6-3 plus 6-6 — against a StackMat timer that stops when the last cup touches the mat (World Speed Stack Association, n.d.). The sport was invented in 1981 by Wayne Godinet at a Boys & Girls Club in Oceanside, California, and turned into a commercial and competitive discipline when Colorado PE teacher Bob Fox and Larry Goers built the StackMat timer in 1998 and founded Speed Stacks; the World Cup Stacking Association (WCSA), founded in 2001, was renamed the World Sport Stacking Association (WSSA) in 2005 and runs annual world championships (Wikipedia, n.d.). The WSSA is based in Larkspur, Colorado, and the current world records are held by William Orrell — cycle 4.578 seconds, 3-6-3 1.863 and 3-3-3 1.436 — while the women's cycle record stands at 7.43 seconds set by Emily Fox in 2002; at the 2026 Asian Championships on 9 November 2026, You Chen Lu of Chinese Taipei won the men's all-around with 9.254 seconds (Wikipedia, n.d.; World Speed Stack Association, n.d.).

Figure: MastaIroh. (2007, January 7). Sport-Stacking [Photograph]. Wikimedia Commons. Public domain. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sport-Stacking.jpg

Sport stacking is open to all ages and both genders in the same events, and women such as Emily Fox have held world records; in schools the activity is used to teach hand-eye coordination and ambidexterity, with separate boys' and girls' divisions only at youth level in some leagues (World Speed Stack Association, n.d.).

Rules

Official WSSA competition uses 9 or 12 identical plastic cups per stack and a StackMat timer with touch pads; events are the 3-3-3 (three pyramids of 3 cups), the 3-6-3 (a 3, a 6 and a 3), the 6-6 (two stacks of 6), the 1-10-1 (a single cup, a pyramid of 10 and a single cup) and the cycle, which runs 3-6-3, then 6-6, then 1-10-1 in one continuous timed sequence with a mandatory down-stacking of each pattern before the next begins (World Speed Stack Association, n.d.). Each event is timed three times, the best time counts, and a false start, knocking a cup out of pattern or failing to down-stack correctly adds a one-second penalty; competitors may compete one-handed only in designated events. Team relays (three or four stackers per team, one pattern each) are also official, and world championships are held annually with age divisions from under 8 to senior plus an open all-ages category. Men and women compete in the same events with identical rules and equipment, and separate men's and women's world records are kept (World Speed Stack Association, n.d.; Wikipedia, n.d.).

History

Sport stacking was invented in 1981 by Wayne Godinet, a youth worker at a Boys & Girls Club in Oceanside, California, who began teaching children to stack cups in patterns as a coordination game and trademarked the Karango Cupstack brand (Wikipedia, n.d.). The competitive sport took off when Bob Fox, a physical education teacher in Colorado, and Larry Goers developed the StackMat electronic timer in 1998 and founded the Speed Stacks company; the World Cup Stacking Association was founded in 2001, renamed the World Sport Stacking Association in 2005, and the first world championship was held in 2002 at the Denver Coliseum, becoming an annual event since (Wikipedia, n.d.). Early icon of the sport was Emily Fox, whose 7.43-second cycle in 2002 stood as the women's world record for years (World Speed Stack Association, n.d.).
