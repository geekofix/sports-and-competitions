Turtle Racing

Basic Information Rules History Sources Important Links

Basic Information

Turtle racing is the novelty racing event in which turtles (typically small pet-shop or pond turtles) sprint "short distances" along a marked circular or linear track, all starting from a central ring and racing toward the boundary: the first shelled starter to cross the outer edge wins. A long-running fixture of American bars, festivals and church-picnic fairs, it belongs to the grand tradition of animal novelty racing.

Rules

The classic format places a group of turtles in the inner circle of a painted ring (or at the start of a marked lane), with the winner the first turtle to reach the outer edge or finish line: race organizers hand out numbered turtle-assignment marking and let backers cheer their entrant, and heats and finals run through a tournament's card. The turtles move at their own pace and in their own directions, so tactics are limited to patience and lucky orientation rather than training; house rules govern handling (no touching the turtle once it has started, no baiting with food), the categories (individual races, which race organizers assign randomly in many cases), and welfare standards for the animals. Events award nominal prizes, and the winning turtle typically receives a title rather than a timeout.

History

Turtle races have a long history as an American novelty event, a fixture of county fairs, bars and fund-raisers through the twentieth century, with famous long-running venues like the Chicago Turtle Races (the Billy Goat Tavern circuit of the city's bar scene) making the format a beloved local tradition. The appeal has stayed constant: the slowest form of racing (occasionally won by the turtle that wakes up first), commentary by jesting emcees and a small spectator wager or guessing fee for charity. Events are contested to this day at bars and festivals across the United States and sporadically worldwide, preserving one of folk sport's gentlest absurdities.
